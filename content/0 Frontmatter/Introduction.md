# Introduction

Every great market was built by someone. The categories that now feel like geography (CRM, cloud computing, ride-sharing, plant-based meat) each began as one company's claim about what had changed in the world, repeated until buyers, analysts, and competitors treated the claim as terrain. Salesforce argued in 1999 that business software belonged in the browser; Impossible Foods argued in 2016 that meat was a molecule problem. Both claims came before their markets existed. Marketing, practiced at full strength, is the discipline of making that happen deliberately. This book is a language for practicing it.

This book is written for the people building a new technical or enterprise market: the founder turning a technology into a category, the challenger redrawing a market's terms from inside it, the new-venture team whose parent company's playbook does not fit, the growth-stage operator crossing from early adopters into a mainstream that buys differently. A marketing leader, a product manager, and a copywriter on those teams read the same language from different altitudes. The book has little to offer steady-state demand generation for a settled product, and nothing for the reader hunting growth hacks; tactics are exactly the layer it declines to compete on.

*Technical* and *enterprise* are a boundary. The patterns were quarried from markets where the product is technical, the buyer is an organization, and the purchase is a decision several people have to agree on. Much of what follows assumes machinery that only exists in markets like that: a pipeline you can inspect, a CRM that remembers what happened, an account that can grow after the first sale, a free tier that lets someone use the product before anyone signs a contract. Some of the argument reaches past it. [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] and [[MS-018 Price Creates the Buyer]] hold for a supermarket brand or a two-sided marketplace as well as they hold for a database company. The systems in Parts II and III travel less easily, and a consumer, retail, or hardware business will have to translate them before running them. The Examples show the ground each pattern has been tested on; read them for the boundary.

## A Pattern Language
The method of this book comes from architecture. In 1977 Christopher Alexander, with Sara Ishikawa and Murray Silverstein, published *A Pattern Language*: 253 patterns for towns, buildings, and construction, each one describing, in Alexander's words, "a problem which occurs over and over again in our environment, and then the core of the solution to that problem, in such a way that you can use this solution a million times over, without ever doing it the same way twice." The form proved portable. In 1994 Gamma, Helm, Johnson, and Vlissides carried it into programming with *Design Patterns*, and it changed how a generation of engineers shared design judgment. This book carries the same form into marketing: 123 patterns across four parts, each naming a situation you will recognize, the pressures that keep the obvious response from working, and a recommendation that holds.

Every pattern here has the same skeleton. A short abstract places the pattern among its neighbors. A boxed **admonition** then names the specific error the pattern exists to prevent, and it is the one place in a pattern that tells you what to do; the prose beneath it describes a problem you will know from the inside and leaves the verdict for later. The **Complications** are the competing pressures that break the easy answer; they are what raise a recurring problem above a tip. The **Recommendations** section states the invariant as a claim you can score yourself against on Monday: a count, a threshold, or a concrete test. The **Implications** say what the recommendation unlocks and which tensions it creates next, because every real solution starts the next problem. Then the **Examples**, which are where you should look hardest.

The patterns link. Each one names the larger patterns it lives inside and the smaller ones that complete it, and the links carry the argument: a pricing decision reaches positioning, positioning reaches the sales motion, and the motion decides what the operations team has to build. Alexander sorted his patterns by scale, from towns down through buildings and rooms to construction details. This book keeps his four levels and calls the axis altitude. Part I, **Market Structure**, is the territory: [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] opens the language, and the thirty-five patterns after it establish who buys, what a price admits, and where premium survives. Part II, **GTM Systems**, builds the machine that moves through that territory, from [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] through first contact, demand, pricing systems, channels, and the growth loop. Part III, **Operations**, runs the machine week over week, opening with [[OPS-001 Marketing as Sensemaking]] and closing with measurement. Part IV, **Craft**, is where all of it becomes the sentence a stranger actually reads, beginning at [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]]. Dependency flows downhill through the first three parts. At the craft level it runs both directions, because a value proposition that will not compress into one sentence is usually a positioning problem surfacing at the keyboard.

There are three ways in. Read front to back and the language unfolds in the order a market gets built: structure first, craft last. Start instead from a Monday problem (a homepage that will not convert, a demo that wanders), find its pattern in Part IV, and climb the larger links until you reach the structural decision your craft problem is expressing. Or read by role: a founder begins in Part I, an operations lead in Part III, a writer in Part IV, and every path crosses the others because the network connects them. Each pattern is written to be opened cold; five minutes gives you the situation, the recommendation, and the cases behind it. Before any of those routes, though, read [[The Pattern Summary]]: all 123 patterns compressed to one line each, in order. It's the fastest way to find the pattern that names the problem in front of you, and the fastest way to see how much ground the language covers. In the digital edition the network renders as a live graph you can wander. Wherever you enter, the links carry you to the rest.

Alexander closed his introduction by telling readers the language was theirs to speak. The same holds here. These patterns are a vocabulary and a set of claims about which arrangements work; the sentences you build with them, the market you draw, the category you argue into existence, are yours.

See your market clearly, then shape it on purpose.
