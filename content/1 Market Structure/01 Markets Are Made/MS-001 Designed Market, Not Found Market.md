> *The position you take engineers the market; segmentation only maps the ground someone else already owns.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A great market begins with a deliberate claim about how the world has changed, and positioning turns that claim into the terms on which every buyer judges the product. It is the root of Part I; [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] develops the claim the market hears, and the remaining smaller patterns carry that design into buyer, price, focus, and economics.

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat the market as a pre-existing object to find. Take that frame and you inherit the incumbent's pricing and comparison set, and every sale gets argued on the incumbent's terms.

A founder who waits to "find" a market is waiting for permission. "Searching for product-market fit" casts the founder as an explorer hacking toward a golden idol already buried in the jungle. It suggests that fit will be found, and it's only a matter of time. It subtly promises that tweaking the landing page or adjusting the pricing will surface a hungry crowd ready for what you built. And it puts the founder's own judgment out of scope: an explorer doesn't decide where the idol is, only how hard to look.

In this model, your product is still moving, so the market has to be the half that's already cut, and matching it is your job. That turns the work into measurement. You run twenty more interviews, watch one more cohort for retention, and write "signal" on the slide. You did everything right. The quarter counts as progress because activity is high and the vocabulary is disciplined, and a board will accept "we haven't found it yet" for a year. What never gets on the calendar is the decision only a founder can make, and no amount of searching returns it.

Steve Blank built a methodology around this. _The Four Steps to the Epiphany_ (2005) tells a founder that a startup's job is to search for a repeatable business model, and the customer development loop it introduced has become the standard for how founders talk to buyers before they build. Blank's methods are powerful, but they have a hidden assumption. Search presumes the market is finished before the founder shows up, its boundaries drawn and its questions already settled, so the only work left is to locate it. 

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne called contested markets red oceans in _Blue Ocean Strategy_ (2005), arguing that a company does better to create uncontested space than to fight for share in one. Their question is where a company should compete.

Most founders answer that question with segmentation, which makes no choice at all. Slicing a population by firmographics, geography, and vertical produces a board-friendly TAM slide and nothing else. It describes the world as it exists. Whether anyone in it will listen to a new claim is a question segmentation can't ask.

Take a segment and you take your customer's existing mental models with it. You enter their budget line, inherit their preconceived notions of pricing, and accept their choice of incumbent as your competition. A database buyer compares you to Oracle; a CRM buyer, to Salesforce. You are fighting on terrain an incumbent has already surveyed and by rules an incumbent wrote. Your sales cycles lengthen; your win-loss reports repeat "feature gap" and "price."

The reflex is to funnel-tweak your way out of this, but that rarely works because the frame was set long before the buyer ever reached your page. No amount of copywriting or A/B testing touches what the market assumes you are. A market left untouched defaults to the cheapest reading of a new thing and classifies you as a commodity. (How the market reads you belongs to [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]]; the mental shelf it assigns you to, to [[MS-005 Category Before Product]]; the segmentation reflex itself, to [[MS-031 Market Segmentation]]; the sizing ritual, to [[MS-030 Sizing Is Storytelling]].)

This is the choice most founders avoid on purpose: whether a market is something you locate or something you build. They avoid it for good reasons, because every pressure inside the company points toward the found market.

## Complications

- **Guesswork that looks rigorous.** A TAM slide is top-down guesses multiplied together, and it survives because it looks like proof. A claim about how the world has changed can only be believed or doubted, and the founder who brings one gets asked for the TAM slide anyway.
- **The pull of a fast first sale.** Slotting into an existing category usually does get you revenue faster, and it locks you into the incumbent's price. The trade looks like traction on a monthly board update and reads as a race to the bottom two years later.
- **The comfort of looking safe.** A claim that can't be wrong is a comfortable claim. A designed market commits to something specific enough to fail in public, and most founders quietly take the comfort.
- **A hired executive improves the frame he inherits and never questions it.** Positioning is the founder's work, and founders are the scarcest labor in the company. A VP of Sales hired to improve results inside the frame he inherits will do exactly that, because that's the job.
- **The shift has to be real, and it doesn't wait.** A designed market must ride a real change in the world. Without a genuine shift, it is fantasy. Without a founder designing for the shift, it is a missed window. See [[MS-003 Window of Inevitability]] for the window itself.
- **A wrong guess can hide for years; a wrong claim can't.** A found market limps along on a bad guess and nobody outside the company notices. A designed market fails fast and publicly, because you have named what the world is, and if you named it wrong the market will know it.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Make deliberate positioning the founder's first fixed decision, naming the change in the buyer's world and the market that change creates. The frame passes when a buyer or analyst can state the change and category before a rep explains the product.

Start with a specific insight about how the world has changed. Use it to redraw the context in which your product is judged. Positioning leaves the product untouched and changes the frame so entirely that your solution becomes the only sensible conclusion. Building that argument, in the order a buyer will accept it, is [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]]. What gets decided here is the input to it: the shift you are betting on, and what that shift makes untenable.

Do not delegate this. It is a decision about what the company means, and only the founder can make it hold. Interrogate your technology with these three questions: What do we understand about the world that others ignore? Who loses if we win? What happens to our customer if they don't buy us? Those answers are the raw material a position gets built from.

You will know it is working when the metrics turn. Sales velocity climbs because reps are confirming why the buyer needs this rather than explaining what it is. Acquisition cost falls because the narrative does the persuading before a rep dials. When analysts shift from asking "where do you fit?" to asking "what should we call this?", you have moved from finding to building. The market is adopting your frame as its own.

## Implications

A built market reorders everything beneath it. Because you chose the frame, you are no longer priced against an incumbent's category. This raises the price ceiling and changes which buyer you can reach. The design becomes concrete through its smaller patterns: the [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] you project, the [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] you build, the [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]] you draw, the [[MS-010 ICP Definition]] you aim at, and the [[MS-018 Price Creates the Buyer]] that filters who walks in. Each makes sense only after the decision above it. This is why this pattern sits at the root of Part I, with nothing larger above it.

Understand the cost of this power: a designed market is a falsifiable bet on reality. You have told the world what to expect and staked the company on it, which is why the claim needs revisiting the moment evidence contradicts it. Conviction should follow the evidence.

## Examples

**Heroku (around 2008–2010).** The found market was clear and terrible: developers who wanted to host Ruby apps, a commodity fight against every VPS, decided on specs per dollar. We didn't go there. We argued against infrastructure management itself, on the claim that modern development shouldn't require ops expertise, and sold a world where you deploy code and never touch a server. We invented "time-to-deploy" as the yardstick and owned it because the incumbents had no answer to it. We did not discover a crowd that hated sysadmin work; we converted low-grade irritation into a market requirement.

**Mesosphere (2015).** Selling container orchestration as a feature inside an infrastructure conversation put the product into a bake-off. We renamed the thing entirely and sold the "Datacenter Operating System," a model in which a datacenter runs like a single computer. Once a CIO bought that reframe, the feature-by-feature bake-off with competing tools became irrelevant; we had won the argument about what the thing *was*. Pricing it as a strategic platform moved the purchase to a different budget line. The frame changed the answer.

**Salesforce, "No Software" (service launched February 2000).** The found market: people who want cheaper on-premise CRM. Salesforce didn't build that. It argued against packaged software itself, its implementations and its IT tax, on the claim that the web had made hosted software possible, and sold a world where a buyer simply logs in. Salesforce's own registration statement dates the company to February 1999 and the start of its on-demand CRM service to February 2000. The red "no software" circle was the thesis compressed into a logo. It created the category "SaaS," which outlived the pitch and became the water the industry swims in.

**FedEx and overnight delivery (1971–1978).** The obvious entry into air freight was to broker space on passenger airlines and be judged on rates per pound, the way the incumbents ran it. Fred Smith, who traced the idea back to a term paper he'd written at Yale, built the Memphis hub-and-spoke system that made an overnight promise physically possible before any customer had asked for one. FedEx's own history dates the first night of service to 17 April 1973, when fourteen Dassault Falcon jets carried 186 packages to 25 cities. In an existing freight business that volume is a rounding error; in a category nobody had priced yet it was the proof. The 1978 Ally & Gargano line "when it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight" named the category, and buyers who had never held a budget for overnight delivery wrote one on FedEx's terms.

**Red Bull and the energy drink (1984–1987).** Believing the market research would have meant shelving the product; the tests Dietrich Mateschitz ran reported that people disliked the taste, the logo, and the name. He launched in Austria on 1 April 1987 anyway and designed the market the research couldn't see, pricing the slim can far above cola to mark it as a different kind of drink and seeding it through students and extreme-sport athletes until "energy drink" became a Western category. The drink already existed in Thailand; the market for it was built in Austria.

**Tesla and the desirable electric car (2006–2008).** The electric car the market already imagined was a virtuous compromise judged on range and price. Elon Musk's 2006 "Secret Master Plan" redrew what the category meant, opening with a Roadster priced near $100,000 that beat gas sports cars off the line, so the first mental file the market opened for Tesla held a car people wanted. The why-now was the lithium-ion cell, matured by a decade of laptops into a battery that could move a car. The march downmarket that followed belongs to [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]].

**Airbnb and the spare-room market (2008–2014).** Airbnb could have fought hotels and hostels for the budget traveler on price per night. It treated the world's spare rooms as inventory no market had ever counted, then built the trust machinery, two-way reviews, profiles, payments held by the platform until check-in, that made sleeping in a stranger's home ordinary. The rooms and the travelers had always existed; the why-now was that machinery. By the 2014 "Belong Anywhere" rebrand, Airbnb sold a kind of stay hotels had no shelf for, and the incumbents answered with zoning lawyers.
