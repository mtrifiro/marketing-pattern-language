> *A playbook borrowed from the wrong kind of market fails no matter how well you run it.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Markets come in four types: one that already exists, one resegmented on price, one resegmented for a niche, and one nobody has sold into before. Which type you're in decides your market entry strategy. It's partly your choice, since price, problem width and buying center can move a market from one type to another. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] and above [[MS-017 Adoption Curve Ownership]], [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]], [[MS-023 Category Creation]], [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]], and [[GTM-008 Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't copy a go-to-market plan from a company whose market type was different from yours. The tactics travel; the assumptions underneath them stay behind, and the assumptions are what made the plan work.

A go-to-market plan almost always arrives as a borrowed one. A founder names three companies they admire, someone reconstructs how those companies launched, and the plan gets assembled out of the tactics that worked: this content cadence, that pricing page, that conference, that analyst relationship. The tactics are usually described accurately. What travels with them, unannounced, is a set of assumptions about the market the original company was standing in, and those assumptions decide whether any of it can work.

You can enter an existing market by outperforming what's already there, competing on features against incumbents who define the terms of comparison. You can resegment an existing market on price, taking the customers at the low end who will accept good-enough performance in exchange for paying substantially less. You can resegment it for a niche, serving a subset whose specific needs the incumbents ignore, sometimes at a higher price and sometimes with worse performance on attributes that subset doesn't care about. Or you can create a market, where the customers couldn't do the thing at all before. Steve Blank set out these four in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005), and reports that over half of startups take one of the two resegmentation paths.

How you enter changes the target, the argument, the clock and the budget together:

|                       | Existing market                        | Resegmented, low cost                       | Resegmented, niche                          | New market                                 |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Who you target        | The incumbents' customers              | The incumbents' price-sensitive low end     | A subset the incumbents underserve           | People who couldn't do this before         |
| What they want        | Better performance                     | Good enough, substantially cheaper          | Fit for a need nobody serves                 | Simplicity and convenience                 |
| How you compete       | Features against a known list          | Price, against incumbents who can cut theirs | Fit, against incumbents defending profit     | Against non-consumption and other startups |
| What you risk         | The cost of entry against an incumbent | Incumbents matching you on price            | Redefining the market wrongly                | Adoption never arriving                    |
| First-cycle objective | Take share                             | Take share and educate at once              | Take share and educate at once               | Move the count of possible buyers off zero |

Blank treats those four as a diagnosis, a question you answer about a market that already is what it is. Three things you control move a market between them. Price moves it: enter far enough under the incumbent and what would have been an existing-market fight becomes a low-cost resegmentation. The width of the problem you claim moves it: narrow the problem to a subset nobody serves and you're a niche entrant, widen it past anything currently purchasable and you're creating a market. The buying center you enter through moves it too, and that one has no slot in Blank's four. A settled category entered through the practitioner comes with incumbents, known budgets and a formed buying process, plus a buyer who has never been sold to, so it runs on existing-market economics and new-market education at the same time. [[MS-014 Practitioner and Purchaser Are Different People]] holds why the two seats behave differently, and [[GTM-021 Practitioner-First GTM]] holds what entering through the lower one costs.

The expensive direction is running a new market on existing-market assumptions: funding demand capture when there's no demand yet to capture, buying search terms nobody types, staffing a sales team against a buying process that hasn't formed. None of it looks like waste while it's happening, because every line item is something a competent company does.

The obvious correction is to run the plan and watch the numbers, adjusting when they disappoint. This won't work in new markets, because the numbers a healthy new market produces early are indistinguishable from the numbers a failing product in an existing market produces: low volume, long cycles, prospects who engage and don't convert. Read as an execution problem, those numbers call for more spend and more reps. Read against the right type, the same numbers are on schedule and the correct response is to protect the runway. No metric tells you which situation you're in.

## Complications

- **The plan needs the type fixed, and the type expires.** A market can be new when you enter it and ordinary three years later. The type that justified a patient budget stops being true while the budget is still being spent, and nothing in the plan announces the change.
- **It presents as four boxes and behaves as a continuum.** Blank couldn't place his own central case, assigning Webvan to niche resegmentation and then conceding the idea was radical enough to count as a new market. A reader who treats the four as bins will force a market into whichever bin it least badly fits.
- **The flattering type is the expensive one.** "New market" buys a longer runway and a more patient board, and it's the type a founder can always argue for. "Existing market" obliges you to explain how you'll beat a funded incumbent. The incentives point away from the honest answer.
- **The slowest type produces the most defensible position.** A created market takes longest to pay and yields the strongest hold once it does, so the pressure to switch to something faster works directly against the reason for choosing it.
- **A price-led resegmentation buys customers by fixing a ceiling.** Coming in underneath the incumbent is the fastest way to get anyone to try you and the surest way to set the top of your range, and the customers it brings are the ones least willing to fund the move upmarket that would lift it.
- **The levers that move your type get heavier as you use them.** Price, problem width and buying center are all yours to set before launch and expensive to change afterward: a published price anchors what the market thinks you're worth, and a buying center you've sold into for two years has staff, comp and content built around it. The type stays a choice for about as long as it stays a plan.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Choose your market type before you set the objective for your first planning cycle or fund demand creation, and write it down where the plan can be tested against it. The choice is real only if changing it would change that objective, your positioning basis and your spending curve; where all three survive a change of type, nothing was chosen.

The objective for that first cycle is the part that most often goes unstated, and it differs by type. In an existing market the goal is share, and the work is demand capture and acquisition against a buying process that already runs. In a resegmented market the goal is share plus education at the same time, which is harder than either alone and is why the most common entry is also the trickiest. In a new market the goal has nothing to do with share; the measure is whether the count of people who could plausibly buy has moved off zero toward something meaningful. Blank frames it as the year-one target and calls that single reallocation worth the price of his book. A new-market company judged on share will be declared a failure by its own dashboard while it's doing exactly the right work.

How fast you're allowed to spend follows from the same choice, and the rule belongs to [[GTM-008 Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture]]: size demand creation against the buyers who can act inside the cycle you're funding, whatever the category will eventually be worth.

Where two types both fit, take the one you can fund. Each carries a different bill. A low-cost resegmentation needs a cost structure that survives the incumbent matching your price. A niche one needs a subset large enough to live on. A new market needs enough runway to pay for the education before anyone buys. When the only type you can afford isn't the one you want, you have a financing problem wearing a market-type label, and it belongs to [[MS-035 Go-To-Funding vs. Go-To-Market Balance]].

For the existing-market type, price the fight before you take it. Blank borrows the New Lanchester Strategy, which puts the cost of a head-on attack on a dominant incumbent at three times their sales and marketing budget, and at 1.7 times in a market with several competitors. Treat those as an attackability test. Blank concedes he can't derive the formulas, and he states the 1.7 denominator three different ways across the book, so the number signals a scale and won't survive contact with a spreadsheet. What survives is the direction of the relationship: the cost of entry scales with how concentrated the incumbents are, and [[MS-025 Competitive Geography]] maps that concentration.

## Implications

Once the type is written down, several arguments that normally run on taste get a governing input. The order of creation and capture in [[GTM-008 Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture]] stops being universal and becomes conditional: an existing market has a demand pool to harvest from day one, and a new market has nothing to capture for as long as it takes buyers to appear. The motion variables in [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] gain the one property they lack, since price band, committee size, the user-payer split and account concentration are all properties of the transaction, and none of them tells you whether demand exists. Positioning in [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]] gains its exception, because contrast needs something to contrast against and a genuinely new market supplies no reference point. The width of the gap in [[MS-017 Adoption Curve Ownership]] stops being a constant.

The new tension the pattern creates is a recurring obligation. A type chosen once and never revisited keeps governing after it stops being true, because everything downstream still obeys it. That makes it a standing question for [[OPS-019 Quarterly Recalibration]] rather than a founding decision that gets filed.

## Examples

**Palm and Handspring, one team and two market types (1996 and 1999).** Blank's anchor case is a single team running opposite plans three years apart. Donna Dubinsky ran Palm Computing when the Pilot shipped in 1996 and was CEO of Handspring when the Visor shipped in 1999. At Palm the work was convincing people a handheld was worth wanting at all. At Handspring the PDA market was, in Blank's description, a billion-dollar one, buyers compared products on specifications, and differentiating on expandability and performance produced $170 million of revenue in twelve months, on his figure. His verdict on the pair is that each company would have failed running the other's plan: Handspring using Palm's evangelism, Palm burning its cash on Handspring's feature comparison. Blank calls 1996 a market that didn't exist. The sharper claim, and the one [[MS-026 Fast Follower Arrives]] establishes with dates, is that handhelds had been on sale since the Psion Organiser in 1984 and had never sold in volume. What changed between 1996 and 1999 was buyer literacy, and buyer literacy is what the four types are sorting.

**Webvan, a plan that obeyed two market types at once (1996 to 2001).** On Blank's account: founded December 1996, $10 million raised in 1997, a further $393 million privately before the IPO, more than $800 million in total capital. It beta-tested to about 1,100 people in May 1999, launched its first regional store in June, filed to go public 60 days after that, and was worth $8.5 billion on the day of its IPO, larger than the top three grocery chains combined. It was bankrupt within 24 months of that IPO. Blank can't settle which type it was in, assigning it to a niche resegmentation of the grocery business and then conceding the idea was radical enough to count as a new market. The spending says which type the plan assumed. Automated warehouses and delivery fleets built ahead of demand are existing-market moves, priced for share in a $450 billion category, run against a buyer nobody had yet confirmed would come back. A founder who can't choose between two types is usually looking at a market that sits between them, and the workable response is to name which type each part of the plan is obeying.
