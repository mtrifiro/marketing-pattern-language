> *Every shift opens a window when a new approach is becoming inevitable but not yet obvious; too early you evangelize and starve, too late the door has closed.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> An enabling shift opens a window you can see coming: the early side charges for education and the late side is occupied. Timing becomes a structural force the company can study and fund. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat market timing as luck. Enter too early and you spend your capital educating a market that can't move, while a better-timed entrant reaps what you taught it; enter too late and the fast-followers already hold the ground.

Behind every strong "why now" is a window, and behind the window is the shift that opened it: a technology crosses a cost threshold, a regulation changes, a behavior turns, a platform arrives. That shift makes a new approach first possible and eventually inevitable. The distance between possible and obvious can last years. Across that stretch the approach is becoming the future without yet being the present, and the window of inevitability is exactly that interval: after the enabling shift has begun and before the rest of the market has recognized it.

The two ways of missing a window are symmetric. A company that arrives too early, before the enabling shift is real, spends all its capital evangelizing a market that isn't ready to move; it's right about the destination and broke before it arrives, and a better-timed entrant walks in later to reap the market it educated. Plenty of companies were right too early, which in financial terms is hard to tell apart from being wrong. A company that arrives too late, after the shift is obvious and the "why now" is consensus, finds the window closed: incumbents have adapted, fast-followers have taken the ground, and the premium it could have earned has already compressed under [[MS-007 Maturity Kills Premium]]. By the time the opportunity is obvious, the ground inside it is claimed.

The window isn't random. The enabling shift that opens it is usually visible in advance, in cost curves bending, regulations moving, behaviors changing, which makes timing a structural force a company can study. A company that calls the timing luck forfeits the one advantage that reading the shift would have given it. (The market you enter through a window is the one engineered in [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]]; the recognition of the window is the "why now" of [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]]; the window's far edge is the compression of [[MS-007 Maturity Kills Premium]].)

## Complications

- **The window is bounded on both sides.** Too early and the market isn't ready; too late and the ground is taken. The opening is a finite interval, and it closes whether or not you're ready to enter.
- **Possible precedes obvious by a gap.** The interval between an approach becoming feasible and becoming consensus can last years, and payroll runs the whole time.
- **Being right too early looks like being wrong.** Financially, correct-before-ready is indistinguishable from mistaken. The bank balance reports the same number either way, and so does the board.
- **The enabling shift is readable.** Cost curves, regulation, and behavior change are usually visible in advance. The people who must fund the move read the same signals and still wait for company.
- **Waiting for certainty guarantees a miss.** Certainty arrives together with competitors. The evidence that would justify the bet is the same evidence that tells everyone else to make it.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Name the enabling shift and track the observable threshold that makes the approach possible; enter after the shift is real and while the position is still open. The timing thesis fails if the shift doesn't progress as predicted.

Work it in three moves:

1. **Name the shift.** Identify the structural change that turns your approach from impossible to inevitable: the specific cost curve crossing a threshold, the regulation, the behavior change, the platform arrival. Track it, because the shift is the clock the window runs on.
2. **Enter inside the gap.** Move as the shift becomes real and before it becomes consensus. That interval contains a market ready enough to move and a position no one else has taken.
3. **Fund the wait.** Calibrate your evangelism budget to where you sit in the gap. An early entrant has to pay for a period of market education and, better, be genuinely useful before the window fully opens. Enter early with no patience-capital and no near-term utility and you're betting you can hold your breath longer than the market takes to arrive; you'll usually lose.

The window connects the timing patterns across the language. Creating a category ([[MS-023 Category Creation]]) is timing a window and helping the market recognize it; the "why now" of [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] is how you articulate the window to the buyer; and the window opens at the innovator and early-adopter edge of the [[MS-017 Adoption Curve Ownership]], which is why an early entrant lives among visionaries before the mainstream arrives. Hold your timing thesis as a hypothesis. The window either opens as the shift plays out or it doesn't, so let the market prove it and don't defend the bet against the evidence.

## Implications

Read timing as a structural force and "luck" becomes a discipline: anticipate a window by tracking the enabling shift, size your patience and your evangelism to the gap, and enter as the approach is becoming inevitable. This grounds the "why now" that [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] and [[MS-023 Category Creation]] depend on, and it sets the far boundary of the opportunity, since the window closes as [[MS-007 Maturity Kills Premium]] arrives and the premium compresses. It sits inside [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] as the time dimension of when a designed market can actually be built.

Hold the tension honestly: the window is far easier to see in hindsight than from inside it, and the two errors disguise themselves unequally. Too late is obvious in retrospect; too early stays indistinguishable from wrong until a better-timed competitor's delayed success reveals what you were early to. Because certainty arrives with competition, you can't wait for proof, so act on a readable but unproven shift and accept that some windows you read will fail to open. Timing is a bet. Make it a bet on a structural shift you can study rather than a roll of the dice.

## Examples

**The container window.** Around 2014 and 2015 the enabling shifts behind containers, the maturing kernel features, the rise of cloud, the appetite for portability, were real and readable, and a window opened in which container orchestration was becoming inevitable without yet being obvious. Companies that entered in that window and helped the market recognize the shift rode it. Companies that entered a few years earlier, when the technology was possible but the enterprise wasn't ready, spent their capital evangelizing until better-timed successors absorbed them and arrived to a market they had primed.

**Early to the edge.** At Vapor IO we bet on edge computing: that the cost and latency curves would make distributed compute at the edge inevitable. The hardest part was timing. The enabling shift was readable, but the gap between possible and ready was long, and being early meant funding a period of market education while the window slowly opened. An early entrant on a readable but not-yet-arrived shift lives or dies on whether it can stay useful and solvent long enough for the market to catch up to the thesis.

**Right too early.** The clearest cautionary case I know is the company that had the correct thesis a decade before the window opened, evangelized heroically, ran out of money, and watched a later entrant win the market its education had prepared. In financial terms that company wasn't distinguishable from one that had simply been wrong, which is the cruelty of the early side: the market eventually proved the thesis, and by then the company that first held it was gone.

**Iridium, and the shift that closed the window (1987–2000).** Motorola engineers designed Iridium in 1987 around 66 low-Earth-orbit satellites, a genuine advance over the geostationary alternative that made calls slow and handsets enormous. Sydney Finkelstein and Shade Sanford laid out the numbers in "Learning from Corporate Mistakes: The Rise and Fall of Iridium" (*Organizational Dynamics*, 2000). Iridium spent more than $5 billion, launched service on 1 November 1998 behind a $180 million advertising campaign, and charged $3,000 for a handset plus $3 to $8 a minute. Its chief executive, Edward Staiano, had forecast 500,000 subscribers by the end of 1999. Iridium had 10,000 by April and 20,000 by August, against the 52,000 its loan covenants required, and it filed for Chapter 11 on 13 August 1999, two days after defaulting on $1.5 billion. Finkelstein and Sanford give the cause plainly: terrestrial cellular built out faster than Iridium's plan assumed, so by launch the traveling executives it had been designed for could already place a call. The satellite thesis held up on its own terms. A bankruptcy court approved the sale of the constellation for $25 million in November 2000, less than one two-hundredth of what building it had cost, and those satellites went on to carry a working business. Iridium tracked one cost curve and was overtaken by another.
