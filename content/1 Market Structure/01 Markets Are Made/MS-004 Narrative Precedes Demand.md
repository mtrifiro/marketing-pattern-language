> *A market reads you before you speak, and correcting that read costs more than authoring it.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A buyer sorts a product in seconds using the story already in circulation, and that first reading fixes the comparison set and price before the product gets to speak. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] and above [[MS-005 Category Before Product]], [[MS-023 Category Creation]], [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]], and [[GTM-009 Awareness Cascade]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat demand as a function of volume. Buying more attention buys recognition and leaves your revenue untouched, because a buyer prices you from inside the reading they already have, whatever you spend to be seen.

By the time a buyer looks at your product, the question of what you are has already been answered, and it was answered by whatever story was circulating before you showed up. Buyers file first and evaluate second. The reading arrives ahead of the evaluation, and your price gets read off the reading. (Which category that reading files you into, and what the wrong one costs you, belongs to [[MS-005 Category Before Product]].)

That sort takes seconds and runs on whatever material is available at the moment it happens. Publish a story first and the sort runs on your story. Publish nothing and it runs on the nearest familiar thing, which is usually a competitor's category, and you spend the life of the company arguing with a verdict that was reached before anyone from your team was in the room. Awareness money arrives after the sort and can't reopen it; it buys more people holding the reading you already have.

Most companies leave that reading to chance. They fill the homepage with features, 99.999% uptime and low-latency routing and SSO, and hope the buyer translates specs into business value. The buyer won't, because they're busy and risk-averse. Handed a pile of puzzle pieces, they walk away and reach for the cheapest reading available, which is "commodity."

The cheapest reading of all is that nothing needs to change. [[MS-025 Competitive Geography]] maps the alternatives a new offering actually competes against, and the decision to do nothing sits at the top of that map, because it costs the buyer nothing and puts no one's job at risk. A claim of improvement invites comparison, and comparison supplies the reason to wait. How you draw the contrast the story dramatizes is [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]].

## Complications

- **The budget follows the metric.** Impressions and reach report cleanly and can be bought by the unit. A reading has to be argued, and it shows up on no dashboard until revenue moves. Money flows to the number that reports, so the spend that changes demand is the spend nobody can defend in a review.
- **Deadline pressure favors the demo.** Features are shippable and demoable, so under a date the team lists specs and defers the argument. The specs arrive on time and arrive meaning nothing in particular.
- **The reading beats the pitch.** A buyer settles what you are long before they read anything you wrote to tell them, so the story has to be in the market before the product is. That asks you to publish a claim about something you can't yet demo, which is the moment it feels most reckless to make one.
- **Doing nothing costs the buyer nothing.** Your real rival carries no price and no switching pain, which [[MS-025 Competitive Geography]] maps in full. A claim of improvement leaves the buyer exactly where they already are, and improvement alone gives them no reason to absorb the cost of moving.
- **A published claim can be refused.** The story has to be specific enough to be wrong, and you publish it while the evidence is still thin. The safe move is a feature list, which asserts nothing about the world and therefore can't be contradicted, and that safety is most of why teams choose it.
- **Committing early costs you what the market would have taught you.** A story published before launch fixes your reading at the moment you know least about the buyer. The information that would have corrected it arrives after the claim is public, when withdrawing it is expensive and looks like retreat.
- **A large story invites a louder teller.** A reading big enough to move a market is a reading an incumbent with more distribution can adopt and outspend you on. Authoring it first reserves nothing, and the better the argument, the more worth stealing it becomes.
- **Early is hard to tell from wrong.** A story the market can't yet hear reads as a bad story, and the company that funds the education often isn't the one that collects on it. [[MS-003 Window of Inevitability]] holds the timing this depends on.

## Recommendations

> [!check] The story reaches the market before the product does, and two surfaces score it: your homepage headline and your opening slide. If either one describes your company rather than a change in the buyer's world, the reading is still unauthored and your spend is buying awareness.

Don't confuse this story with "once upon a time" or a founder origin tale. In technical marketing a story is an argument, and it follows a fixed order. Andy Raskin set that order out in "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" (2016), reading it off Zuora's sales deck. His five steps, in his sequence:

1. **Name the change.** State what has shifted in the world, independent of your company, that makes the old way untenable.
2. **Name the winners and the losers.** Show that the shift sorts companies, and that standing still puts the buyer on the losing side of it.
3. **Tease the promised land.** Describe what life looks like on the far side, in the buyer's terms, with your product still out of frame.
4. **Introduce the product as the way across.** Features enter here and nowhere earlier, each one answering an obstacle between the buyer and the promised land.
5. **Show evidence you can make it true.** Customers who crossed, results, anything that turns the claim from an assertion into something a buyer can check.

Step two is the one most decks drop, and it's the one that pays for the deal. A shift with no losers is a piece of news. A shift that sorts the market puts a price on staying put, and a line item appears where none existed. Lead with the change and each feature becomes proof of the argument; "identity-based access" stops being a setting and becomes the answer to a perimeter that has dissolved.

Then run the story through every layer of go-to-market. Open the first slide on the change in the world; the company bio can wait. Write the website headline as the new reading, so that "The database for real-time AI" claims a category where "the fastest database" claims only a rank. Argue the shift in your content instead of documenting features. Because the story fixes the category the buyer files you in (see [[MS-005 Category Before Product]]) and therefore the price they expect, it can't be handed to marketing after the fact. The founder sets the reading, and sets it before the product ships.

## Implications

When the story precedes the product, the product lands in a market already primed to receive it. Demand stops arriving in units you bought and starts arriving from buyers who already know what you are. A durable story opens the patterns that make it permanent: build the stable category with [[MS-005 Category Before Product]], then name and standardize it with [[MS-023 Category Creation]] so the market keeps using your frame. Draw the contrast with [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]], carry it outward with [[GTM-009 Awareness Cascade]], and apply the same argument to market size with [[MS-030 Sizing Is Storytelling]].

Authoring a reading also puts you on a clock. The fifth step of the argument comes due whether or not you have it, and the market picks the moment: an analyst asks for reference customers, a competitor ships the thing you described, a buyer who believed you goes looking for someone who has already crossed. A story published early buys the reading now and borrows against proof you owe later. How long that credit lasts is [[MS-003 Window of Inevitability]].

## Examples

**Salesforce Desk.com (launched 30 January 2012).** We could have led with "a cloud-based support ticketing system," which would only have made people aware of a tool. We built the story around a change ("the social consumer now expects support on Twitter and Facebook alongside email"), a sorting of winners and losers ("legacy helpdesks wall your team off from social, and that becomes brand risk"), and a promised land ("every support agent as a brand ambassador"). We were selling a way to survive the social-media era.

**The container wars (2014–2015).** Awareness of Docker was total; every developer knew what a container was. Enterprise demand lagged anyway, because CIOs read containers as "a developer toy for laptops." At Mesosphere, and later across the Kubernetes vendors, we stopped talking about "lightweight virtualization" and started talking about application portability and the end of cloud lock-in: build on raw EC2 and you can never leave; build on an orchestration layer and you keep your options. The technology didn't change while that argument ran. A container was still cgroups and namespaces, and the buyers who had filed it as a laptop tool began filing it as an infrastructure decision. Whether the window was open for that argument at all is [[MS-003 Window of Inevitability]].

**Slack's internal frame (31 July 2013).** Two weeks before Slack's preview release, Stewart Butterfield wrote a memo to his own team, later published in full, arguing that a saddle company can sell saddles or it can sell horseback riding, and that Slack was in the horseback-riding business. The memo fixed what the company believed it was selling, which was an operating system for work. The launch pitch stayed narrow and specific; the wide reading was the destination rather than the opening line, and that sequence belongs to [[MS-006 Wedge-to-Platform Gradient]]. The story was authored inside the company first, and the product shipped into a team that already knew which market it was in.

**Listerine and halitosis (1920s).** Joseph Lawrence formulated Listerine in 1879 as a surgical antiseptic, and for the next four decades Lambert Pharmacal sold it as whatever seemed plausible, reaching dentists by 1895 with an oral-antiseptic pitch. Kenvue, which owns the brand today, says it wasn't clear what the product was for. While the company listed the uses and let each buyer pick one, a bottle of germ killer was priced at whatever germ killer cost. What changed was naming a condition and selling the liquid as its answer. Company president Gerard B. Lambert and his copywriters Milton Feasley and Gordon Seagrove took the word "halitosis" out of an old medical dictionary, made chronic bad breath a verdict your friends would never say out loud, and introduced the product last, as the way out of it. The historian Roland Marchand laid out both sides of the reframe in *American Heritage* (April/May 1985): Lambert Pharmacal's profits ran near $100,000 a year in 1920 and 1921 and passed $4,000,000 by 1927, while the Listerine advertising budget rose from $100,000 in 1922 to $5,000,000 in 1928. By 1926 rival advertisers were describing their own work as "the halitosis appeal." The formula in the bottle held still through all of it.
