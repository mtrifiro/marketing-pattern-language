> *Every product sits somewhere on the line from narrow wedge to broad platform and that position governs its pricing, its moat, and its path to expand.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Wedge and platform are points on a continuous line: each adjacent use case moves the company toward greater breadth and changes how it prices, defends, and expands. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat wedge and platform as fixed identities to declare. Claim platform breadth you haven't earned and the buyer with one urgent problem finds nothing specific to adopt, so you win no beachhead to expand from.

The two ends of the gradient behave differently. A wedge is one sharp use case for one clear buyer: easy to adopt, easy to explain, fast to sell. A platform covers many use cases for many buyers, which raises the ceiling and deepens the moat and makes the thing much harder to sell, because diffuse value invites the fatal question "what is it?" that [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] warns about. Neither end is an identity a company gets to declare. Between them runs a continuous gradient, and every product sits somewhere along it whether anyone has named the position or not.

Misreading that position breaks things in two directions. A company that forces the platform play early leads with breadth its buyer can't parse, dilutes the one sharp value that would have won a beachhead, and ends up a vague everything-tool nobody urgently needs. A company that clings to a pure wedge too long caps its own ceiling and leaves the surrounding territory open for a platform to absorb its use case as one feature among many. Neither position is permanent, and a team that treats the gradient as a fixed identity can't see the move sitting in front of it.

What the gradient asks of a company is a position and a direction: where it sits on the line today, and which adjacent territory it takes next. (The market this gradient describes is the one being engineered in [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]]; the wedge, in market terms, is the [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]]; the breadth you accumulate becomes the moat of [[MS-028 Data Gravity Lock-In]].)

## Complications

- **The wedge sells easily and caps low; the platform sells hard and reaches high.** Sharp focus wins fast and hits a ceiling, while breadth has a high ceiling and confuses the buyer at the door.
- **Ambition pulls toward platform; the market adopts a wedge.** You want the bigger platform market, and your buyers with a specific pain reach for a specific solution first. Both pulls are real at once, and they point at different first pages.
- **Leading with breadth dilutes the wedge.** A platform pitch to a buyer with one problem blurs the value that would have won them, and the blur costs you the deal you were closest to.
- **The moat changes along the gradient.** A wedge's advantage is depth and focus, while a platform's is breadth, data gravity, and network. Defensibility built for one doesn't carry to the other, so the advantage you spent years compounding is the one you have to rebuild.
- **Moving along the gradient must be earned.** Expanding from wedge toward platform requires you to win each adjacent use case in turn, and a stage skipped leaves nothing underneath the next one.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Enter as one sharp wedge and expand only through adjacent use cases the current buyer reaches for. Ask a buyer to complete “we use it for ___”: one crisp answer means wedge, several crisp answers mean platform, and hesitation means the middle is still muddled.

1. **Enter as a wedge,** because the market lets you in that way and a sharp one wins its [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] completely. Win one use case for one buyer so thoroughly you own it. Price and position for exactly that: wedges price simply and sell on a single sharp value. Do not pitch like a platform you have not become.

2. **Expand along the gradient deliberately,** adding the adjacent use cases your buyer already values (the land-and-expand motion that Part II develops). Accumulate breadth as earned territory rather than as an early claim. As the breadth grows, let the moat evolve with it: the focus-and-depth advantage of the wedge gives way to [[MS-028 Data Gravity Lock-In]] and network advantages, and pricing shifts from a sharp per-use-case value toward consolidation-and-breadth.

3. **Match your story to your position.** Announce yourself as a platform before you have earned one and you confuse the buyer and build nothing deep. Read your own position honestly: know whether this quarter's job is to win the wedge or to take the next use case. The platform is a destination reached by a sequence of won wedges; the sequence itself is the strategy.

4. **Locate yourself with this test:** ask your buyer to complete the sentence "we use it for ___." A wedge earns one crisp answer; a platform earns several. A vague, hesitant answer means you are stuck in the confusing middle, having left the wedge without earning the platform. That is where companies stall. Rewrite your story until the answer is crisp.

5. **Keep the expansion crisp at every step.** The second use case should be one your buyer reaches for naturally, so the story stays legible as it widens. The danger zone is the muddled middle, too broad to be the obvious answer to one problem and too shallow to be the system of record for many. Widen only as fast as you can keep that sentence sharp. A legible product expands; a diffuse one drifts.

## Implications

The gradient gives your expansion a shape and a sequence: win the wedge, take the adjacent use case, accumulate breadth, let the moat evolve from depth to data gravity. It connects the focus patterns to the moat patterns (a [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] is a wedge and a mature [[MS-027 Economic Moat]] is often a platform's breadth). It hands Part II the land-and-expand motion that carries you along the axis. Build the shelf with [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]], then name the depth with [[MS-027 Economic Moat]] and [[MS-028 Data Gravity Lock-In]] as the breadth arrives. This pattern sits inside [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] as one structural coordinate of the market being designed.

The tension runs both directions and demands constant judgment. The pull to claim platform early is strong; the platform story raises better and dreams bigger. Yield to it before the wedge is won and you produce a diffuse product that wins nothing. Rarer and quieter is the opposite failure: so comfortable in your profitable wedge that you never expand, until a platform absorbs your use case as a feature and your ceiling becomes your coffin. Do not drift there. Knowing which move the moment calls for (hold the wedge or take the next step) is the judgment the gradient demands. Make it again as your product and market move.

## Examples

**Slack from wedge to hub (2013–2019); the move to follow.** Slack entered as a sharp wedge: team messaging that beat email for one job. It won that use case completely before becoming anything wider. Only once it owned the wedge did it expand along the gradient, adding integrations, apps, and workflows until it sat closer to the platform end as the hub tools connected through. Had Slack launched as "a platform for work," the buyer would have had nothing specific to adopt. The wedge was the way in; the platform was the earned destination.

**Amazon from books to platform (1995–2006); the patient sequence.** Amazon began with the sharpest consumer-commerce wedge: books. The October 1995 press release described a web retailer built around more than one million titles. Amazon's company history still names the online bookstore as the starting point. The company then expanded through adjacent retail categories before AWS turned internal infrastructure into a platform business in 2006. The platform was the earned result of a sequence that began with one narrow category.

**Shimano from one part to the whole drivetrain (1921–1984).** Shozaburo Shimano opened Shimano Iron Works in Sakai, Japan, in February 1921 making one thing, the bicycle freewheel, which the company's own founder history says he picked because it demanded the highest level of technology of any bicycle component. A wedge chosen for that reason is hard to take away from you. Shimano added neighbouring parts over the following decades, and its product history dates the Dura-Ace line to 1973, supplied first to a professional road racing team as a group of components meant to be raced together. Sheldon Brown's Dura-Ace reference puts indexed shifting on the Dura-Ace 7400 generation in 1984, and the gradient's far end shows itself there: for a lever click to land on a gear, the shifter, cable, derailleur and cogs have to be engineered as one system. Shimano was by then selling the drivetrain, and compatibility inside its own catalogue became a defense that no single excellent part had ever given it. Sixty-three years run from the freewheel to the indexed group, and Shimano took the adjacent parts in order.

**The everything-tool that won nothing; the trap to avoid.** I watched a company insist from day one that it was a platform, leading every pitch with breadth and a diagram of a dozen use cases. Buyers with one urgent problem could not find themselves in the diagram, adopted nothing, and the company never won a wedge to expand from. We cut the story to a single sharp use case and won that segment completely. The breadth the company had been claiming finally started to become real, one earned use case at a time. The difference was everything.
