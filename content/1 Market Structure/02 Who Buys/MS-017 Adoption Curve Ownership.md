> *Each segment of the adoption curve is a separate market, with its own buyer, its own message, and its own price.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Each adoption-curve segment is a separate market. The product, proof, message, and price that win one segment can repel the next at the crossing, so every crossing demands a deliberate retool of the offer. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] and [[MS-002 Market Type Sets the Playbook]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat "the market" as one audience moving toward you at one speed. It adopts in distinct segments, and the stall at the seam between two of them looks like a demand problem more leads would fix.

Everett Rogers named the segments and Geoffrey Moore made them operational in *Crossing the Chasm* (1991): innovators, early adopters, the early majority, the late majority, laggards. The two that matter most for a young market are the early adopters and the early majority, separated by the widest gap and wanting opposite things. Early adopters buy vision. They'll tolerate rough edges, missing features, and hand-holding in exchange for an advantage before anyone else has it, and they're comfortable being first. The early majority buys proof. They want references from people exactly like them, a complete product, and above all a reduction of risk, and being first is the last thing they want to be. Between "give me the edge" and "show me it's safe" lies Moore's chasm.

From inside a company, the chasm feels like traction. You win the innovators and early adopters, you see real usage and real love, and you conclude you've got product-market fit. Then growth stalls, and the stall reads as a demand problem to be solved with more leads or a lower price. The very qualities that won the early adopters, novelty, configurability, being cutting-edge, are the qualities the early majority distrusts, because "cutting-edge" reads to them as "unproven" and "configurable" reads as "unfinished." A company still selling the future to a segment that wants a safe standard pours money into a funnel that no longer converts and never learns why.

The segments don't share references. A mainstream buyer wants a company just like theirs, in their industry, at their scale, running the thing without drama, and a pack of enthusiastic visionaries counts for very little. Proof earned in one segment doesn't cross into the next. (Which segment you commit to first is [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]]; the referenceable groups inside a segment are [[MS-015 Buyers Come in Clusters]]; the stable name the mainstream needs to file you under is [[MS-005 Category Before Product]].)

## Complications

- **Segments buy for opposite reasons.** Early adopters buy vision and advantage; the early majority buys proof and safety, so one pitch can't serve both.
- **Early love misleads.** Enthusiast adoption looks like product-market fit but doesn't predict mainstream demand.
- **Winning qualities become repelling qualities.** Novelty and configurability win the early segment and alarm the next, so the traits you were praised for become the ones you're asked to defend.
- **References don't travel across the chasm.** The mainstream trusts its own kind, so proof accumulated among visionaries carries almost no weight with pragmatists.
- **Crossing feels like betrayal.** Owning the next segment means dropping some of what made you special to the first, and your earliest users will be the first to say so.
- **The gap's width varies with the market you entered.** Steve Blank argues in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005) that how far apart the two segments sit depends on the kind of market you're in, and that inside an established market the visionaries and the pragmatists are often the same buyer, so the crossing can be short or absent.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Treat each segment as its own market to be won in turn, each with an owner, a message, and a price, and re-tool deliberately as you cross from one to the next.

1. **Diagnose honestly which segment you're actually in.** The wrong self-diagnosis is fatal. While you're selling early adopters, sell vision and advantage, price for strategic value rather than volume, and accept that the product is incomplete and the support is high-touch.
2. **When you cross to the early majority, change the goods rather than just the effort.** Complete the product into the whole product, the thing that solves the mainstream buyer's problem without any assembly required of them. Bill Davidow named that idea in *Marketing High Technology* (1986), and Moore made it the centre of the crossing. Lead with references and risk reduction instead of novelty. Give the buyer a stable [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] to file you under, because the mainstream buys established categories rather than curiosities. Cross on a narrow front: win one tight, referenceable [[MS-015 Buyers Come in Clusters]] of the early majority completely, a [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]], and let its proof pull the next cluster over.
3. **Shift the message and often the price by segment.** Early adopters will pay a premium for the edge; the mainstream pays for safety and standardization, and as the market matures the premium erodes on its own, which is the concern of [[MS-007 Maturity Kills Premium]]. Treat owning the curve as [[MS-031 Market Segmentation]] along the axis of time and readiness, priced deliberately at each stage ([[MS-018 Price Creates the Buyer]]). Never run one message, one price, and one product across the whole curve and wonder why the machine that worked last year has seized.
4. **Tell a chasm apart from an ordinary slump, and respond to the chasm.** The two look identical on a growth chart and call for opposite responses, so read the specific tells. Your win rate falls even as lead volume holds, because the new leads are pragmatists your enthusiast pitch doesn't convert. Prospects ask questions your early adopters never did, about references in their own industry, about support SLAs, about who else like them runs you in production. Deals stall on "who else uses this" rather than on price, and your champions, who used to sell your vision internally, start asking you for proof they can show a skeptical boss. When you see that cluster of signals, don't pour money into the top of the funnel; a wider funnel of the wrong segment just fails more expensively. Spend instead on what the mainstream is asking for: complete the whole product for one beachhead vertical, manufacture two or three references from inside it, and settle the category name so a cautious buyer can file you. Cross by re-tooling, and read the stall correctly rather than buying your way deeper into the chasm.

## Implications

Own the curve and you give the market a sequence instead of a single push. Use it to explain the post-early-adopter stall that founders so often misread, and to tell you what to change, the product's completeness, the proof, the category, the price, rather than just how hard to push. Open [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] for the narrow front you cross on, and feed the demand-generation and positioning machinery of Part II, which you'll re-tune segment by segment. It sits inside [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] and beside [[MS-015 Buyers Come in Clusters]], [[MS-005 Category Before Product]], and [[MS-007 Maturity Kills Premium]].

Crossing requires giving up some of what made you beloved to your first segment, and the timing is a judgment call rather than a formula. Cross too early and you dilute the focus that was winning the early adopters; cross too late and you exhaust the enthusiast market and stall at the chasm's edge. Plan for different people and a different motion inside the company at each crossing, and hold owning the curve as an organizational discipline as much as a marketing one.

## Examples

**The stall I mistook for a demand problem.** A company I worked with had a beloved early-adopter base and a growth chart that suddenly flattened. We spent a quarter buying leads to fix a "top-of-funnel problem" that wasn't one. The real issue was the chasm: the pragmatists we were now reaching wanted a reference from their own industry, a finished product, and a category name, and we were still handing them a configurable toolkit and a vision deck. Growth resumed only when we completed the whole product for one vertical and led with a peer's logo.

**NoSQL and the mainstream's need for a category.** The NoSQL market crossed its chasm when the category stabilized into a named part of the modern data stack, with clear operational and analytical uses, more than when the engines got better. Gartner published its first Magic Quadrant for operational database management systems on 21 October 2013 with MongoDB and its NoSQL peers inside it, and from that point a cautious buyer had a shelf to point at. The revenue followed the shelf rather than leading it: MongoDB reported $101.4 million of revenue and more than 3,200 customers for the year ending 31 January 2017. Early adopters had bought the databases regardless of category; the early majority refused to until the market told them what shelf these things sat on and which of them was safe to standardize on.

**Slack and Salesforce completing the whole product.** Both crossed from an enthusiast base into the mainstream by building the parts the early majority demanded and the early adopters never asked for. Slack added the admin, security, and compliance layer that let cautious enterprises adopt what startups already loved, and shipped it as Enterprise Grid on 31 January 2017, three years after its public launch; IBM had 165,000 people on Slack by 2019 and made it the standard for all 350,000 employees in February 2020. Salesforce built the references and the enterprise assurances that moved it beyond its early SMB believers, and by February 2007 it could name a 25,000-seat deployment at Merrill Lynch. In each case the message shifted from "the exciting new way" to "the proven standard." Make the same shift when you cross; that re-tooling is what a crossing requires.

**Hybrid seed corn, 1943.** One product met two segments and needed two kinds of proof. Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross interviewed 259 farmers in two Iowa communities about hybrid seed corn and published the study in *Rural Sociology* in 1943. The seed plainly yielded more, and adoption still took about thirteen years to reach nearly every farm. The two segments took their evidence from different places. Farmers first heard about hybrid seed from salesmen, and what persuaded them was a neighbor who had planted it, with about five years between hearing and sowing. Everett Rogers built the diffusion curve out of this study, and the behavior it recorded, enthusiasts buying on a vendor's claim while the majority waits for a peer's field, is the same seam you cross today.
