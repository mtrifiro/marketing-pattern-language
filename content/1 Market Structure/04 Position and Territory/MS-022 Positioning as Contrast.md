> *A buyer learns what you are by understanding what you replace; choose that contrast, or the market will choose it for you.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A buyer learns what a product is by holding it beside a known alternative, so whoever chooses that comparison also chooses the position. It sits below [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]], [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]], and [[MS-002 Market Type Sets the Playbook]] and above [[CR-002 Positioning Statement]] and [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't describe what you are in a vacuum, listing features and benefits. A position stated without a contrast gets filed against the incumbent by default and judged on criteria the incumbent wrote.

Meaning is relative. Al Ries and Jack Trout built *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981) on that observation, arguing that a position is held in the buyer's head against whatever is already filed there. They were describing the ladder of consumer brands in a person's memory; the same reflex decides which alternative a B2B committee measures you against. When a buyer meets something new, they place it against the thing they already understand, because that's the only way an unfamiliar object acquires meaning at all. First they file it under a category, the move [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] covers. Then they pick something inside that category to weigh it against. When a company doesn't choose that second thing, the buyer chooses it, reaching for the most familiar reference in the room, which is almost always the market leader.

The choice arrives as a question, several times a week. Buyers ask what else you're looking at. Procurement asks what this replaces. The business case has to name the budget line the money comes out of. Each answer fixes a comparison, and a company that hasn't picked one supplies whatever comes to hand. Sales names the rival it lost to last quarter. Marketing names the leader, because that's who the analysts write about. The website compares against three vendors who turned up in the same search results. Nobody decided anything. Six people each answered a live question with the nearest available reference, and the company now gets measured against a leader it never chose to fight.

Being filed against the leader is expensive. The incumbent trained the market to value what it happens to be good at, so its criteria arrive pre-loaded and you read as a thinner version of it: missing features, less proven, a risk. You can be genuinely better in a way that matters and still lose. What to do about the criteria themselves belongs to [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]]. The prior question is which alternative you're standing next to when they get applied. April Dunford makes the competitive alternatives the first component of positioning in *Obviously Awesome* (2019), and counts the status quo among them, since the buyer was handling the problem somehow before you arrived. [[MS-025 Competitive Geography]] maps that whole field. This pattern spends the map on one decision: which alternative you ask the buyer to hold you against.

The obvious response is to describe yourself better. Sharper copy, a cleaner feature table. It doesn't work, because a description answers only what you do. The buyer also has to know what you stand in place of, and a description that leaves that blank gets it filled in by whoever's most familiar. You can win every row of the comparison table and still lose the deal, because the table belonged to the incumbent before you filled it in.

## Complications

- **The default costs nothing and yours costs time.** The buyer's nearest reference is free and instant. A contrast you choose has to be taught, and teaching it takes meetings you may not get.
- **The flattering contrast may not be credible.** The alternative that shows you best might not be the one the buyer actually reaches for, and you can't impose a frame the market won't accept.
- **Choosing a contrast means conceding something.** Naming the alternative you beat also admits the ones you don't, which feels like surrendering ground you could have contested.
- **The deal and the category pull apart.** A rep re-frames against whoever's in the room this week. Each of those local choices is defensible, and the sum of them erases the contrast the company chose.
- **Sharpening the contrast shrinks the market.** Becoming the obvious answer for one slice means becoming irrelevant to the rest, and the revenue arithmetic gets worse before it gets better.
- **The status quo trades a fast fight for a slow one.** Contrasting against doing nothing takes you off the incumbent's ground and puts you in a longer argument about whether to change at all.

## Recommendations

> [!check] A contrast counts as chosen when you can name the single alternative you're asking to be judged against and state the difference in one sentence a buyer repeats back unprompted. Anything that takes a paragraph is a description, and the market will file you against the leader.

Start from the real alternatives the buyer weighs, drawn from [[MS-025 Competitive Geography]], and pick the one where your strongest difference decides the outcome. Then work these moves:

1. **Name what you replace before you name what you do.** Write the sentence a buyer would use inside their own business case: this takes the place of X. If three people in the company write that sentence three different ways, the contrast hasn't been chosen, and every rep is picking one live in the room.
2. **Re-segment when the sharpest contrast calls for it.** "The CRM for healthcare providers" beats "a better CRM", because it changes which alternative the buyer weighs you against. You stop competing with a leader that fits everyone adequately and start competing with a general-purpose tool that fits this buyer badly.
3. **Contrast against the status quo when that's the honest frame.** Plenty of deals get decided against a spreadsheet, a manual process, or a decision to wait. Name that alternative out loud and the conversation moves to whether to change at all, the question [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] exists to answer.
4. **Hold the contrast when a live deal offers you a different one.** A buyer who volunteers a comparison is handing you their frame, and accepting it wins the meeting at the cost of the position. Answer in your own terms and steer back.
5. **Keep it true.** A buyer who accepts your frame and then finds you don't own the ground you claimed will punish the gap harder than they'd have punished honest positioning. Pick the alternative where the advantage is real and provable, and let the proof carry it.

Compression is the evidence that a choice got made. A company that has genuinely picked its alternative can say what it stands in place of in a handful of words. A company still hedging needs five slides to gesture at it, and the buyer has stopped listening well before slide five.

## Implications

A chosen contrast hands the buyer a frame in which your strengths are the deciding criteria, and it moves the sale off the incumbent's home field. Flow it down into the [[CR-002 Positioning Statement]] and the [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]] of Part IV, and use it to lift the price ceiling by giving the buyer a more expensive comparison set ([[MS-020 Profitability Envelope]]). Keep the division clear: this pattern chooses the alternative you ask to be judged against, and [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]] chooses the dimension that judging runs on. It sits inside [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]], the interpretation it serves, and beside [[MS-023 Category Creation]], the more radical move of building a new frame instead of choosing among existing ones.

Hold the contrast as a commitment and defend it. Accept a comparison and you accept its weaknesses too, so pick a frame the buyer finds credible; a flattering contrast nobody believes is just a claim. Contrasts also decay. Competitors reposition, the market learns, and the alternative that once separated you turns into a peer, so the choice comes up for review on a schedule.

## Examples

**Avis named its alternative out loud (1962 to 1963).** Avis was the second-largest car-rental company in America and had been losing money for years when DDB copywriter Paula Green, working with art director Helmut Krone, wrote "When you're only No. 2, you try harder. Or else." The line pointed at Hertz and made effort the criterion a renter should judge on, an axis the leader couldn't claim without conceding it had been coasting. Ries and Trout count thirteen straight losing years before the campaign, then $1.2 million of profit in its first year, $2.6 million in the second and $5 million in the third. Seth Stevenson, writing in *Slate* in 2013, puts the share gap against Hertz at 61 percent to 29 in 1963, closing to 49 against 36 by 1966.

**Zoom against the incumbents (2013 to 2019).** Zoom released version 1.0 in January 2013, after a public beta the previous August. Its S-1 would later name Webex and Skype for Business as the legacy web meeting services it competed against, alongside Google and LogMeIn. Matching that field feature for feature meant fighting on a checklist someone else wrote, and Zoom refused it. It chose a contrast, simplicity and reliability, and made "it just works" the criterion that mattered, against tools everyone found clunky. By defining the comparison as ease and call quality while the incumbents sold feature breadth, Zoom turned their bloat into a weakness and became, for a while, synonymous with the category it entered. Its March 2019 S-1 reported an average customer Net Promoter Score over 70 in 2018 and revenue of $330.5 million for the year ended 31 January 2019, up 118 percent.

**Snowflake against the old warehouse (2020).** Snowflake didn't invent data warehousing; it entered an existing category and chose its contrast precisely. It positioned against the traditional on-premise warehouse, turning the old category's real pains, rigid capacity, the coupling of storage and compute, the operational burden, into the selection criteria, and presented itself as the cloud-native answer to each. Customers already believed they needed a warehouse, so Snowflake spent its energy defining what a good one now looked like, on axes the incumbents couldn't follow. Its August 2020 S-1 reported revenue of $264.7 million for the year ended 31 January 2020, up 174 percent.

**The re-segmentation I watched work.** A company I advised was losing every deal as "a cheaper alternative to the market leader", a contrast that made it look like a discount and little else. We changed the alternative. It stopped positioning against the leader for everyone and started positioning as the only tool built for one underserved vertical, where the leader was a clumsy fit. The same product, measured against a different alternative, went from a weak second choice to the obvious first choice for that slice, and the discount conversation disappeared.
