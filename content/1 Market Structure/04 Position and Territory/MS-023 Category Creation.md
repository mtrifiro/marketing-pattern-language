> *If no shelf fits you, build the shelf and teach the market its name.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Category creation begins when no existing shelf can carry a genuinely new product. The company has to name the space and teach buyers why it deserves a budget. It sits below [[MS-005 Category Before Product]], [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]], and [[MS-002 Market Type Sets the Playbook]] and above [[MS-024 Category Entry Point]] and [[MS-026 Fast Follower Arrives]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't force a genuinely novel product onto the nearest existing shelf; the wrong shelf buries the value that makes it matter. And don't stand up a new category unless you'll fund the years of market education it demands, because most companies quit halfway.

You reach this fork when your product does something the existing categories don't name. Positioning inside an established category ([[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]]) is faster, cheaper, and lower-risk, and for most products it's the right call, because it borrows the market's existing awareness and budgets. But when your product's real value lives outside every existing shelf, piggybacking on the nearest one caps you at that shelf's ceiling and hides the thing that makes you matter. A truly novel data product filed under "database" inherits database expectations, database budgets, and database comparisons, none of which fit.

Creating the category instead means budgeting for the real work, and misjudging that work is why so few succeed. Category creation goes well beyond a naming exercise or a clever tagline; it's a multi-year campaign to install a new idea in the market's head. Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead, and Kevin Maney gave the work its name in *Play Bigger* (2016), calling it category design and arguing that the company which defines a category takes most of that category's economics. The claim here is narrower and harder to dodge: the campaign is what makes a budget line exist, and holding the category afterward is a separate job. Your buyer doesn't yet know they have the problem, or has no language for it, or doesn't believe it deserves a budget, so the campaign evangelizes the problem and makes it legible and urgent before it offers a solution. It also convinces analysts to write about a space that doesn't exist yet, and where nobody in the buyer's org owns the mandate to care, it defines a new job title so someone does. Companies routinely start this work, run out of patience or money when the market won't convert on the old sales timeline, and retreat to a "better X" position after paying the education cost and capturing none of the reward.

The worst outcome pairs the expense of category creation with the ceiling of piggybacking. (The frame within which a category is judged is [[MS-005 Category Before Product]]; the narrative that carries the new idea is [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]]; the trigger that brings a buyer into the new category is [[MS-024 Category Entry Point]].)

## Complications

- **A novel product resists every existing shelf.** When the value is genuinely new, the nearest category hides it, yet the market has nowhere else to file it.
- **Creation is slow; the sales clock is fast.** Installing a new idea takes years, while quarterly pressure pushes a retreat to a familiar category before the education pays off.
- **You must sell the problem before the product.** Buyers won't fund a solution to a problem they can't name, so the first campaign is about the problem and feels like it's selling nothing.
- **The reward is winner-take-most.** Whoever defines a category tends to become its default, so the prize for finishing is large and the penalty for quitting halfway is total.
- **Defining the category and keeping it are different achievements, and the evidence on the second one is unkind.** Peter Golder and Gerard Tellis studied roughly 500 brands across 50 product categories for the *Journal of Marketing Research* in May 1993, and in the 36 categories with records good enough to score, 47% of market pioneers failed outright against 8% of the early leaders who followed them. Those early leaders entered an average of 13 years after the pioneer, and they hold the category today in 53% of the cases against the pioneer's 11%. The authors add that their sample was chosen in a way that favoured pioneers, so 47% is a floor. What transfers to the follower is the expensive part: the moment the category works it becomes legible to everyone, and the education you paid for is sitting in the market where anyone can pick it up.
- **Analysts and language are gatekeepers.** A category becomes real when analysts name it and buyers use its words, and neither is under your direct control.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Create a category only when the value has no existing buyer shelf, then fund the full teaching campaign. Treat the category as established when prospects, analysts, and competitors use its vocabulary without attribution.

Begin with the problem rather than the product. Make the buyer feel a pain they had tolerated or never named, and give that pain a memorable frame, because a category is built on a universally recognized problem more than on a clever solution. Then run the category's own disciplines, the same ones [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] names for the create path:

1. **Standardize the vocabulary.** Teach the market the words to use, and use them consistently everywhere.
2. **Define the metrics of success.** Set the yardstick the new category measures itself against, so you own the scorecard.
3. **Enlist the analysts.** Pitch them the space rather than your product, so a market guide appears and a budget line follows.
4. **Name the new role.** If no one in the buyer's org owns the problem, define the title, because a category with no owner inside the account has no champion.
5. **Fund the timeline.** Category creation converts on a multi-year education curve, so fund and measure it as market-building rather than as this quarter's pipeline.

Compose the move with the rest of the position patterns. The new category still needs a sharp [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]] (usually against the old, unnamed way of coping), a [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] of change to make the "why now" land, and designed [[MS-024 Category Entry Point]], the specific triggers that bring a buyer into the category. Done fully, you win the category-king position: the market thinks of the problem in your words and reaches for you first.

One signal tells you the category has taken hold: the market starts using your words without attribution. When prospects, analysts, and even competitors describe the problem in the language you introduced, you're the reference point, whatever your current share. Until then, keep working, and don't declare victory early; that's the most dangerous moment in the campaign, because a half-installed category can still collapse back into the adjacent one it came from. Fund category creation as a separate line from product marketing, give it its own multi-year horizon, and track its own measures: share of the conversation, analyst coverage, the spread of the new vocabulary, the existence of the job title. Judged on quarterly pipeline, category creation looks like it's failing right up until it succeeds, so hold the line past the point where most companies stop one year too soon.

## Implications

Once real, a created category is among the most durable advantages you can hold, because the market thinks about the problem in language you defined, and every competitor who enters gets compared to the definition you wrote. Move next to [[MS-024 Category Entry Point]] and own the moments buyers enter the category, and feed the demand-creation machinery of Part II, since a new category is demand creation rather than demand capture. Keep it inside [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] as the ambitious branch of that pattern's fork.

Hold the discipline on cost and reversibility. Category creation is expensive and slow, and you can spend heavily and still fail if the problem you named turns out not to be urgent or broad enough to sustain a market. It also invites imitation once proven, so convert your head start into a real [[MS-027 Economic Moat]] before fast-followers arrive with the category's education already paid for. Creating the category is necessary but not sufficient; own it durably, which is the harder, later work.

## Examples

**Gainsight and Customer Success (2009–2020).** In the early 2010s no one was shopping for a "customer success platform," because the category didn't exist. Gainsight could have filed the product under an existing software category and inherited its ceiling. Instead it evangelized the discipline of Customer Success, argued that managing renewals and churn deserved dedicated software and dedicated people, invested in conferences and thought leadership, and helped define the job title of the Customer Success Manager. The work was slow and looked, for a while, like marketing that wasn't selling anything. The payoff was large. Vista Equity Partners announced a majority investment in Gainsight on 30 November 2020 that valued the company at $1.1 billion, eleven years after it was founded, and Forbes, reporting the deal, described Gainsight and its chief executive Nick Mehta as having spent the better part of a decade popularizing customer success as a software category. Eleven years is the length of timeline this pattern is telling you to fund.

**Salesforce and "No Software" (service launched February 2000).** Salesforce named an era rather than shipping only a product. Its own registration statement dates the company to February 1999 and the start of the service to February 2000. Salesforce framed on-premise software itself as the enemy and evangelized a new way to consume applications, standing up the category the market would come to call SaaS. The category outlived the specific pitch and became the default assumption of the whole industry, which is what a fully won category creation looks like.

**Impossible Foods and plant-based meat (2011–2019).** Impossible Foods did category work in consumer food, where the buyer was deciding what dinner should be. It framed the problem around meat's environmental cost first, then built a product meant to satisfy meat eaters, launching the Impossible Burger in restaurants in 2016 and reaching Burger King locations across the United States with the Impossible Whopper in 2019. Burger King put the Impossible Whopper into more than 7,000 of its United States restaurants on 8 August 2019, and Restaurant Brands International's third-quarter results that year credited the launch with 5% comparable sales growth at Burger King in the United States, its strongest since 2015. The category work mattered because the product had to be read as meat made another way rather than as a veggie burger for vegetarians. Company materials and 2016–2019 launch coverage make the sequence to copy visible: problem frame first, restaurant proof second, mass-channel entry after that.

**Cirque du Soleil (1984 onward).** Competing as a circus meant a shrinking category with an audience of children and rising animal-act costs, on a shelf Ringling Bros. had owned for a century. Guy Laliberté's Quebec street troupe kept the acrobats, cut the animals and the star names, added theatrical narrative and original scores, and set ticket prices against theater, several times a circus ticket. The buyer changed with the frame: adults on a night out, and later corporate clients. W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne opened *Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005) with the case because what Cirque built fit neither existing shelf. Cirque never ran the vocabulary campaign this pattern prescribes; the show taught audiences what it was on its own, so in consumer markets the artifact itself can carry the education.
