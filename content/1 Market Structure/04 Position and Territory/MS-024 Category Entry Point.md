> *Nobody wakes up wanting your category; a situation pushes them into it, so own the situations.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A category enters the buyer's mind through a concrete trigger, the moment a situation turns an abstract problem into something worth solving now, so the company that owns the cue becomes the first name recalled when need appears. It sits below [[MS-023 Category Creation]] and [[MS-005 Category Before Product]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't market your category as if buyers were continuously shopping for it; they enter only when a specific situation triggers the need, and a company that isn't present at those trigger moments is invisible at the exact instant demand appears.

Most of the time your prospective buyer has no thought of your category; they're running their business, and the need for what you sell stays dormant until something makes it real: a system falls over, a competitor does something alarming, a regulation changes, a spreadsheet finally breaks, a new hire arrives with a different expectation. That cue, the moment a latent need turns active, is the category entry point, and it's where demand is actually born. Everything before it is background, and everything after it is a race among the vendors the buyer can bring to mind.

Companies nonetheless spend as if the buyer were always in-market, broadcasting product messages to people with no live need who will forget them. Jenni Romaniuk and Byron Sharp of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute made the point precisely, and gave the category entry point its name: a brand's growth depends on being remembered at the entry points that matter, on being mentally available in the situations that trigger the category. They measured it in consumer categories, where the trigger fires weekly and the buyer is always somewhere in the cycle. A business buyer can sit out of market for years, which raises what it costs you to be forgotten. Product messaging can be excellent and still leave a company absent from the buyer's mind at the one moment it counts. In a category you created yourself ([[MS-023 Category Creation]]), the entry points may not exist in the buyer's head at all, so part of creating the category is teaching buyers to recognize the moments that should send them looking.

The demand goes elsewhere: the need fires, the buyer reaches for whoever comes to mind, and it isn't you, for the simple reason that you weren't present at the trigger, whatever the quality of your product. (The category the entry points feed into is [[MS-005 Category Before Product]]; the act of standing up a new one is [[MS-023 Category Creation]]; whose situations to map is a question about [[MS-009 Buyer Archetypes]].)

## Complications

- **Buyers are out-of-market most of the time.** The need is dormant until a situation activates it, so continuous product broadcasting reaches people with no live demand.
- **Demand is born at the trigger.** The vendor who isn't associated with that moment is invisible when it fires, and you pay for that association long before there's any pipeline to justify the cost.
- **Mental availability decides the shortlist.** At the moment of need the buyer reaches for whoever comes to mind first, so being remembered at the trigger beats being persuasive later.
- **Entry points are many and situation-specific.** A category has several distinct triggers across different archetypes, and owning one isn't owning the others.
- **New categories have no triggers yet.** In a created category the buyer doesn't recognize the situations that should send them looking, so the education bill includes teaching them to recognize the moment itself.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Rank the real situations that send buyers into the category and attach the product to each one. For every trigger, ask buyers which name comes to mind first; the entry point isn't owned until yours does.

1. **List the moments.** For each [[MS-009 Buyer Archetypes]] you serve, write down the concrete events, failures, milestones, and changes that turn a dormant need into an active search. Describe them in the buyer's own language ("our data outgrew Postgres," "we failed a security audit," "a customer churned and no one saw it coming"), rather than in your feature language.
2. **Build presence around the triggers.** Create the content, the search presence, the partnerships, and the memorable associations that put you in the buyer's mind precisely when each situation occurs, rather than running a steady product drumbeat.
3. **Teach the trigger in a new category.** Where you created the category, help buyers recognize that a particular situation is a signal to act, which turns a background annoyance into a live category need.

That teaching is why entry points are a demand-creation instrument: they manufacture the moments intent is born and then own them, rather than harvesting intent that already exists. Own several entry points across archetypes to turn a category presence into compounding demand.

Produce the artifact: a short, ranked list of the situations that actually send buyers looking, written in the buyer's words and validated against real deals rather than imagined. For each entry point, ask a concrete question and then act on it: when this happens in the wild, are you the first name that comes up, and if not, what would put you there. Follow the answers to where marketing effort compounds, the content that ranks for the situation, the partnerships with the tools present at the moment of failure, the communities where the trigger gets discussed, and cut the effort that merely feels productive while reaching people with no live need. Claim only the entry points you can credibly own because you have genuine authority in that situation, so let the list discipline your ambition: own three triggers completely rather than fading into a faint presence at twenty. Owned deeply, a handful of entry points becomes a demand engine that runs whether or not the marketing team is spending that week.

## Implications

Own the entry points and you're present at the birth of demand rather than competing for attention after it, which lowers acquisition cost and pre-loads the shortlist. Hand the Part II demand engine its targets, since demand-generation programs built around real triggers convert far better than product broadcasts, and sharpen the [[MS-009 Buyer Archetypes]] work by grounding each type in the situations that actually move it. Keep this inside [[MS-023 Category Creation]] and [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] as the mechanism by which a category actually gets entered.

Hold the discipline against confusing entry points with keywords or campaigns. Entry points are numerous and shifting, so don't chase a long tail of trivial triggers and spread yourself thin, and don't mistake a search term for a genuine situation and win clicks from people with no real need. The triggers move as the buyer's world changes, so maintain the map. Owning the entry points is powerful because it puts you at the source of demand, and demanding because that source keeps moving.

Whole categories of compliance and security tooling get entered on a shock rather than a schedule: a failed audit, a breach in the news, a customer's security questionnaire the company suddenly can't answer. A vendor that is the obvious first search at that moment captures demand no amount of steady-state advertising to un-triggered buyers would have reached, because the buyer wasn't listening until the trigger fired.

## Examples

**Square and the lost card sale (2009 to 2014).** Square's history starts with one entry point: in 2009 Jim McKelvey lost the sale of a glass faucet at his St. Louis studio because he couldn't accept the buyer's American Express card, and he called Jack Dorsey about it. Square could have entered a regulated payments market through banking vocabulary aimed at buyers with no live need. Instead it owned a concrete trigger, a sale at risk because the merchant couldn't take the card in front of him. A payments category built for larger merchants suddenly became legible to food trucks, craftspeople, and independent shops. Square's 2015 S-1 reported $23.8 billion of gross payment volume in 2014.

**De Beers manufactured the moment (1938 to 1941).** Diamond prices were falling in 1938 when De Beers hired N.W. Ayer to attach the stone to one situation: the proposal. Ayer's program sent lecturers into American high schools to talk about the diamond engagement ring, so a girl learned the association years before she'd need it. Edward Jay Epstein, writing in *The Atlantic* in February 1982, reports that by 1941 Ayer could tell De Beers that US diamond sales had risen 55 percent since 1938. Nobody woke up wanting a diamond. De Beers made the engagement the moment that sent them looking.

**Teaching the trigger in a new category.** When I worked on a genuinely new category, the hard part wasn't the product; buyers didn't yet recognize the situation that should have sent them looking. We taught the entry point, publishing the story of the specific failure our category prevented so the next team to hit it would recognize the moment and know a category existed for it. Only once buyers could name the trigger did the category start generating its own demand.
