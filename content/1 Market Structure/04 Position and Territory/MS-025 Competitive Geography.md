> *Most of the deals you lose go to a spreadsheet, an in-house build, or no purchase at all.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> The buyer's competitive field includes named rivals, an in-house build, a manual workaround, and doing nothing. Each alternative changes the price, objections, and position the deal must survive, and the deciding rival often sits outside the vendor shortlist. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't define your competition as the handful of direct rivals you can name. A buyer weighs a much wider field, and most deals get decided against an alternative you never counted, most often the buyer's decision to keep things as they are.

Asked who its competitors are, a company names three or four vendors that look like it, then builds its battlecards, its pricing, and its positioning against them. Meanwhile the deals get decided against opponents that appear on no battlecard. The buyer who purchases nothing hasn't chosen a rival; they've chosen the status quo, a spreadsheet that mostly works, a manual process the team has learned to tolerate, or a plan to build it themselves next quarter. In business software the single most common outcome of a competitive deal is no decision. Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna measured it across roughly 2.5 million recorded sales conversations for *The JOLT Effect* (2022) and put it between 40 and 60 percent of qualified deals, with most of that loss traceable to the buyer's fear of getting the purchase wrong rather than contentment with what they already have. Either way the winning "competitor" is inertia, while the attention stays fixed on the vendor across the street.

The field runs wider in every direction. Substitutes solve the problem differently: the spreadsheet, the consultant, the workaround. Adjacent tools the buyer already owns can stretch to cover the need. The in-house build competes perennially for any technical buyer who can imagine writing it themselves. And underneath all of it sits non-consumption: the large population who have the problem and buy nothing at all, because the current options are too expensive, too complex, or too unfamiliar to be worth it. Clayton Christensen showed that non-consumption is usually the biggest market of all, and it stays invisible to a company that studies only the rivals it recognizes. April Dunford builds positioning on the same ground in *Obviously Awesome* (2019): the first component of her model is the set of competitive alternatives a buyer weighs, and she counts doing nothing among them, since the customer was handling the problem somehow before you arrived. Her use for the map is a positioning statement. The use here runs upstream of that, because the alternative that decides the deal also sets which territory you can hold and what the market will pay.

A narrow map has a price. You position against the wrong alternative, price against the wrong reference, and prepare for objections the buyer never raises while missing the one that kills the deal: "why change at all?" The act of designing a market rather than accepting the given field is [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]]; the contrast you draw once the map is clear is [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]]; the demand hidden in non-consumption is created through [[MS-023 Category Creation]].

## Complications

- **The rival you track is only part of the field.** You track the competitors that look like you, while deals get decided against the status quo and substitutes you never mapped.
- **Non-consumption is the largest segment.** The people who buy nothing usually outnumber the buyers of every vendor combined, and a rival-focused view can't see them.
- **Substitutes and in-house builds don't advertise.** The spreadsheet, the workaround, and the internal team compete silently and never show up in a vendor analysis.
- **The map changes the whole strategy.** Positioning against a vendor and positioning against "doing nothing" need different messages, pricing, and sales.
- **A wide map is uncomfortable.** Admitting your real competition is inertia or a spreadsheet flatters less than a rivalry with a named peer.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Map every alternative the buyer weighs and build around the one that decides the deal. Validate the map against one quarter of lost deals by recording what each buyer actually did; the chosen alternative is the competitor that mattered.

Draw the geography completely, then aim. Work it in this order:

1. **List every alternative the buyer weighs.** Start with the direct competitors, then keep going: the adjacent tools the buyer could stretch, the substitutes that solve the problem another way, the option to build it internally, and, above all, the status quo of doing nothing.
2. **For each, ask why the buyer currently prefers it and what would have to change for them to move.** Do not ask "how do we beat it on features." The answer to the first question is the real sale.
3. **Match the strategy to the deciding alternative.** Where a direct rival decides the deal, win it with a sharp [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]]. Where the status quo decides it, fight inertia with a [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] that makes the cost of doing nothing vivid and the moment to act clear ([[MS-024 Category Entry Point]]). Where an in-house build decides it, argue total cost and opportunity rather than features.
4. **Go after the non-consumption.** The largest prize is usually the buyers who have the problem and purchase nothing. Serve them by removing whatever makes the current options too expensive, too complex, or too unfamiliar to bother with, which is why non-consumption is the natural target of [[MS-023 Category Creation]] and of [[MS-021 Free Changes the Question]].

Keep the map honest with one practice: at the close of every lost deal, ask what the buyer actually did instead of buying, and record the answer literally. Run that across a quarter and the real competitive geography draws itself, usually showing that named rivals account for a minority of losses while the status quo, the in-house build, and the deferred decision account for the rest. Trust that record over any analyst's market map, because it describes the alternatives your specific buyer weighs. It also corrects the flattering error of treating every loss as a product gap, when the more common truth is that the buyer never became convinced the problem was worth solving. Aim the strategy at the one or two alternatives that decide the most deals, and redraw the map each year, because the terrain shifts even when the named rivals stay the same.

## Implications

Draw a complete map and it changes where you spend your effort. It shows that the largest opportunity is often non-consumption rather than share-stealing, points your positioning at the alternative that actually decides deals, and explains losses a rival-focused view would misdiagnose as product gaps. Feed it into [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]] as the true set of alternatives to choose a contrast from, and hand the Part II demand engine an honest picture of what it's really competing against. It sits inside [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]] as the terrain a designed market is drawn upon.

Do not let the widest map paralyze you; "we compete with everything" is as useless as "we compete with three vendors." Map completely, then decide which one or two alternatives govern the deal for your chosen buyer, and aim there. Keep the map alive as the market moves, because a map drawn once and filed away leaves you, a year later, fighting an enemy that has already changed.

A competitive analysis of vendors that never lists the in-house build misses the alternative that takes a large share of the deals. Selling to technical buyers, the most persistent competitor is the team that believes it could write the thing itself. A build mapped as a real competitor opens the argument about total cost of ownership and opportunity cost, the years of maintenance and the roadmap the team won't build while it's busy reinventing infrastructure. Naming it as a competitor is what makes that argument possible.

## Examples

**Losing to the spreadsheet.** A company I worked with tracked two named rivals obsessively and kept losing deals it had no rival in. We ran honest win-loss and followed where it pointed. The winner in most lost deals was a spreadsheet and a determined analyst; the buyer decided the problem was tolerable and the change wasn't worth it, and no battlecard addressed that opponent. Once we accepted inertia as the competition, the whole approach changed, from feature comparisons to a narrative about the mounting, hidden cost of the status quo, and the no-decision rate started to fall.

**Airbnb and the lodging map (2008–2011).** Assuming the competition is hotels gets the field wrong. Airbnb's own timeline says Airbed & Breakfast launched around SXSW in March 2008 with two bookings, then relaunched for the Democratic National Convention in August 2008 with 80 bookings. The fuller field held friends' couches, conference scarcity, spare rooms, landlords, trust, payment risk, and the choice to skip the trip. Airbnb's market came from lodging demand hotels couldn't serve well and from hosts who had never thought of their spare room as supply. A wider map shows the market the hotel set hides.

**Hearing aids and the market nobody was counting (2022).** A share map drawn from the handful of hearing-aid manufacturers misses that most of the market has never bought from any of them. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders puts about 28.8 million United States adults in the group who could benefit from a hearing aid. Among adults aged 20 to 69 who could benefit, about 16% have ever used one; among those 70 and older with hearing loss who could benefit, fewer than one in three has. Buying nothing is the largest alternative here, and it outweighs every named vendor combined. The move against it changed the terms of entry rather than the product. The Food and Drug Administration published a final rule on 17 August 2022, effective that 17 October, creating an over-the-counter category of hearing aids for adults with perceived mild to moderate hearing loss, and gave its aim as more affordable and more accessible options. Those are the two alternatives a vendor map can't see: the price and the appointment. Removing them is the fourth step above, and a regulator took it before any manufacturer did.
