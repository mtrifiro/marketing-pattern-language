> *Build the market size as a story; the number is only as good as the argument behind it.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A credible market size is a story about who buys, why they enter, and how one winnable segment leads to the next; TAM, SAM, and SOM stay grounded in a named buyer and an actual route to market. It sits below [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat market sizing as a spreadsheet fact to maximize. A top-down figure with no named buyer behind it tells an experienced reader you haven't chosen a market yet, and it lets your team mistake a huge total for a plan.

The classic artifact is the slide claiming a "$150 billion market," which turns out to mean "we sell HR software and HR is a huge industry." The number is arithmetic rather than insight: the whole industry multiplied by a hopeful share. It impresses no serious investor, and it does something worse than fail to impress. It reveals that the founder hasn't identified a beachhead, because a founder who knew exactly whom to win first would have said so. A vague, sprawling total addressable market is a vanity statistic, and vanity statistics buy wasted effort, false signals, and strategic drift: a team that believes it is chasing a $150 billion opportunity refuses the focus that actually wins.

Founders routinely misread the three numbers. Total addressable market is the theoretical ceiling, the revenue if every possible buyer on earth bought from you. Serviceable available market is the slice your product and motion could actually reach. Serviceable obtainable market is what you will realistically capture in the plan in front of you. None of them is a measurement. Each is a claim about how far a story extends: TAM is the far horizon if everything works, SAM is what your current model can serve, SOM is what you can honestly win next. Sizing becomes useful at the point where it names which pond you can own first and what owning it opens.

A number without that story is theater, and experienced readers know it. Why the smaller traceable figure outreads the sweeping one is the credibility mechanism [[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]] owns. (The market the sizing describes is the one you are designing in [[MS-001 Designed Market, Not Found Market]]; the discipline of building an argument rather than reciting features is [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]]; the first slice the story starts from is the [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]].)

## Complications

- **Sizing looks like arithmetic.** A market size is a number, so the pull is to maximize it and pass off a claim about the future as a fact about the present.
- **Big is easy and useless; precise is hard and useful.** A vast top-down TAM is trivial to assert and impossible to act on, while a credible bottom-up figure takes work and guides strategy.
- **Inflation backfires.** Serious investors read an oversized TAM as a signal of unfocus, so the vanity number damages the credibility it was meant to build.
- **Two audiences pull in two directions.** The board rewards a big number and the team needs an honest one, and the same slide has to serve both.
- **The powerful story looks less ambitious.** A precise, smaller figure feels modest even when it's the stronger argument, and modesty is the first thing the room hears.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Size the market from a named buyer, a real price, and a reachable motion, then trace the adjacent segments. Every figure must survive “where does that number come from?” with a named segment and the motion that reaches it.

Calculate bottom-up, and refuse the top-down shortcut: begin from a defined number of real buyers, at a real price, in a segment you can describe, then trace a credible path of expansion. Anchor the whole thing in the [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] so the sizing reads as a strategy. Build it in four moves:

1. **Start from one account.** Take a single sold or winnable buyer, at a real price, and count how many like them sit in the beachhead. That count is a serviceable obtainable market you can defend.
2. **Extend to the reachable neighbors.** The segments adjacent to the beachhead, reachable by the same motion with modest changes, are your serviceable available market.
3. **Name the horizon.** The full reach if the whole expansion works is the TAM. Put it last, as the final chapter rather than the opening claim.
4. **Fuse the number to the plan.** Write the sizing and the go-to-market as one document, so every figure traces to a named buyer and a real motion.

Then test it adversarially. Hand the number to a skeptical partner and have them interrogate it line by line; a figure you can defend that way, even a modest one, beats a larger number that collapses at the first serious question. Make each layer survive the question "where does that number come from," answered with a named segment and a real motion rather than a percentage of an industry total. When the number and the plan disagree, trust the plan and cut the number, because a market size that cannot be traced to a specific way of reaching specific buyers is a wish. Read the finished sizing as the shortest honest statement of your strategy, and produce it to think as much as to raise.

## Implications

Treating sizing as storytelling turns a vanity slide into a strategy artifact. Use it to force the focus a huge TAM lets a team avoid, to make the expansion path explicit, and to feed the fundraising narrative, where a credible bottom-up story raises better than a dazzling top-down one. Lean on [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] for the starting point, and carry the number into [[MS-035 Go-To-Funding vs. Go-To-Market Balance]], where you must reconcile the size you tell investors with the size you run the company on.

Enforce one discipline against a room that rewards the opposite. The honest number is smaller and less dazzling, and a fundraise pushes hard toward inflation, so hold the disciplined story anyway. Treat the sizing as a hypothesis to revisit as you learn rather than a monument to defend: the expansion path may not materialize and the adjacent segment may resist, so revise the number when reality corrects it. A market size, like any narrative in this language, earns its confidence through contact with reality.

## Examples

**The $150 billion vanity slide.** I've sat across from founders whose TAM slide claimed the whole of a huge industry, and the number always did the opposite of what they hoped. A top-down total tells the investor you haven't chosen a beachhead, can't say whom you'll win first, and will spread thin chasing an ocean. The founders who impressed me showed a smaller, bottom-up number they could defend to the decimal, and a story of how the small win opened a larger one.

**Uber's expanding story (2014).** Uber's sizing persuaded because it was a sequence rather than a single number. Bill Gurley made the argument in public in "How to Miss By a Mile: An Alternative Look at Uber's Potential Market Size" (Above the Crowd, July 2014), his answer to Aswath Damodaran's estimate that the company was worth $5.9 billion. Damodaran had sized Uber against the existing taxi and car-service market, roughly $100 billion, and taken a share of it. Gurley's objection was structural. A number anchored to the market as it stands can't price a company whose whole thesis is that cheaper rides and shorter waits pull in trips nobody takes today, so the sizing has to be built from the behavior you expect to change and defended step by step. The sequence started where the company could win completely, black cars in San Francisco, then told a credible expansion, from black cars to a cheaper tier, from one city to many, from rides to logistics. The far-horizon TAM was enormous, and it earned belief because every step traced from the one before it.

**Airbus, Boeing, and the same twenty years (2000–2021).** Two manufacturers looking at the same airlines, the same routes, and the same twenty-year window published market sizes that differed by nearly four times. Benjamin Esty and Pankaj Ghemawat collected the series in "Airbus vs. Boeing in Superjumbos: Credibility and Preemption" (Harvard Business School working paper 02-061). For passenger jets above 500 seats, Boeing's 2000 Current Market Outlook forecast 330 deliveries over twenty years; Airbus's Global Market Forecast for the same period put it at 1,235. Neither number was a percentage of an industry total. Each was built from the bottom, and the two models were arguing about different futures. Airbus estimated demand route by route, across some 10,000 passenger routes and nearly 2,000 airports, on the premise that traffic would keep concentrating through big hubs. Boeing forecast economic growth by region and then traffic across 51 markets, on the premise that traffic would fragment onto more direct routes. Airbus committed in December 2000 to a 555-seat aircraft at a launch cost of $12 billion, with internal projections of 1,500 planes over twenty years and an accounting break-even at 250. It announced the end of A380 production on 14 February 2019 and handed over the last one, the 251st, to Emirates on 16 December 2021. The forecast was 1,500, the break-even 250, and the delivered total 251. The sizing was an argument, the argument carried a $12 billion commitment, and the market answered it two decades later.

**Shrinking the TAM to win the room.** I once helped a founder cut their headline TAM by an order of magnitude before a raise, replacing "the entire market" with the specific segment they could win in eighteen months and the adjacent one it opened. The big number had walled the founder off from the money. The smaller one came with a plan investors could interrogate and believe. They leaned in.
