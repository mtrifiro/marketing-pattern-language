> *When fifty accounts decide your year, stop trawling for leads and go spear the fifty by name.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> When a short list of accounts decides the year, concentrated pursuit of each named target fits the market better than a net cast for anonymous leads and converts the power law into a named-account motion. It sits below [[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]] and [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] and above [[GTM-016 Account-Based Everything]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't run a volume marketing machine in a market where a handful of named accounts determine viability. Buy broad keywords and chase lead counts and you'll spend your budget reaching thousands of prospects who can't move the business, while the fifty who can go unaddressed.

You were probably trained to trawl: buy broad keywords, sponsor the big trade shows, generate marketing-qualified leads, and measure success by the quantity of fish in the net. In a market that follows a power law ([[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]]), that training is a category error. When most of the revenue and nearly all of the credibility will come from a small number of accounts, a thousand leads from the long tail are close to worthless, and the metric that rewards them, lead volume, is a vanity number that keeps the team busy reaching people who could never validate or fund the company. The net comes up full of minnows while the whales swim past unnoticed.

The waste runs deeper than money. A company optimized for volume builds the wrong organs: a demand-gen team tuned for reach, a sales floor of junior reps working a high-count pipeline, content pitched to the anonymous many. None of that machinery can win a specific Fortune 500 CTO, which is what this market requires. And because the volume machine produces impressive top-of-funnel numbers, it hides the failure; the dashboards look healthy right up until the quarter misses, because the fifty accounts that would have made the quarter were never on anyone's list. Trawling is measurable, scalable, and, in a power-law market, aimed at the wrong ocean.

The problem is a marketing motion at war with the market's structure. (The concentration that makes the few accounts decisive is [[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]]; the focused segment the named accounts often sit inside is the [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]]; the discipline of refusing the long tail to free the resources is [[MS-034 No Is the Most Profitable Word]].)

## Complications

- **Volume is measurable; named pursuit is bespoke.** Lead counts scale and report cleanly, while pursuing specific accounts one at a time is hard to systematize and slow to show numbers.
- **Most leads can't move the business.** In a power-law market the long tail can't validate or fund the company, so lead volume measures effort rather than progress.
- **The whales require intelligence more than reach.** Winning a specific large account depends on knowing its people, its needs, and who can introduce you, none of which broad marketing provides.
- **High-touch pursuit looks inefficient.** A dinner for ten or a custom package to one prospect reads as expensive on a cost-per-lead basis, even when it's the only spend that can land a decisive account.
- **Naming the list means ignoring the tail.** Concentrating on the accounts that matter requires declining the pipeline of those that don't, which feels like abandoning demand, and some of that demand goes and buys elsewhere.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Name the accounts that would make your year, pursue each one individually with concentrated, custom effort, and shift spend from awareness to intelligence.

Start from the tier-one list that [[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]] builds, the twenty to fifty accounts whose wins would make success inevitable, and run zero-waste marketing against it, where every dollar is aimed at a name on the list:

1. **Host the dinner, skip the webinar.** Fly ten CIOs to an intimate dinner instead of running a generic webinar.
2. **Send one package instead of a mass email.** Spend real money on a single customized package aimed at one prospect.
3. **Hire the heavy-hitter, retire the phone bank.** Bring on one senior rep who already knows the buyers and can get in the room instead of five juniors cold-calling a long list.
4. **Move the budget from awareness to intelligence.** Find out who the actual people are in each account, what they need, what will get them to act, and who can introduce you.

If only fifty companies in the world can make or break your year, treat spending to market to the other fifty thousand as waste, and name that waste out loud; that naming is the whole discipline.

Spear fishing composes with the rest of the focus patterns. Run it as the account-level tactic inside a [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]] when that beachhead is a short list of large accounts, and as the natural motion of a [[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]]. It demands the refusal that [[MS-034 No Is the Most Profitable Word]] describes, because the resources for high-touch pursuit come from declining the long tail. Done well, it becomes the origin of the account-based programs Part II formalizes, where marketing and sales orient the whole engine around named accounts rather than around a funnel of anonymous leads.

## Implications

Spear fishing aligns your marketing motion with the market's power-law structure, so the budget lands where the revenue and the credibility actually are. Expect it to restructure the organization: fewer and more senior reps, content aimed at specific accounts, intelligence and relationships ahead of reach. It opens the account-based-everything and support-is-sales motions of Part II, where the named-account orientation runs through the whole go-to-market. Treat it as the tactical face of [[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]] and a concentrated [[MS-032 Beachhead Market]].

Watch the boundary: spear fishing doesn't scale past the named list, so run it in power-law markets and stay out of it in volume ones. Misjudge which market you're in and you waste money in either direction. Trawl a power-law market and you miss the whales; spear fish a volume market and you leave most of the demand untouched. Concentrating on a few accounts also raises the concentration risk that [[MS-016 Power Law Accounts]] warns about, since a company that has spear-fished its way to a handful of enormous customers now lives and dies by them. The motion is powerful because it's focused, and fragile for the same reason.

Produce a named list and a plan for each name, closer to a military campaign than a marketing funnel. For every target account, answer four concrete questions: who will decide, what do they actually need, what would make them act now, and who can get you a warm introduction. Let the answers point you at bespoke moves: an executive dinner where the right buyers meet each other, a piece of original research aimed at one company's problem, a relationship cultivated patiently over quarters. Accept that these moves render the usual top-of-funnel metrics meaningless, because a program that will win ten accounts should be judged on those ten and nothing else. The hardest adjustment for a team raised on lead volume is learning to feel productive while generating almost no leads, because the work of spear fishing looks idle on a dashboard built for trawling right up until a decisive logo signs. Measure the named accounts, ignore the vanity counts, and staff for depth ahead of reach.

## Examples

**The dinner instead of the webinar.** The budget could buy a thousand cheap leads, which report well. In the markets where I've watched this work, the winning play looked absurd on a cost-per-lead spreadsheet. Fly a handful of the right CIOs to a private dinner, or spend five thousand dollars on a single package aimed at one prospect. That dinner put the company in a room with three people who could each sign a seven-figure deal, and one of those relationships was worth more than the entire lead list the same budget would have bought.

**Mesosphere going after the marquee names.** We could have run a broad campaign and hoped a Fortune 500 would wander in. When we needed enterprise validation at Mesosphere, we named the specific companies whose adoption would change every later conversation and pursued them individually, because one marquee logo running the platform at scale was worth more, in revenue and in precedent, than any volume of small accounts. We built the whole marketing motion around a short list of names.

**ASML's customers bought the company (July 2012).** ASML sells extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines to a handful of leading-edge chipmakers, and its Customer Co-Investment Program shows what marketing to a list that short looks like. Announced on 9 July 2012 and completed that August, it had Intel, TSMC, and Samsung fund €1.38 billion of ASML's research over five years and buy an aggregate 23 percent minority stake for €3.85 billion in cash. No campaign was involved and no leads were counted. ASML sold its roadmap to the only three buyers who could pay for it, and they underwrote it. The posture travels down-market: when the tier-one list is short, the budget belongs on the names.

**The healthy dashboard that missed the quarter.** Rising lead counts are easy to celebrate in a plainly power-law market. I've seen that fail exactly as the pattern predicts. A company ran a volume machine, watched the lead counts climb, and missed its number anyway, because the fifty accounts that would have made the quarter were never targeted by name. The dashboards had measured the wrong ocean the whole time. Only when the team threw out the lead-count goal and built a named-account list did the pipeline start to reflect the business that actually existed.
