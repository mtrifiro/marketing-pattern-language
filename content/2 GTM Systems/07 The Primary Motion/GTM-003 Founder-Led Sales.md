> *Before there's a playbook, the only person who can sell the product is the person who can speak like a co-founder, because that person is one.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Before a playbook exists, the founder is the only seller holding enough product judgment and narrative context to discover the motion a later team will repeat. The founder-led stage ends when that motion transfers to the team. It sits below [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] and above [[GTM-004 Repeatable Sales Motion]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't hire an experienced salesperson to own go-to-market before product-market fit, however much you dislike selling. There's no motion to hand off yet, so the hire arrives with nothing to run and stalls within a few calls.

The impulse is understandable. Selling feels like a specialized job, so as a technical or product founder you reason that the company should hire a specialist and get you back to building. The trap runs in a standard sequence. You hate sales, you decide someone can be brought in to figure it out for you, you get dazzled by a great résumé from an iconic large company, and you expect that person to build the go-to-market function from scratch. The hire arrives, listens to a few calls, and is sent off to sell. It doesn't work.

It doesn't work because the early sale and the enterprise sale are different jobs. A rep from a large company is trained for a settled motion: defined pricing, a deck, reference customers, a category the buyer already understands, and a playbook that tells them what to say at each stage. None of that exists yet. At this stage the only way to sell is to speak like the founder and carry the story as if you're a co-founder. A conventional rep can't do that, because the story isn't written down and the product's value isn't stable enough to script. Every early deal is partly a product conversation, partly a pricing experiment, and partly a discovery interview about what the buyer actually needs, and you're the only person with the authority to change the product, the price, and the pitch in the same meeting. Pete Kazanjy's *Founding Sales* (2020) is built on the same premise, that the founder runs the early sales cycles personally and the hiring waits until those cycles produce something teachable. Steve Blank made the case fifteen years earlier for a different reason. *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005) puts the founder in front of customers because the early calls are the company's only instrument for testing who the buyer is, what the problem is, and what they'll pay. Hand them to a rep and you've automated the one activity that was supposed to teach you something.

The early hire costs twice. Months and salary go to a rep who can't gain traction, and the learning those early sales conversations were supposed to produce never arrives. Deal by deal, you were going to find out who feels the pain sharply enough to pay, what language makes them nod, and which objections keep recurring. Handed to someone who can't yet tell the story, that channel closes, and you stay blind to your own market while the runway shortens.

The exit is where a great many companies stall, which makes it part of the same pattern. Once the founder has closed enough deals to believe the motion works, the obvious next step looks like adding salespeople and multiplying. Reps arrive from bigger companies, get a short ramp and a territory, and miss. The miss reads as a hiring problem and gets answered with more reps, when what actually happened is that nothing sellable changed hands: the thing that made those deals work is a narrative and a set of judgments nobody wrote down. "The founder can sell this" got mistaken for "this can be sold." The cost compounds in two directions. Scarce runway goes to reps who can't yet sell, and the company risks going deaf, because those early sales conversations were the channel through which the market told it what to build.

## Complications

- **The early narrative is tacit.** Before there's a deck and a playbook, the pitch lives in your head as judgment rather than as a document a rep can study.
- **Authority to change the deal sits with you.** Early sales are also product and pricing negotiations, and the person selling needs the standing to reshape the offer on the spot.
- **The sale is also research.** Each early conversation is discovery about who has the pain and what they'll pay, and that signal has to reach the person who can act on it.
- **Your time is the scarcest resource.** You also have to build the product and run the company, so selling every deal personally can't continue indefinitely.
- **Founders often dislike or distrust selling.** The temptation to escape the work by hiring it out is strongest exactly when you're least equipped to judge whether a sales hire is any good.
- **Outcomes are a poor early gauge of a rep.** Without product-market fit, wins and losses turn on the market as much as on skill, so quota attainment doesn't yet apply.
- **A tacit narrative transfers only by immersion.** Judgment can't be handed over on a slide, so a first rep absorbs it by sitting beside you deal after deal, and the transfer costs you the calendar you were hiring to get back.
- **You can hand off; you can't abdicate.** A founder who pulls out entirely at the transition breaks the motion that was working.
- **Discovery is rarer than closing and harder to teach.** The first hire's real job is asking questions sharp enough to learn the market and carry what they hear back to product.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Keep the founder in sales until the same kind of deal closes twice for the same reasons. Expand beyond the first one or two discovery-oriented reps only after one of them can close a deal without the founder in the room.

Run the phase in four moves:

1. **Sell to learn, and only then to close.** Accumulate enough closed deals that a pattern appears: the same kind of buyer, feeling the same pain, moved by the same story, blocked by the same objections. Dave Kellogg sets the bar for the exit: a founder has to have a certain amount of repeatability to show a salesperson first, because you can't hire someone to solve a sales problem you haven't already solved, even inefficiently. Manufacture that repeatability one conversation at a time; it's the raw material [[GTM-004 Repeatable Sales Motion]] will later codify.
2. **Judge the phase by activity rather than by outcomes.** Because pre-product-market-fit wins turn heavily on the market, goal your early effort on inputs you control, the number of calls or the number of meetings, rather than on bookings; concretely, your goal this month is to set 20 meetings. Stay in enough live conversations to find the pattern, and resist reading too much into any single win or loss.
3. **Hire the first rep for discovery, and teach by immersion.** The model is the Renaissance rep, an entrepreneurial first hire chosen because they ask really great, insightful questions. If a rep can master discovery, the mechanics and the pricing are fixable; a polished closer who can't run discovery has no way to learn the market or feed it back to the product, so the résumé that looks safest is frequently the worst fit. Then put that rep beside you in every deal instead of handing over a few recorded calls, and keep selling while they absorb the story deal by deal until they can carry it alone.
4. **Gate the size of the team on proven repeatability.** Hold to one rule: don't add salespeople until you really feel you've got something repeatable with your first one or two Renaissance reps. When a rep who isn't a founder can close your kind of deal using your story, the motion has survived outside your head; only then hire the next several and write the story down for [[GTM-004 Repeatable Sales Motion]]. Avoid the premature hire that skips the learning, and avoid the opposite failure, the founder who never lets go and becomes the ceiling on the company's growth.

Two tests bracket the stage. Before you write the job description, prove to yourself you can close the same kind of deal twice for the same reasons; if you can't, keep selling. Before you expand the team, sit out one deal, let the rep run it alone, and watch whether your story still closes.

## Implications

Running sales personally gives you your first real read on the market: which buyer feels the pain, what the pitch is, where the price lands, and which objections recur. That knowledge is what makes everything downstream possible, so bank it deliberately. A successful exit changes what you are, from a founder who can sell into a small team that can, with a live channel carrying market signal back to the product. Hand that proven, person-to-person motion to [[GTM-004 Repeatable Sales Motion]], where the winning motion becomes a documented playbook so your third, fifth, and tenth hires ramp without shadowing you. Under [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]], founder-led selling is the earliest form a sales-led or hybrid lead takes, and the handoff is how you keep that lead intact while removing yourself as its single point of failure.

Enforce the timing at both ends. Every hour you spend selling is an hour you don't spend building, and the stage can't last forever; leave too early, before the deals are genuinely repeatable, and you hand off a motion that doesn't exist yet, so the first rep is set up to fail. Leave too late and you cap the company's growth by remaining the only person who can close, your ceiling set by one calendar. Resist the standing pull of the impressive résumé at both moments, and hire for curiosity over credentials while the motion is still being written.

## Examples

**Stevie Case joining a founder-led company before Twilio.** Stevie Case, who went on to lead mid-market sales at Twilio and then to become Vanta's first chief revenue officer in 2022, described joining an earlier company as its 16th employee and first non-technical hire, when it was "very much founder-led sales with no support, success, or sales anything." She shadowed the co-founders "in every single customer interaction" to absorb the narrative, while helping to "define pricing, value proposition, and start defining who actually sees value." Both halves of the pattern show here: the founders were the sales motion, and the first commercial hire earned the narrative by immersion rather than getting handed a deck and a territory.

**Kevin Plank selling shirts out of his car (1996).** Under Armour started with no sales force, no deck, and no category. Plank had the network he'd built as a special-teams captain at the University of Maryland, and he drove up and down the East Coast selling the first compression T-shirt out of the trunk of his car; the company's own history names Georgia Tech as its first college customer that year, and the year closed with $17,000 in sales. Every team-by-team conversation doubled as product research on a garment no equipment manager had a budget line for. The S-1 the company filed in 2005 sets that stage against what followed: "Our business began in 1995 as an idea of our founder and Chief Executive Officer, Kevin A. Plank," and net revenues went "from $5.3 million in 2000 to $242.2 million for the 12 months ended June 30, 2005." A founder carrying a story nobody has written down is the whole mechanism here, and apparel needs it as much as software does.

**The iconic-résumé hire that stalls (composite).** A pre-product-market-fit founder reads a great résumé as readiness for an unsettled motion, hires a senior salesperson from a large, well-known company, and expects them to build go-to-market from nothing. The rep is fluent in a settled motion and lost without one; a few calls in, with no script to run and no authority to reshape the product or price, they stall. The problem is the timing: there was no repeatable motion to hand them, which is exactly what you were supposed to build first.

**The premature raft of quota reps (composite).** On the strength of your early wins, you hire several experienced reps at once, give them a short ramp, and watch them miss. The sequence is close to universal. The wins looked repeatable but had never left your head, so there was nothing to transfer.
