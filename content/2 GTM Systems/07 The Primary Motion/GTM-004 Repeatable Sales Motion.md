> *Don't build a sales team of artists. Turn your winning deals into a system a trained hire can run rather than one that needs a brilliant closer in the room.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A repeatable motion turns the tacit pattern inside winning deals into a named buyer, trigger, message, proof, and set of stage exits that a trained hire can run twice. It sits below [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] and [[GTM-003 Founder-Led Sales]] and above [[OPS-005 Funnel Stages, Yours]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't hire more reps against a motion that lives only in the founder's and first rep's heads. Each new person has to rediscover it from scratch, so results track whoever happens to be in the room and the pipeline can't be forecast or coached.

The symptom is a sales team of artists. A couple of gifted people close deals through instinct and hard-won context, everyone else underperforms, and no one can say why the good deals worked. The diagnosis is short: the company is hiring artists where it needs a factory. When selling is an art practiced by a few, three problems hit at once. Ramp is slow and unpredictable, because a new hire learns only by osmosis. Results are opaque, because no one can name which stage a deal is really in or why it stalled. And the motion is fragile, because it walks out the door when a star rep leaves.

Hiring more experienced reps deepens the problem. Without a documented motion, even good reps improvise their own, and the company ends up running several sales processes under one roof, each with its own idea of what a "qualified" deal is and what "ready to close" means. Forecasts turn to fiction, because "Stage 3" means something different to every rep. Coaching stops working, because there's no standard to coach against. Activity gets mistaken for a system, and a rep who is genuinely close is indistinguishable from one who is merely optimistic.

The deeper cost is that nobody can learn. A repeatable motion is also a measurement instrument: when everyone runs the same stages with the same exit criteria, a stalled deal shows where the motion breaks. Where everyone improvises, a loss teaches nothing that generalizes, and the same lessons get paid for again.

## Complications

- **Only a written system scales.** A motion carried in a few reps' heads can't be hired against or coached.
- **Forecasting needs shared definitions.** A pipeline is legible only when every rep means the same thing by each stage.
- **Codifying too early freezes the wrong thing.** You can document only a motion that already wins; a playbook written before [[GTM-003 Founder-Led Sales]] finds repeatability enshrines guesswork.
- **A playbook can calcify into a script.** Over-rigid process kills the discovery and judgment that made early deals work.
- **The market keeps moving.** The ICP, the objections, and the competition shift, and a playbook nobody revises starts teaching new hires to answer objections that stopped coming up.
- **Enablement is a real cost.** Turning wins into a teachable system takes time from your best sellers.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Extract the winning motion from the founder's and early reps' closed deals, codify it into a documented, teachable playbook with a named buyer, message, proof, and objective stage-exit criteria, then train and certify every new hire on that system before they sell, so the motion becomes a repeatable science rather than an individual art.

Mark Roberge ran HubSpot's sales and services organization from 2007 to 2013, taking it from one person to 450 and annualized revenue from zero to $100 million, then set the method down in *The Sales Acceleration Formula* (2015), where hiring, training, and management each run as a measured process rather than on instinct. Build the playbook from evidence rather than opinion.

Roberge's method assumes you already hold deals worth mining, and Steve Blank supplies the test for that in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005). It's stricter than most companies apply to themselves: three to five purchase orders at or near list price, from real customers, before the product ships, plus a written sales roadmap and the closer's judgment that an ordinary salesperson could repeat what just happened. His reason for insisting on money is that nothing cheaper discriminates. Giveaways establish that people will accept a free thing. What you need to know is whether a buyer will move budget, survive their own approval path, and defend the number to a colleague, and only a paid order at a price you'd quote publicly demonstrates all three. Count the orders before you write the playbook. A motion codified from deals that closed on discounts, favors, or free pilots documents something you can't run again.

Work the method in order:

1. **Mine your closed-won deals.** Forensically analyze the last ten to twenty won deals for what actually recurs: the specific pains, the moments the buyer's understanding shifted, the personas involved (champion, economic buyer, technical validator), and the objections that kept appearing.
2. **Compile a Sales Bible.** Turn those patterns into the single source of truth for how you sell: ICP and persona definitions, pain-to-value mapping, discovery questions for each persona, competitive comparisons, standardized demo flows by use case, and objection handling. Write down the motion you've already proven so it survives outside the people who discovered it.
3. **Make the stages objective.** Map each sales stage to an explicit exit criterion instead of a rep's optimism: reach "Stage 2: Technical Validation" only on a completed proof of concept or a signed validation document, never on a demo that went well. Objective gates are what make a pipeline forecastable and a stalled deal diagnostic. Put your qualification framework here (BANT, MEDDIC, or SPICED) as the shared definition of "qualified," which [[OPS-005 Funnel Stages, Yours]] installs in the operating funnel.
4. **Train, certify, then release.** Have every new hire learn the playbook and certify on it through a mock demo or call before they speak to a live prospect. Certification is what turns a document into a motion; it proves the hire can run the system rather than just read it.

Keep the playbook alive. Revise it as the ICP sharpens and the competition changes, and hold it as a game plan rather than a script so reps still run real discovery inside a shared structure. Push the supporting systems, the enablement content and the shared record of every deal, out to the companion patterns; own the discipline of repeatability here.

Test yourself: hand the playbook to your newest hire and watch them run a mock deal cold. If they can't, you've got a document rather than a motion; fix the gap before you let them sell.

## Implications

A documented, certified motion changes what a sales hire costs and what a pipeline means. Expect ramp to shorten, because a new rep executes a known system; expect forecasting to become real, because every deal sits in a stage with an objective definition; and expect coaching to work, because you finally have a standard to measure against. Reach this state before you ask a sales-led motion to scale past its first reps, and hold it so [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] can keep a sales lead as the team grows. Feed the stages, qualification, and enablement you name here into the operations layer of Part III, which instruments and refines them.

Enforce the discipline between structure and judgment. A playbook that hardens into a recited script strangles the discovery that made the early deals work, so keep the motion a game plan a rep adapts and maintain it as the market moves rather than freezing it at first draft. Mind the timing hazard on the front end too: codify before the motion is genuinely repeatable and you enshrine guesswork, which is why you wait on the repeatability that [[GTM-003 Founder-Led Sales]] produces.

You keep the winning approach in your best reps' heads and hope the next hire absorbs it. The company that writes it down instead analyzes the last ten to twenty won deals, extracts the recurring pains, personas, and objections, and compiles them into a single playbook covering ICP definitions, discovery questions, battle cards, demo flows, and objection handling. New hires certify on it through a mock demo before touching a live prospect. That turns the founder's and early reps' wins into a system the next hires can run, which is the difference between a team of artists and a repeatable motion.

"Stage 2" means whatever each rep feels after a demo that went well, and your forecast inherits the guessing. Under an evidence gate, a deal reaches "Stage 2: Technical Validation" only on a completed proof of concept or a signed technical-validation document, never on a demo that felt good. Stages defined this way make the pipeline forecastable and turn a stalled deal into a specific diagnostic, because everyone means the same thing by each stage.

## Examples

**Salesforce's process discipline.** Salesforce built its scale on a codified, repeatable sales motion: a defined methodology, a staged pipeline, and an enablement machine that let it hire and ramp large numbers of reps against a common playbook rather than relying on individual stars. The best-documented piece of it is the outbound prospecting process Aaron Ross built there and published as *Predictable Revenue* (2011), which he credits with adding $100 million in recurring revenue; the role-splitting that process prescribes belongs to [[OPS-016 Generalists Then Specialists]].

**NCR's Primer, June 1887.** John Patterson could have let a hundred cash-register salesmen each invent their own pitch. Instead he took the handbook his brother-in-law J.H. Crane had written about how he sold, issued it to the whole force as the NCR Primer, required the script memorized, and broke the call into four steps: approach, proposition, demonstration, close. NCR sold just under 2,000 registers that year, close to double the year before. The Primer then absorbed a Book of Arguments answering the objections reps kept hearing, and the two merged into the NCR Sales Manual by 1894; Patterson also opened a sales school on the NCR campus in Dayton, where new sellers learned the manual before they went out. Nothing in that method needs software: a written motion, an objection library, and certification before a rep meets a buyer.
