> *You can only harvest a field you planted; in a new market there's nothing yet to reap.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Demand creation plants a field by teaching the problem, and capture systems can harvest it once buyers know what to seek. It sits below [[GTM-009 Awareness Cascade]], [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]], and [[MS-002 Market Type Sets the Playbook]] and above [[GTM-011 Content as Lead Filter]], [[GTM-015 Dark Funnel Illumination]], [[OPS-005 Funnel Stages, Yours]], and [[CR-023 Call to Conversation, Not Conversion]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't run the tactics that harvest existing demand when you're entering a market you're trying to build. In a genuinely new category few people are searching yet, so there's no intent pool to capture and you'll harvest almost nothing.

The mistake wears the costume of competence. Search ads go up, the category keywords get bought, the review-site profiles get filed, the landing pages get tuned, all the moves that work in a settled market. Then the pipeline stays thin, and the team blames execution: the ad copy, the bid strategy, the page conversion rate. The cause sits upstream. Capture tactics collect people who are already looking, and in a new category almost no one is looking yet, because the buyer doesn't yet have words for the problem the product solves. Chris Walker made this the center of Refine Labs' argument through the early 2020s: demand generation's real work is creating demand, and collecting the demand that already exists is the smaller, later job.

Your own metrics mislead you here. Search volume for a category that doesn't exist is close to zero, so a capture budget spent against it returns a handful of expensive, low-intent clicks and a conclusion that the market is small. What the empty funnel actually measures is demand nobody has created yet.

The available fixes all live inside the capture frame. Bidding higher, adding keywords, hiring an SEO agency, buying more review-site placement all assume the searchers exist and the job is to reach them first, and none of them generates a single new buyer who wasn't already looking. The buyer journey runs awareness, then consideration, then decision, and capture tactics only meet buyers who've already crossed into consideration on their own. In a new market almost no one has crossed, and the awareness stage where they would cross is the one the whole apparatus skips.

## Complications

- **Capture is cheaper and faster to measure, so budget drifts toward it.** Search ads and SEO produce click-through numbers and cost-per-lead figures you can report this quarter, while creation's payoff shows up months later and in channels a dashboard can't see.
- **A new category has little existing intent to harvest.** When the buyer can't yet name the problem, search volume is near zero and review sites have no category page.
- **Buyers do most of their research before they'll talk to you.** Gartner's B2B buying research puts only about 17% of a buyer's purchase time in meetings with potential suppliers, so the want forms in the buyer's own reading and peer conversations.
- **Deals in a new category are long and multi-touch.** A buyer who starts with no awareness needs dozens of educational contacts before capture is even possible.
- **Creation and capture reward opposite content.** Early-stage buyers want problem-focused education; late-stage buyers want solution and decision content, and content built for one stage does nothing for a buyer standing in the other.
- **Capture works beautifully once the category exists, which tempts imitation too early.** Watching an incumbent in a mature category win on SEO and search ads makes those tactics look like the whole game.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Fund buyer education before demand capture in a new market. Approve capture spend only after buyers actively search for the problem or category, and rerun that check each quarter.

Run the two jobs in order, and hold the order one way:

1. **Create first.** Generate awareness and want where none existed: teach the market that a problem is worth naming, that it's costing them, that a new kind of answer exists. This is what the [[GTM-009 Awareness Cascade]] spreads, and it's what fills the awareness stage of the buyer's journey with people who weren't there before.
2. **Match content to the journey stage.** Early, give the buyer problem-focused education that names the problem and frames why it matters, delivered without a sales ask; this is where created demand is born and where content does double duty as a qualifier, developed in [[GTM-011 Content as Lead Filter]]. Later, as the buyer crosses into consideration and decision, shift to solution comparisons and decision support.
3. **Capture second, sized to the intent creation produced.** Turn on search ads, SEO, and review-site presence to meet the buyer who has already decided to look. Capture has something to collect only after creation has moved buyers into that searching posture.
4. **Track readiness in the nurture.** Meet each buyer at the stage they're actually in; pushing a decision-stage ask onto an awareness-stage lead breaks the sequence and repels the buyer. The logic that decides what to send a buyer who isn't ready to declare themselves, and when, belongs to [[OPS-008 Nurture Architecture]].

Fund creation as the leading investment and treat capture as the trailing one, sized to the intent creation has actually produced. As the category takes hold, search volume appears, review sites open a category page, and your harvest tactics finally have a field to reap; at that point shift the balance, because creation built the pool capture draws from. Never reverse the order inside a category you're creating. Capture that runs ahead of creation harvests nothing, and creation without eventual capture leaves the demand it stirred up ungathered.

Name the boundary as you go, because this sequence governs new categories and says nothing about channels. Where the category already exists and buyers are already searching, capture leads and creation becomes the trailing investment that widens the pool. Red Bull ran the sequence in 1987 with no search box anywhere in it, which tells you the rule turns on whether the want exists rather than on which medium carries it.

Which way round the order runs is decided by the market type you chose in [[MS-002 Market Type Sets the Playbook]], which also sets the objective for your first planning cycle and owns the reasoning behind it. Entering an existing market, the demand pool is already there and capture leads. Entering a new market, there's nothing to capture until buyers appear, so that early target stops being share at all. Resegmenting an existing market carries both jobs at once, which is what makes it the hardest of the three to budget for.

Size the creation budget against the buyers who can act inside the cycle you're funding rather than against the category's eventual size. Blank's reason for insisting on that is sharper than a warning about waste: money spent reaching people who can't yet buy doesn't wait in an account until they can, and it's missing from the year they finally arrive.

Before you approve a capture budget, ask whether anyone is searching for what you sell yet. If the honest answer is no, move the money to education and re-run the check next quarter.

## Implications

Get the sequence right and the rest of the demand engine opens up. Once creation is producing want the market can't yet see in a funnel, instrument for it: much of the created demand shows up in private channels, peer conversations, and anonymous research a standard dashboard misses, so turn to [[GTM-015 Dark Funnel Illumination]] to see it and to reach the buyers it finds who aren't ready to buy. Count them through stages you defined yourself, which is [[OPS-005 Funnel Stages, Yours]]. Build the content that does the creating to sort as it teaches, so the education itself separates serious buyers from tire-kickers, the job of [[GTM-011 Content as Lead Filter]]. Each of these assumes the creation-first sequence you set here.

Enforce the discipline against the spreadsheet. Capture will always look more attractive because it's legible and near-term while creation is slow and hard to attribute; that pull tempts you to underfund creation and over-index on harvest, which quietly starves the pool capture depends on. Hold the timing call too: turn on capture too early and it wastes money against an empty field, turn it on too late and demand creation stirred up goes to a competitor who was ready to harvest it. Re-read when the category has enough intent to reward capture, and base that reading on what the created demand is actually doing, which loops back to [[GTM-015 Dark Funnel Illumination]].

## Examples

**HubSpot and "inbound marketing," 2006 onward.** HubSpot's founders Brian Halligan and Dharmesh Shah put the term in a book title, *Inbound Marketing: Get Found Using Google, Social Media and Blogs*, and the company built a certification academy and an annual conference around teaching it. The S-1 HubSpot filed on 25 August 2014 credits the result to the education itself: brand recognition "comes from our thought leadership, including our blog, which attracts approximately 1.5 million visits each month," and the company reported 11,624 customers in more than 70 countries as of 30 June 2014. The same filing lists the position as something it could lose, in a risk factor that reads "If we fail to maintain our inbound marketing thought leadership position, our business may suffer." By the time buyers were typing "inbound marketing software" into a search box, HubSpot had built the category that made the search exist, and the capture harvested a pool it had planted.

**Salesforce educating the market on "no software," 1999 into the 2000s.** Salesforce was incorporated in February 1999 and began offering its on-demand CRM service in February 2000, and it spent the years after that teaching buyers that client-server software was the problem and a browser-delivered alternative was the answer, the campaign with the crossed-out disk. The company took that education seriously enough to register it: the S-1 amendment it filed in June 2004 lists the "No Software" logo and "The End of Software" among its registered trademarks. The same filing shows the arithmetic of creation-first. Paying subscribers rose from roughly 30,000 on 1 February 2001 to over 147,000 by 30 April 2004, and marketing and sales ran 66% of total revenues in fiscal 2003 and 57% in fiscal 2004, far above what a capture-led company spends. Only once the market accepted the category did search demand for hosted CRM exist to harvest, and Salesforce was standing where it landed.

**Spending a year early (Steve Blank's account of PhotosToYou, 1999).** In *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*, Blank describes an online digital-photo-printing company whose reachable market depended on digital-camera adoption. Camera sales were growing by more than 50 percent a year, but the 1999 installed base was still small. The company funded a national advertising campaign anyway. Blank warned that the following year would bring eight times as many available customers, after the company had spent the money needed to reach them. PhotosToYou exhausted its venture funding in its first two years. It then raised at one-tenth of its prior valuation and replaced its executive staff, including the three founders. The reachable buyer population set that year's useful creation budget. Market type controls the longer clock; [[MS-002 Market Type Sets the Playbook]] owns that distinction.

**Red Bull, creating want with no search box to capture (1987 onward).** Facing zero existing intent, Red Bull manufactured the awareness stage by hand, free cases carried into student parties, cars with a giant can bolted to the roof, sponsorship of the extreme-sport athletes its buyer already watched. The education ran for years before "energy drink" existed as a shelf label or a query. Once the words existed, Red Bull owned them, and every capture tactic that followed, branded coolers by the register included, harvested a field the company had planted itself.
