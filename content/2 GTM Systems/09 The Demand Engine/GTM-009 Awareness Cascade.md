> *Don't buy attention with a megaphone. Earn it from a few trusted insiders and let it spread outward, one credible relationship at a time.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Awareness spreads from a few trusted insiders through their reference groups. Earned credibility becomes a cascade that reaches the broader market. It sits below [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]] and [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] and above [[GTM-008 Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture]], [[GTM-010 Technical Content Marketing]], [[GTM-012 Targeted Reputation Building]], [[GTM-013 Authority Amplification]], and [[GTM-014 Brand as Moat]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't try to buy attention by broadcasting to the whole market at once. A skeptical market absorbs the spend and returns nothing, because a billboard never reaches the people whose judgment that market follows.

The instinct is to treat awareness as a volume problem: spend more, reach more, repeat the message until it sticks. In a technical or expert market that instinct fails, because the audience has an ad-blocker in its head. A developer, a scientist, a security lead discounts anything that looks like marketing and gives its trust to people instead of logos. Your product is invisible because your people are. With nobody credible associated with the company, every go-to-market effort feels like pushing a boulder uphill, and paid reach makes the boulder heavier.

The spend also lands on the wrong stage of the journey. A buyer moves from awareness through consideration to decision, and the early stages run on problem-focused learning and peer validation rather than on a sales pitch. Most of that early learning happens where a broadcast can't reach: a private Slack group, a conversation with a respected colleague, a blog the buyer has read for months. Money poured into the top of a funnel the buyer traverses privately buys impressions and no trust.

Awareness has a structure and runs on relationships. Attention travels a path: it starts with the people whose judgment the market already respects and moves outward through the groups that look to them. The same message sprayed at everyone bypasses the path and collects none of the compounding the path provides. The interpretation the market will assign is set upstream by [[MS-004 Narrative Precedes Demand]], and this pattern's concern is how that interpretation spreads along the path attention actually travels.

## Complications

- **Buyers trust people over brands.** In a skeptical market, credibility attaches to individuals whose expertise the audience already recognizes, and it transfers to a company only through them.
- **The halo effect carries trust across a link.** When a prospect trusts an expert, that trust extends to the brand and product the expert is associated with, and it reaches only as far as that expert's own standing does.
- **Attention is relational and staged rather than broadcast.** It propagates through reference groups: an insider convinces peers, peers convince their communities, and the message moves in private, largely untrackable channels before it surfaces as an inbound request.
- **The early journey is won by teaching.** Awareness and consideration turn on problem-focused content and peer validation, and a hard sell at that stage repels the buyer.
- **Insider attention is scarce and slow.** A handful of credible voices can start the cascade, but they earn their trust over time, so you can't rush the motion with money the way you can a broadcast.
- **Concentrating on people creates key-person risk.** When awareness is anchored to one star expert, their departure takes the audience with them.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Build awareness through credible insiders and let trust spread through their reference groups. The cascade belongs to the company only when more than one credible person can carry it.

Run the cascade in order:

1. **Start where the trust already lives.** Put your own credible people, founders, engineers, respected practitioners, into the conversation the market is already having, teaching rather than pitching, so the halo effect can carry their credibility to the company.
2. **Let the insider's content seed the peer channels.** Turn that teaching into the material peers share in private channels, and use the insider's standing as your legitimate reason to participate in communities.
3. **Extend outward through external experts.** Have your internal authorities build peer relationships with respected outside voices who carry the message to audiences you can't reach directly.

A founder who writes a deep technical post every week, with the marketing team promoting the post rather than the product, becomes a voice the field follows; inbound requests start arriving from people who say they've trusted the approach for months. Each hop is a distinct pattern, [[GTM-013 Authority Amplification]] for the internal experts, [[GTM-012 Targeted Reputation Building]] for standing with named buyers, [[GTM-010 Technical Content Marketing]] for the teaching itself; insist they run in the right order, insiders first.

Match the effort to the stage of the journey. Early on, supply the problem-focused learning a buyer researching a problem needs, and let peer validation, references, communities, respected voices, do the convincing a pitch can't. That early attention is demand creation, whose deep treatment belongs to [[GTM-008 Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture]]; here, remember that creation happens through the cascade and reach alone won't produce it. As the cascade widens and the market recognizes your name on its own, let the accumulated reputation harden into a durable advantage, developed in [[GTM-014 Brand as Moat]].

Protect the cascade from its own fragility. Because it runs on credible people, anchor the authority in a team and a company identity rather than a single individual, so losing one expert doesn't collapse the awareness you've built. You own the cascade as an asset only when more than one person carries it.

## Implications

Build awareness as a cascade and you open the rest of the demand engine. Hand off to [[GTM-008 Demand Creation vs. Demand Capture]] to separate the attention the cascade creates from the attention it merely harvests; run it through the channels [[GTM-010 Technical Content Marketing]] and [[GTM-012 Targeted Reputation Building]] supply; develop the internal-expert engine at its center with [[GTM-013 Authority Amplification]]; and let [[GTM-014 Brand as Moat]] name what the cascade becomes once it's wide and self-sustaining. Feed the awareness produced here into the first-contact patterns of the motion, since a practitioner who already trusts the company is far more likely to try the product alone.

Hold the discipline against the cascade's slowness. It compounds, but it won't respond to money the way broadcast does, and much of its work happens in private channels a dashboard can't see, which will tempt you to abandon it for the legible metrics of paid reach; don't. Manage the standing key-person risk too: the more the cascade leans on one charismatic expert, the more exposed it is, so keep spreading authority across a team as people come and go. Instrument the hidden middle of the cascade through [[GTM-015 Dark Funnel Illumination]].

External influencer relationships are the outward extension of internal authority. Your own experts build genuine, peer-to-peer relationships with respected outside voices, who then carry the message to audiences you couldn't reach directly. The trust still moves person to person; extend the cascade one credible relationship at a time.

Promoting the product runs into the market's ad-blocker, which discards it. A founder publishes a substantial technical post every week instead, with the marketing team amplifying the writing rather than the product. Over months the founder becomes a go-to voice in the field, and demo requests begin to carry a telling phrase: the prospect has followed the founder's work for six months and already trusts the approach. Attention started with one credible insider and cascaded outward into inbound demand, with no broadcast anywhere in the sequence.

## Examples

**Community adoption before a sales conversation.** In developer-tool markets the cascade shows in the order of events, a respected engineer tries a tool and vouches for it, peers in their community pick it up, and the tool is widely adopted inside teams long before a salesperson is involved. HashiCorp's early growth ran this way, with documentation and a community of practitioners carrying the tools outward. Its November 2021 S-1 reported the open-source products downloaded approximately 100 million times in the fiscal year ended 31 January 2021, and over 219,000 stars on its GitHub repositories, against $211.9 million of revenue for that year. The practitioners who carried the awareness outnumbered the customers who paid for it by orders of magnitude.

**Hybrid seed corn in two Iowa towns, 1943.** Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross interviewed corn growers in Jefferson and Grand Junction, Iowa, and pulled two questions apart: where a farmer first heard of hybrid seed, and what actually decided him. Salesmen were the first source for 49% of farmers and the most influential source for 32%. Neighbors were the first source for only 14.6% and the most influential for 45.5%. Adoption took about five years from first hearing to first planting, and most of it happened between 1933 and 1939. The seed company could buy the first hearing; the neighbors supplied the decision. The cascade runs the same way in a farming county, and the study that measured it predates the software industry by half a century.
