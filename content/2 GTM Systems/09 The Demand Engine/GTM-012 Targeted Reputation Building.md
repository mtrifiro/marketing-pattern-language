> *Don't try to be famous; be known and respected by the thousand people who can actually buy.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A useful reputation is narrow enough to be known by name: one trusted voice on one problem for the small set of people who can buy. It sits below [[GTM-009 Awareness Cascade]] and [[GTM-011 Content as Lead Filter]] and above [[GTM-013 Authority Amplification]] and [[GTM-016 Account-Based Everything]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't buy broad awareness measured in impressions and followers. The reputation it builds is wide, shallow, and disconnected from anyone who can sign a purchase order.

The mandate arrives with the money. Investors and boards want a brand that looks big, and the fastest way to look big is to buy reach: conference banners, sponsored content, follower counts, a chart of impressions that climbs every quarter. The awareness a broadcast buys is real in the sense that a dashboard can count it. It's also thin. A million people who half-recognize your logo produce nothing if none of them owns the problem you solve.

The gap opens between the metrics and the pipeline. Impressions rise, follower counts rise, and the sales team still can't get a meeting with the accounts that matter, because the people who can actually buy were never the ones being reached. Broad brand spend aims at a general audience that mostly can't purchase, and it evaporates the moment the campaign stops. Nothing compounds, because the reputation was never attached to a specific buyer with a specific need.

The obvious response, spending more to reach further, widens the shallow reputation instead of deepening a narrow one. In a market a company is trying to define, the buyers are skeptical and few, and they grant trust to a source that has demonstrably understood their problem while withholding it from a name they've only seen on a banner. Volume can't manufacture that trust, and broad reach aims everywhere except at the narrow field where a reputation would convert.

## Complications

- **The board wants a brand that looks big.** Venture funding carries a mandate to be everywhere, and impressions and follower counts are the metrics that satisfy it, so the pressure runs toward reach the whole time.
- **Buyers are few and skeptical.** The people who can actually purchase your product number in the hundreds or low thousands, and they trust a source that has earned standing on their exact problem more than a name they recognize from advertising.
- **Vanity metrics measure the wrong audience.** Impressions and followers count a general population, most of whom can't buy, so a rising chart can climb while the pipeline stays flat.
- **Depth and reach pull against each other.** Becoming the most trusted voice for one ICP on one problem takes concentrated, sustained work, and every dollar and hour spent broadening the audience is one not spent deepening the standing with the buyers who count.
- **Reputation with named buyers compounds; broad spend decays.** Standing earned with a specific buyer persists and converts into pipeline over time, while broad awareness fades the moment the campaign budget stops.
- **Teaching earns trust that selling repels.** The audience grants authority to a source that educates them on their problem and reacts against one that pitches.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Aim reputation at the specific and small set of people who can actually buy, and become the most trusted and authoritative voice for that ICP on the one problem you solve, so standing converts into pipeline instead of dissipating into vanity metrics.

Run the reputation program in this order:

1. **Name the audience before you spend a dollar on it.** The target is roughly the thousand people who can actually buy your product, and everything about the program serves that number. Write the list.
2. **Measure trust rather than reach.** Stop counting impressions and followers; instead measure whether the people on that list know you, trust you, and think of you first when the problem comes up.
3. **Teach instead of sell.** Publish authoritative content that treats the problem in depth, and highlight the success of enterprise users who have solved it with you, so the audience learns from you before they ever hear a pitch. A named buyer who has read your analysis for months arrives at the sales conversation already convinced, which is the conversion broad spend never produces.
4. **Concentrate the field until you own it.** Decline the broad topics where you'd be one voice among thousands, and go deep on the narrow one where you can be the definitive source. That focus is what makes the reputation convert: a buyer facing your exact problem finds one source that has clearly understood it, and the standing translates into a pipeline of people who came because they already trust the approach.

Test the program by asking whether a buyer on your named list would recommend you unprompted; if they wouldn't, go deeper on their problem before you widen the audience.

Route the rest through two engines this pattern doesn't own. Develop the internal experts whose credibility carries the company's standing through [[GTM-013 Authority Amplification]]. When the reputation program hardens into a full go-to-market motion built around named accounts, hand it to [[GTM-016 Account-Based Everything]]. This pattern's job is to set the aim: standing with the buyers who count, earned by teaching, measured in trust.

## Implications

Build reputation this way and you turn standing into a supply of qualified pipeline. The audience you accumulate is small by design and dense with buyers, so the awareness feeds the rest of the demand engine at a far higher conversion rate than broad reach ever does. Once the reputation is deep enough to attract the right people, put [[GTM-011 Content as Lead Filter]] to work sorting them through the published material, and let [[GTM-009 Awareness Cascade]] spread the standing outward through the reference groups those buyers belong to. Use the reputation as the legitimate reason to build the internal-expert engine of [[GTM-013 Authority Amplification]] and, further on, to rebuild the whole motion around named accounts with [[GTM-016 Account-Based Everything]].

Enforce the discipline against the pull back toward broad spend. A narrow reputation is slow to build and easy to abandon under pressure for legible growth: the impressions and followers a broad campaign produces are visible next week, while the standing with a thousand named buyers takes months of teaching and shows up as pipeline rather than as a chart the board recognizes. That gap tempts teams back toward the broad spend this pattern rejects, especially when a new round of funding renews the mandate to look big. Hold the line by measuring the reputation on whether the right people trust you, and defend that measure against the vanity metrics that are easier to grow and worth less.

## Examples

**Honeycomb and narrow observability authority (2016 onward).** Honeycomb, founded in 2016 by Christine Yen and Charity Majors, became the recognized authority on one narrow problem, debugging production systems you can't fully predict, inside one practitioner community, publishing deep technical writing that the engineers who owned that problem read and shared. The audience stayed small and dense with buyers, and the reputation converted into inbound from teams that already trusted the analysis. The standing came in artifacts you can date: Majors, Liz Fong-Jones and George Miranda published *Observability Engineering* with O'Reilly on 14 June 2022, a book about the practice rather than about the product, aimed at the engineers who owned the problem. Educate the specific ICP on the specific problem and the standing produces pipeline.

**Vapor IO and the edge-infrastructure buyers (2017–2019).** I could have bought broad awareness for a category most people had never heard of. Instead, at Vapor IO we aimed the reputation program at a named set of a few hundred people who could actually move edge-computing deals, specific carrier and operator decision-makers, a handful of analysts, and the standards bodies defining the category. We published a point of view on the edge problem and got known and respected by that list by name. When the sales conversations came, the buyers already understood our framing, because we'd built the reputation with them specifically rather than sprayed it at a general market.

**Steinway's roster of pianists (1853 onward).** Steinway & Sons, a piano maker since 1853, keeps a Steinway Artist roster, and the company's own description of it is this pattern in one sentence: "Steinway Artists all own Steinway pianos and, unlike virtually any commercial endorsement, they are not paid to play the Steinway." A 2010 company release put the roster at over 1,500 pianists, and Steinway's European site now says more than 2,000. Qualification, in the company's words, "reflects the artist's dedication to and confidence in the Steinway piano." None of those people is a volume market, and every conservatory, concert hall, and parent buying a first upright takes its cue from them. The list is short enough to write down, which is the condition the thousand-buyer target quietly depends on.

**The Super Bowl spend that bought awareness and no pipeline (composite).** A venture-funded company runs an expensive broad-brand campaign, Super Bowl advertising included, that produces a spike in impressions and name recognition and almost no qualified pipeline, because the audience reached is general and the people who can actually buy are a tiny fraction of it. The awareness evaporates when the spend stops. A narrow reputation with named buyers converts and compounds where broad reach dissipates.
