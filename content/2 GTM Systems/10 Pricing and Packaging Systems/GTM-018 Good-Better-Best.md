> *Give the buyer three doors instead of a menu; each door names who it's for.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Good-Better-Best turns a menu into three or four legible doors. Each door names who it is for and makes the next edition a credible place to grow. It sits below [[MS-018 Price Creates the Buyer]] and above [[GTM-019 Gating Line]] and [[GTM-020 Bundle Logic]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Never leave a buyer facing one flat price or a dozen editions. With no tiers they can't tell which version is meant for them; with too many they freeze, and you lose the small buyer to confusion and the large one to a low ceiling.

A single flat price can't serve every segment. A price set low enough for a two-person team collects two-person money from an enterprise that depends on the product. A price set high enough for the enterprise never gets the two-person team to sign up. A one-price offer optimizes for a customer in the middle who may not exist, and it leaves the willingness-to-pay of everyone above that midpoint uncollected. A flat price leaves money on the table, and the phrase is literal: the value is there, the buyer would pay for it, and your offer has no line item to charge it against.

The opposite error costs just as much. A lineup with seven or eight editions, each differing by a scatter of features, asks the buyer to run a comparison they aren't equipped to run. They don't know which capabilities they'll need in a year, so every difference between tiers reads as a risk of choosing wrong. Analysis paralysis sets in, and a paralyzed buyer stalls or leaves. Your own team feels it too: quoting slows, discounts get invented to rescue confused deals, and the sales motion loses the legibility that made self-service possible.

A small set of well-defined editions works, simple enough to read at a glance and wide enough that more than one segment finds itself. The name for the structure is Rafi Mohammed's, from "The Good-Better-Best Approach to Pricing" in *Harvard Business Review* (September–October 2018), and his account of the three jobs is the part to keep: a stripped-down Good edition brings in customers who weren't buying at all, the existing product in the middle keeps the customers you already have, and a feature-heavy Best edition collects more from the buyers who want more. Landing on three tiers that each do a different job is hard. Companies copy a competitor's tier names, split their features arbitrarily across them, and end up with three doors that all lead to roughly the same room. The buyer can't place themselves in any of the three.

## Complications

- **Simplicity and coverage pull against each other.** Too few tiers can't capture distinct segments and their budgets; too many make the buyer freeze.
- **Each tier has to earn its step.** OpenView Partners advises pricing by sophistication and willingness-to-pay, so every move up the ladder must add value the next segment actually wants. A tier that adds only trivial features is a step nobody takes.
- **Build the lineup as a ladder the buyer can climb.** Good-Better-Best works when a customer starts on the entry edition and grows into the next, which turns packaging into an expansion engine. A lineup of unrelated editions with no upward path collects a first sale and then goes quiet.
- **Buyers self-identify only when the tiers name a segment.** "I'm a small team, so Standard" and "we're an enterprise, so Enterprise" happen only when each edition carries an obvious job and audience. A split by feature-count alone leaves the buyer unable to place themselves.
- **The advertised range signals who's welcome.** Dave Kellogg's Tiffany-store point, in "Price Qualification: The Tiffany Way" (Kellblog, 10 February 2019), is that a Tiffany showroom keeps one side at $200 to $2,000 and another at $10,000 and up, and the staff quote the two ranges rather than asking what you can afford; customers place themselves. A published span from fifty dollars a month to five thousand and up does the same job, and the range is a proxy for deal size. A narrow range quietly turns one end of the market away.
- **A rival's tiers reveal their read of the market.** Their tier structure reveals how they read the market, and a lineup copied from theirs buries your own value.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Package the offer as three or four named editions, give each one a clear job and target segment, size the steps so every upgrade adds value the next segment will pay for, and build a natural trigger that prompts the move from one edition to the next.

Start with the jobs each edition does before you split any features. A common four-part lineup assigns each edition a distinct role:

1. **Free.** Run it for lead generation and market education rather than revenue.
2. **Basic or Standard.** Make it the entry door for the small buyer.
3. **Pro.** Build it as the commercial workhorse for the mid-market, and expect it to carry most of the revenue.
4. **Enterprise.** Keep it high-touch and high-end, sold rather than self-served.

Once each edition has a job and an audience, let the feature split follow from the jobs instead of guessing it. Give the editions distinctive names that imply rising value, so the lineup reads as a ladder the moment a buyer sees it. Hold the count to three or four. Past that, sales execution gets harder and buyers get confused, and the extra tiers stop earning their complexity.

Send the two decisions inside the lineup to the smaller patterns, and here only frame where they act. Which capabilities to hold back for the higher editions, including the free-to-paid boundary that decides when a user has to pay, belongs to [[GTM-019 Gating Line]]; a tier structure is only as good as the line drawn between its steps. Grouping modules so a package reaches a budget a single edition would miss belongs to [[GTM-020 Bundle Logic]]. From Good-Better-Best, set the number of doors, the job behind each one, and the ladder that connects them.

Design the trigger last. Make the upgrade feel like the customer's own idea, prompted by a limit they hit while succeeding: a Basic account that crosses a thousand records and finds Pro waiting on the far side of that wall. The trigger turns three static editions into an expansion path, and it decides whether a lineup captures a first sale or keeps earning as the account grows. Set the trigger where the customer's value is already obvious, so the move up reads as buying more of something that's working. Then check it against one question and act on the answer: does a genuinely successful customer hit this wall while winning? If not, move it.

## Implications

With a three- or four-tier lineup in place, your offer becomes legible to buyers and to the sales team at once. A small buyer self-selects into the entry edition without a conversation, a mid-market buyer lands on the workhorse tier, and the enterprise buyer sees a door that signals the high-touch treatment it expects. Let the advertised range do its quiet work, telling each end of the market it belongs. Treat expansion as a property of the packaging rather than a separate motion: the ladder and its triggers carry accounts upward, which is the mechanism [[GTM-029 Land and Expand]] depends on.

Enforce one discipline against a standing tension: the lineup calcifies while the market moves. Tiers set for last year's segments trap this year's value in the wrong edition, and re-tiering reopens every renewal because customers organized their budgets around the plan they chose. Most companies reopen it anyway. In OpenView's 2023 SaaS Benchmarks report, drawn from more than 700 companies, half had changed both pricing and packaging that year, a quarter had changed pricing alone, and 22 percent had changed neither. Watch the trigger too. Place the upgrade wall past the point where value is obvious and the ladder never pulls anyone up; place it too early and the wall feels like a shakedown and stalls adoption. Where those lines land belongs downstream to [[GTM-019 Gating Line]], so re-examine the lineup on a schedule against whether each edition's job still matches the segment you drew it for.

## Examples

**Slack's Free, Pro, Business+, and Enterprise Grid (structure as of 2024).** Free educates the market and seeds teams, Pro is the small-team workhorse, Business+ adds the compliance and administration a growing company needs, and Enterprise Grid handles multi-workspace organizations through a sales motion. The lineup reads as a ladder, and the ninety-day message-history limit on Free, announced on 18 July 2022 and effective that September in place of a 10,000-message cap, is the trigger that pushes an active team toward Pro once the archive it has built starts disappearing.

**Atlassian's Free, Standard, Premium, and Enterprise path (Jira and Confluence, as of 2023).** Atlassian moved customers off legacy Server licenses onto four tiers sized by user count and administrative need. Free and Standard carry the small team, Premium adds the analytics and controls the mid-market wants, and Enterprise serves large organizations. The trigger sits on user-count thresholds, so each account climbs as it grows past the seat and storage limits of its current edition.

**Salesforce's edition ladder (Sales Cloud prices as listed in mid-2026).** Salesforce's published Sales Cloud lineup climbs from Starter Suite at $25 per user a month through Pro Suite at $100 and Enterprise at $175 to Unlimited at $350, with an Agentforce tier above that at $550. Each step holds more advanced capability for customers with more advanced needs, which puts the OpenView principle of pricing by sophistication into practice: the small buyer pays for the entry edition and the large enterprise pays several times more for the capability its scale requires. The breadth also does the Kellogg signaling work, telling both ends of the market there's a side of the store for them.

**Allstate's Your Choice Auto put four named tiers on a car insurance policy (2005 to 2011 in California).** Allstate rolled the program out in 2005, reaching Texas on 23 May, and added three optional packages around its standard policy: the Allstate Value Plan, Gold Protection, and Platinum Protection, with accident forgiveness and deductible rewards reserved for the upper two. Tiering predates software by decades, and in insurance it runs under a regulator, which is the part software companies don't inherit. Allstate began selling the program in California in 2008, and Consumer Watchdog challenged it there as deceptive and overpriced under the state's good-driver discount law. Rather than take it to a hearing at the Department of Insurance, Allstate stopped California sales on 10 January 2011 and phased out the 150,000 or so policies it held in the state by that November. The program charged up to 15 percent above the normal premium in exchange for a promise that a future violation wouldn't raise the rate, and a published step of that size in a rate-regulated market invites the argument that the tier is discrimination wearing the clothes of choice. Where prices are filed and approved, design the ladder with the regulator in the room.
