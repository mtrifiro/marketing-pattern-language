> *Bundle a group the buyer already treats as one thing to win one budget; set the intense minority's capability off to the side as an add-on so it never taxes everyone else.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A useful bundle joins modules the buyer already treats as one job, while an add-on isolates an intense minority's need so broader adoption and power-user depth draw on different parts of the buyer budget. It sits below [[GTM-018 Good-Better-Best]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Never sell products one at a time into an account with the budget and the need for three; you win one small line item and leave the rest uncollected. Over-correcting is just as costly, since a tangle of packages stalls the buyer.

Past your first product you end up with a portfolio: a core tool plus adjacent modules, integrations, or capabilities that different buyers value in different combinations. Sold individually, each one fights its own tiny sales cycle, and a buyer who would happily have spent a larger sum on a coherent group never sees the group offered. The budget existed. You presented one item at a time and reached a fraction of it.

The obvious fix is to package things together, and the obvious way to get it wrong is to over-package. Teams bolt products into bundles to hit revenue targets, and each new combination adds a row to the price page. A buyer who arrives to solve one problem then faces a grid of overlapping options and can't tell which one is meant for them. Choice this heavy reads as risk, so the deal slows or dies. Worse, once everything is available in some cheaper combination, the individual list prices stop meaning anything, and the discipline of your published pricing erodes from underneath.

The same problem has a quieter version. A small group of power users needs one intense capability the mainstream buyer will never touch: advanced security, a high API ceiling, premium support. A company that folds that capability into a standard tier either raises the price on everyone for a feature most of them ignore, or gives it away and leaves the money on the table with the exact users who would gladly pay for it. Either move punishes the majority to serve the minority, or the reverse. Neither trade is worth making.

## Complications

- **Reach versus clarity.** A bundle reaches budgets and buyer needs a single package misses, which argues for more combinations. Every combination you add makes the price page harder to read, which argues for fewer.
- **Coherence lives in the buyer's head.** A bundle only sells when the buyer already treats its contents as one job, so it has to reflect a grouping the buyer recognizes as belonging together. A grouping that only makes sense on your org chart sits on the price page and sells to nobody.
- **The mainstream versus the intense minority.** The core tiers have to stay legible for the many, while a few power users want a capability the many will never use. A shared tier makes one of the two groups pay for the other.
- **Discount signal versus value signal.** Pricing a bundle below the sum of its parts creates a reason to buy the group, and it teaches the buyer that the list prices are soft.
- **Cross-sell reach versus tier integrity.** Bundles and add-ons both widen what an account can buy, and both can quietly undercut the [[GTM-018 Good-Better-Best]] ladder if a bundle delivers a higher tier's value for a middle tier's price.
- **A bundle is a competitive claim.** A well-built bundle differentiates on a sentence: our suite includes these pieces together for less than the competitor's parts cost separately. That same claim invites a direct price comparison.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Bundle only the modules a buyer already treats as one job, price the group below its parts by enough to justify the wider purchase but not enough to cheapen it, and push every capability that serves an intense minority into an à-la-carte add-on rather than into the core tiers.

Keep the two instruments distinct, because they do opposite work:

1. **Build a bundle as a cross-sell.** Gather modules that solve a connected set of problems and offer them together so more of the suite lands in an account on one purchase. Test it for coherence in the buyer's mind: if a marketing team thinks of email, landing pages, and analytics as one motion, those three make a bundle; if they think of them as separate purchases with separate owners, don't force them together into a package nobody asked for.
2. **Build an add-on as an upsell.** Aim it at the power user who needs one specific capability, and price it on its own so the buyer who wants it can reach it without every other customer paying for it or wading past it. Premium support, an elevated usage ceiling, an advanced security module: ride each alongside the tiers as an optional extra, which keeps the core lineup clean for the mainstream while still collecting from the few who want more.

Apply the same pricing discipline to both. Give a bundle a discount against buying its parts separately, and treat the size of that discount as a real decision instead of a reflex. Open enough of a gap and the buyer sees a clear reason to take the group; open too large a gap and the individual prices stop meaning anything and the whole suite reads as perpetually on sale. The tier lineup these packages live beside belongs to [[GTM-018 Good-Better-Best]]; here you only decide what gets grouped and what gets set to the side.

Economists settled the theory of bundling long before software arrived. William Adams and Janet Yellen gave the formal account in "Commodity Bundling and the Burden of Monopoly" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics* 90:3, 1976, 475–498): grouping goods narrows the spread of what buyers will pay for the package, so one price on the group captures more of the demand than separate prices on the parts. Yannis Bakos and Erik Brynjolfsson carried that into software in "Bundling Information Goods: Pricing, Profits, and Efficiency" (*Management Science* 45:12, 1999, 1613–1630), and theirs is the result that bears directly on how deep your discount can go. Bundling grows more profitable as marginal cost falls toward zero, because the law of large numbers makes a buyer's valuation of a large bundle far easier to predict than their valuation of any single item inside it. Adding one more module to a software bundle costs you almost nothing to ship, so what caps the discount is the damage a deep cut does to your list prices and your tier ladder. Barry Nalebuff's "Bundling as an Entry Barrier" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics* 119:1, 2004, 159–187) supplies the competitive reading: a company holding two goods can bundle them and squeeze a rival holding only one, without having to cut the price of either one.

Use the instruments sparingly. Over-bundling breeds confusion and erodes perceived value, so count the packages a buyer must weigh as a cost in itself. This is the choice-overload result Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper measured with a supermarket jam display ("When Choice Is Demotivating," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 79:6, 2000, 995–1006): the table showing twenty-four varieties stopped 60 percent of passersby against 40 percent for the table showing six, and then 3 percent of the people who stopped at the big display bought a jar, against 30 percent at the small one. Before you add a bundle, confirm a real buyer already groups its contents; before you fold a capability into a tier, confirm the tier's mainstream buyer actually wants it. When the answer is no, put the capability in an add-on and sell the module on its own.

## Implications

A disciplined bundle-and-add-on structure lets one company reach several budgets without multiplying its core tiers. The mainstream buyer sees a clean [[GTM-018 Good-Better-Best]] ladder and picks a rung; the cross-sell buyer sees a coherent suite bundle and buys wider; the power user finds the add-on and pays for intensity the rest never notice. Serve each group through the instrument built for it, and keep the price page legible by letting the complexity live in optional extras rather than in the tier structure everyone reads first.

Grouping more of the suite into an account also does something past the sale: it widens the surface the customer now depends on, one of the raw materials of an [[MS-027 Economic Moat]]. Enforce a discipline against a standing tension: every bundle and add-on is a permanent line on the price page, and the incentive to add one more is constant while the incentive to retire a stale one is weak. Leave the menu unpoliced and it grows back into the tangle this pattern exists to prevent. Prune combinations no buyer actually groups, and keep asking whether a given add-on still serves a minority large enough to be worth the row it occupies.

## Examples

**Microsoft 365 groups apps the buyer already uses together (Office suite from 1990, Office 365 from June 2011, renamed Microsoft 365 in 2020).** Microsoft sells Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook as one subscription rather than four separate purchases, and publishes the price of the group: Microsoft 365 Family lists at $129.99 a year for up to six people after the January 2025 increase, the first since the service launched. The grouping works because the buyer already treats these as one job: office work. The bundle reaches a per-person or per-organization budget that selling Excel alone never would, and the gap against buying each app separately is the reason a buyer takes the whole set.

**HubSpot sells its Hubs separately and as a bundled Customer Platform (product structure as of 2026).** HubSpot sells Marketing Hub, Sales Hub, Service Hub, and others as individual products, then packages them together as the bundled Customer Platform, priced below buying each Hub on its own. A buyer who wants only email marketing takes one Hub; a buyer who runs marketing, sales, and service as one connected motion takes the bundle and lands far more of the platform in the account. HubSpot publishes both sets of prices rather than hiding the gap: the Professional Customer Platform starts at $1,300 a month including six seats, and each Hub carries its own price beside it. HubSpot doesn't publish one headline discount for the bundle, so what a buyer weighs is the arithmetic of their own combination.

**Atlassian bundles Jira and Confluence, and platforms sell capability as add-ons (2025 and 2026).** Atlassian announced its Teamwork Collection on 9 April 2025, grouping Jira, Confluence, and Loom with its Rovo agents for teams that treat planning and documentation as one workflow, and said in the same post that customers can still buy and deploy the apps individually. Separately, the standard à-la-carte move is the platform that sells capacity or an extra capability outside the ladder. GitLab does it plainly: as of August 2026 its Free, Premium, and Ultimate tiers sit beside credits at a dollar each, compute minutes at $10 per thousand, storage at $5 a month for 10 GiB, and Enterprise Agile Planning seats at $15 a user, so the buyer who needs one pays for it and the core tiers stay uncluttered by a capability most will never open.

**Disney priced a three-service streaming bundle under the sum of its parts (2019 onward).** Disney grouped what a household already treats as one line on its credit-card statement. Bob Iger announced the Disney+, Hulu and ESPN+ bundle at $12.99 a month on Disney's investor call of 6 August 2019, to open with Disney+ that 12 November; the three services listed separately at $6.99, $5.99 and $4.99, so the group cost about $5 a month less than its parts. Disney still runs the instrument, listing the ad-supported Disney+ and Hulu pair at $12.99 a month while each of the two alone lists at $11.99. Media companies were pricing bundles like this before software had a price page, and the discipline is the same one: a gap wide enough to move the household to the group, narrow enough that the standalone prices still mean something.
