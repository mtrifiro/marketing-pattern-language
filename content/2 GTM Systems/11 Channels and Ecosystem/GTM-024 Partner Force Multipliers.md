> *A partner lends you their trust and their access, but only after you've proven you can sell without them.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A partner lends trust and access to a motion that has already proved it can sell; direct results make the partner's risk and reward legible before borrowed customer relationships multiply demand. It sits below [[GTM-001 Single Primary Motion]] and above [[GTM-025 Marketplace Presence]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't reach for partners to grow faster before you've proven the product yourself. A reseller's business runs on volume and predictable margin, so you'd be handing them the one product they have the least reason to carry.

The appeal is obvious and the math looks free. A network of resellers, integrators, and agencies reads as a way to reach dozens of markets without hiring dozens of salespeople: one round of recruiting, one round of training, and their salesforces do the selling. The reach is already built, and plugging into it costs a fraction of building your own.

The reasoning breaks on how partners actually make money. A value-added reseller, a systems integrator, an agency all live on the margin and services revenue they earn per deal, and they steer their limited selling attention toward products that close reliably. A new product with no volume behind it is a risky, low-priority sell, and the partner will push the established vendor that's safer and easier every time. Your product sits at the bottom of their catalog. Worse, an early product usually needs hand-holding partners aren't equipped to give, since their reps sell breadth across many vendors and can't absorb the deep, evolving pitch a young product still requires.

Steve Blank states the governing law in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005): channel partners do not create demand, and only your company can. A partner moves product toward buyers who already want it, so a channel signed before you can generate demand yourself adds a margin taker to a problem it has no way to solve.

The hidden cost sits in the program itself. Building a channel program that performs can cost more, and carry more risk, than building a direct sales team, because a real program means partner training and certification, a partner relationship management system, deal registration to keep partners from colliding on the same account, incentive structures, contracts, and co-marketing funds. A company that spends all of that with no proven motion has stood up machinery with nothing reliable to sell. Geoffrey Moore made the underlying point in *Crossing the Chasm* (1991), where he argues that a distribution channel works best when its job is to fulfill demand, and that creating the demand stays with the vendor. The channel multiplies whatever motion you already have, including a broken one.

## Complications

- **Partners are motivated by profit and volume.** A reseller's selling time is scarce, so it flows to whatever closes reliably and pays well; an unproven product with no demand loses that competition to established vendors by default.
- **Reach is real and large.** Indirect channels are how most commerce moves. Jay McBain, then Forrester's channels and ecosystems analyst, put 75 percent of world trade through them, citing World Trade Organization figures, in "Does Your Channel Run In A Silo?" (Forrester, 21 July 2017). The right partner already holds relationships, contracts, and credibility in accounts a direct team would take years to enter.
- **Trust transfers, but only if you have something to lend against.** A partner's endorsement carries their credibility onto your product; that transfer works only once you can show references and a value proposition that survives contact with a real buyer.
- **The channel is expensive to build well.** Certification, deal registration, a PRM system, incentives, and co-marketing cost real money and management attention, and a half-built program performs worse than no program at all.
- **Early products need hand-holding partners can't give.** A young product still teaches its own pitch on every call, and a multi-vendor rep can't carry that depth, so early channel sales stall in exactly the deals that need the most care.
- **Channel conflict is self-inflicted.** When direct reps and partners chase the same account, both pull back or race to discount, and the confusion costs you the deal and the relationship.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Prove you can sell the product directly first, then recruit partners once you have a stable base, marquee references, and demand you can't reach alone, and design every partner relationship as a win for the partner's own business.

1. **Sell it yourself first.** At the beginning, a direct motion is your best route to revenue; once you've built some critical mass, you can turn on the channel. The direct motion you're proving is its own pattern, [[GTM-004 Repeatable Sales Motion]]. Your partners will not sell your product until you have proven you can sell your product. A working direct motion gives a partner the two things they need before they commit their reps: evidence that the product closes and a pitch refined enough to hand over.
2. **Turn on the channel only when the signals appear.** Readiness looks like a stable base of customers, a proven value proposition, and often excess demand you can't serve directly. Watch for inbound interest from resellers or consultancies who've noticed your traction, geographic gaps you can't staff, complementary fit with vendors who already own a channel into your buyers, or a segment that buys through intermediaries by rule, since government, education, and healthcare procurement often force a partner path. Any of these means the channel is now an accelerant.
3. **Land marquee logos directly, then recruit against them.** Close the marquee customers with your own team, then recruit partners using those logos as proof they're joining a winner.
4. **Design a win-win or the program dies quietly.** Partners prioritize products that drive their own services revenue, fill a gap in their portfolio, or pay strong incentives through margin, market development funds, and qualified leads. Build the relationship around the partner's economics.
5. **Draw the conflict lines up front.** Delineate which accounts and geographies are direct and which are partner-led, so your own team and your partners never arrive at the same buyer from opposite directions.

Marketplaces are a related but distinct route into procurement, handled by [[GTM-025 Marketplace Presence]]: a reseller relationship is a person carrying your pitch, a marketplace listing is a self-serve buying path, and the two solve different problems. Before you sign a single partner, ask whether you can already close the deal without one; if the answer is no, keep selling.

## Implications

A working channel changes how far your reach extends. Companies that build one well can end up with partners carrying a large minority of revenue; almost none get there in year one, and the ones that do built the channel on top of a direct motion that already worked. Once partners are producing, let your owned reach and your borrowed reach compound: partner relationships extend the reach you already own, [[GTM-025 Marketplace Presence]] opens the self-serve procurement route alongside them, and the marquee references from the direct motion become the recruiting material that brings the next tier of partners in.

Two things about how channel money and channel information actually move are worth knowing before you sign anything.

An order from a partner isn't a sale. Blank separates sell-in, the product moving from you to the partner, from sell-through, the product moving from the partner to someone who will use it, and only the second one is demand. A partner who is stocking, or who holds product on consignment, or who can return what doesn't move, can report a strong quarter while nothing has reached an end customer; the correction arrives later as returns, as a quarter with no orders at all, or as a request for price protection on inventory they can't shift. Where the arrangement allows returns, count sell-through and treat sell-in as a shipping statistic.

The margin stack also takes more than the folklore suggests, and the published numbers run the other way from the blogs. Channel marketing routinely quotes reseller margins of twenty to forty percent; the audited accounts of companies actually doing the work read differently, because a reseller's margin is measured against everything it invoices rather than as a discount off one list price. Bytes Technology Group, a UK software reseller, reported gross profit at 7.1% of gross invoiced income for the year ended 28 February 2026. Softcat, a broader reseller, reported a 13.7% gross margin for the year ended 31 July 2025. On the distribution tier, TD SYNNEX reported 6.99% gross margin for fiscal 2025 and Ingram Micro 6.67%, both blended across hardware and software rather than software alone. Atlassian's Marketplace Partner Agreement caps a partner's discount at 20% of list. Those are four separate disclosures rather than one published stack, so assembling them into a chain is an estimate rather than a citation, and the useful move is to build the same estimate for your own channel from the agreements you're actually signing, before you design the program.

The named types differ in what they take and what they do for it. A distributor carries credit, logistics and breadth, sells to resellers rather than to end customers, and works on the thinnest margin in the chain. A value-added reseller owns the end-customer relationship and earns on the services around your product as much as on the product. An OEM embeds your product inside its own and sells it under its own name, which takes the most margin and leaves you with the least visibility into who is actually running the thing. Blank's axis sorts all three: the smaller your product is as a share of a partner's revenue, the less selling attention it gets, and no clause in a contract reverses that.

From here the discipline shifts from growth to management. A channel introduces ongoing costs and a permanent risk of conflict, so treat the delineation of direct versus partner territory as a standing discipline and revisit it as you add reps and partners add coverage. Guard against dependency: reach that lives in a partner's salesforce can be withdrawn or de-prioritized the moment a competitor offers better margin, so keep your own direct motion alive even as the channel scales, both as a floor under revenue and as the proof that keeps partners believing the product is worth their time.

## Examples

**Land and expand through the channel (composite).** An unproven product handed to systems integrators sinks to the bottom of their catalog. Companies that get the order right sell the first marquee customers with their own team, refining the pitch and building references in the process, and approach the integrators only afterward. By then the product has proof it closes and named customers the partners can point to, which turns recruiting from a cold ask into an easy yes. This describes how many enterprise software companies have sequenced the channel; the two cases below are named instances of it.

**AWS and its consulting-partner ecosystem for enterprise data platforms (2016 onward).** Data-platform vendors building on AWS, Snowflake and Databricks among them, grew enterprise revenue substantially through AWS's network of consulting and systems-integration partners, who carried the products into large accounts they already served. The sequence held: these vendors proved direct demand and reference customers first, then the integrators, drawn by the services revenue an implementation generates, made the products a priority in accounts a direct team couldn't have entered as fast.

**Caterpillar reaches 191 countries through dealers it doesn't own (structure as reported in its 2023 Form 10-K).** The company builds its reach out of independent businesses whose own income depends on its product. Caterpillar's 10-K describes distributing its machines "principally through a worldwide organization of dealers," 43 in the United States and 113 outside it, serving 191 countries, then states the economics plainly: those dealers "do not deal exclusively with our products; however, in most cases sales and servicing of our products are the dealers' principal business." Both halves of that sentence are this pattern. The dealer's attention is contested every day, and Caterpillar holds it by being the line the dealer's own service business runs on. The company also keeps two product lines out of the channel, selling turbines and locomotives directly to end customers with its own salesforce, which is the conflict line drawn in advance of a collision rather than after one.

Capital equipment is also where the sequencing rule above reaches its limit. In industrial distribution the dealer network is the market-entry structure itself, so there's no direct-first phase to prove; what earns a dealer's investment is the parts-and-service revenue your machine will generate across its life. Partners weigh a product by what it does for their own income statement. When the answer is nothing, no amount of sequencing will rescue the program.
