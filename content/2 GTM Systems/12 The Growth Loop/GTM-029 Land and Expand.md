> *Get in through a side door for almost nothing, then build the account you couldn't have sold on day one.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Land and expand enters through one small foothold, then turns real use inside the account into the proof and opportunity for a much larger relationship. It sits below [[GTM-026 Growth Loop]] and above [[GTM-030 Freemium-to-Enterprise]], [[GTM-031 Support Is Sales]], [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]], and [[GTM-033 Expansion Before Acquisition]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't demand the big enterprise purchase up front, and don't take the small cheap foothold for the win. A land that never grows costs more to serve than it pays, and most companies stop at the land.

A large first contract asks a buyer to commit budget, run procurement, satisfy security review, and predict a company-wide rollout, all before anyone has used the product. That's a long, expensive, high-risk sale, and it filters out most of the accounts that would eventually succeed. The buyer can't justify the commitment because the value is unproven, and you spend heavily to win a deal that half the time collapses in the evaluation. The up-front enterprise motion is where customer acquisition cost goes to die.

A first purchase small enough that almost no one has to say no removes that friction. One team, one use case, a free tier or a modest first contract, and the barrier that killed the big sale disappears. Landing a low-friction foothold and then growing it is the B2B growth motion behind Slack, Zoom, and Datadog. The land on its own, though, is a poor deal. A single team on a free plan or a $10,000 contract doesn't cover the cost of serving it, and where the account stops there the company has traded an expensive sale for an unprofitable customer.

The account's value lives in the second move. Expansion, more seats, more usage, more teams, more products, is where it accumulates, and expansion doesn't happen on its own. A land that never expands is a small, unprofitable account wearing the costume of a growth motion. Companies fall into this because the land feels like a win: the logo is on the board, the product is in use, the metrics look alive. Then no one owns the growth of the account, the expansion is left to chance, and the motion quietly fails while looking like it's working.

## Complications

- **The small land lowers acquisition friction; the small land also lowers revenue.** A cheap foothold sidesteps procurement and proof, so more accounts open, and each one arrives worth less than it costs to serve until it grows.
- **Expansion is far cheaper than new-logo acquisition; expansion is also easy to neglect.** Selling more to an account that already trusts you costs a fraction of winning a stranger, yet comp plans, dashboards, and attention default to new logos.
- **Expansion has to be systematic, and systems are harder to build than hope.** Playbooks, health monitoring, and usage triggers make expansion repeatable; wishing an account will grow makes it random, and the machinery costs more to build than the land did.
- **The motion depends on retention, and retention is a separate discipline.** A customer who churns can't expand, so the whole model rests on the account staying and succeeding first, the work [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]] owns.
- **A land priced too low can anchor the account cheap.** The foothold that wins entry can also teach the buyer what the product is worth, and the later enterprise conversation starts from that number.
- **Expansion revenue shows up later than acquisition cost.** You pay to land now and collect the account's real value over quarters, so the motion looks unprofitable exactly when you're deciding whether to keep funding it.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Enter through the smallest foothold that reaches real use, then run expansion through named owners and product signals. Net revenue retention above 100 percent is the test that expansion is paying for the land.

Treat the land and the expansion as two different jobs.

1. **Engineer the land for the lowest possible friction.** Offer free entry, self-serve signup, or a small first contract that one budget-holder can approve without a committee. Aim it at one thing: real usage inside one corner of the account, because usage is the proof the later, larger sale will rest on. The land is cheap precisely because it sidesteps the big up-front enterprise purchase, and the account's value accrues in the expansion. Keep track of what the land has and hasn't established. Real usage shows the value lands and that people reach it without help; it says nothing yet about whether this account holds a budget and an approval path for the larger contract, and [[GTM-030 Freemium-to-Enterprise]] separates the two.
2. **Build expansion like machinery instead of wishing it into place.** Put someone in charge of the account's growth, [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]] names that owner. Give the growth triggers rather than moods: a team hitting a usage ceiling, a new department appearing in the logs, a support ticket that reveals a second use case, [[GTM-031 Support Is Sales]] reads that last one. Tie the whole motion to net revenue retention, the metric [[GTM-033 Expansion Before Acquisition]] owns: clearing 100% is the signature of a land-and-expand motion that's working rather than one that merely landed. Where the entry is a free product, run the ramp through [[GTM-030 Freemium-to-Enterprise]]; where the whole base is the priority, let [[GTM-033 Expansion Before Acquisition]] govern the budget.

Secure retention first, or the motion collapses. A customer who leaves can't expand, so drive the account to real value and keep it there before any expansion motion has anything to work with. The small land is a bet the expansion has to pay off: it only earns out if the expansion machinery actually runs, so don't celebrate the foothold and then staff nothing to grow it.

## Implications

A working land-and-expand motion changes what your go-to-market team optimizes. Acquisition stops being the whole game and becomes the cheap first step, which opens the patterns that carry the account upward: put [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]] on retention and growth, turn service interactions into expansion signals with [[GTM-031 Support Is Sales]], run [[GTM-030 Freemium-to-Enterprise]] where the foothold is free, and use [[GTM-033 Expansion Before Acquisition]] to decide when the existing base deserves the next dollar before a new logo does. Make the account itself the growth engine, and net revenue retention above 100% the number that proves it, which is how this motion feeds the compounding circuit of [[GTM-026 Growth Loop]].

Then enforce the discipline against a motion that hides its own failure. A land that never expands looks identical to a healthy one for a while: the logo is real, the usage is real, the dashboards are green. The difference only shows up in whether the account grows, and that takes quarters to read, which will tempt you to keep landing new footholds while the old ones stagnate. Don't. And don't price the land so low that it anchors the account cheap and makes expansion a fight; the cleanest fix is a model where usage carries revenue upward on its own, which is why [[GTM-017 Usage-Based Pricing]] sits so close to this pattern.

## Examples

**Slack landing one team, then spreading company-wide (2014 onward).** Slack's motion was to get a single team, often a handful of engineers, onto a free workspace, where the product's value came from daily use inside that team. As the team relied on it, adjacent teams joined, usage crossed departments, and the free footprint grew until it crossed into a paid, company-wide plan that no salesperson had to sell cold. The land cost almost nothing; the value of the account was built entirely after that first team signed up.

**Datadog landing one product and a small host count, then expanding across products and infrastructure (mid-2010s onward).** Datadog typically entered an account with a single product, infrastructure monitoring on a modest number of hosts, priced by usage. As the customer's infrastructure grew and as the company shipped adjacent products, log management, APM, security monitoring, the account expanded across both more hosts and more products. Its usage-based model meant expansion happened partly as a by-product of the customer's own growth, and its reported net revenue retention above 130% from 2019 into 2022 was the number that showed the expansion machinery running.

**Zoom landing a department, then going enterprise-wide (2013 onward).** Zoom often entered through one department or team that adopted it for reliable video meetings, frequently starting on a free or low-cost tier. The product's ease of use spread it through the organization by word of mouth and shared meeting links, and that internal spread turned a departmental foothold into an enterprise-wide contract. The land removed the up-front enterprise commitment; the expansion, driven by usage spreading through the company, is where the large account was built.
