> *The cheapest next dollar is already inside a customer you have; grow the base before you go buy a new one.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Expansion gives the installed base first claim on growth investment because the cheapest next dollar is already inside a customer the company has won, so retained revenue compounds before new-logo spend rises. It sits below [[GTM-026 Growth Loop]], [[GTM-029 Land and Expand]], and [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat new-logo acquisition as the engine of growth and the installed base as a maintenance chore. Point your comp and your product investment at strangers while existing customers go under-served and quietly churning, and you'll acquire ever faster just to stand still.

The bias toward strangers starts with what gets celebrated. A closed-won deal rings a bell; a renewal doesn't. Sales comp pays richly for a new name and thinly, or not at all, for growing an account you already have. Board decks lead with logos added, and the marketing budget follows the same instinct, filling the top and counting the arrivals. The customer who signed last year becomes someone else's problem, handed to a support queue and a renewal reminder. With all the energy pointed outward at strangers, the people who already chose the product get the least attention in the building.

The expense of that order hides in plain sight. Winning a new customer means paying for awareness, demand generation, sales cycles, discounts, and onboarding, and it costs several times more than selling one more seat or one more module to a customer who already trusts the product. Dave Kellogg's reframe, cited in [[GTM-026 Growth Loop]], is that churn was the old obsession and net dollar expansion is the new one; a company counting only new logos is measuring the top of a leaking bucket. An under-served base churns, and churn is the fee for ignoring the customers you worked hardest to win.

Left uncorrected, this runs acquisition up a down escalator. A company depending entirely on new logos has to replace everything the base lost before it shows a single dollar of progress, so the faster it grows the more it spends to stay in place. Retention is the lifeblood. Keeping and growing a customer costs far less than winning a new one, and upselling and cross-selling raise average contract value without generating a single extra lead. Acquisition spend that runs ahead of that repair pours water into a bucket nobody patched.

## Complications

- **Acquisition is legible; expansion is quiet.** A new logo is a visible event with a name and a date, so it draws comp and attention, while expansion accrues inside existing contracts where no bell rings, which is exactly why it gets starved.
- **Expansion is cheaper and higher-margin.** Selling more to a customer who already trusts the product carries no new acquisition cost and little onboarding drag, so an expansion dollar drops to the bottom line at a far better rate than an acquired one.
- **A base above break-even compounds on its own.** When expansion within the base more than offsets churn, revenue grows with no new logos at all, and that growth compounds year over year in a way a fresh acquisition never does.
- **A leaking base makes acquisition futile.** If the base churns faster than it expands, every acquired dollar first backfills a lost one, so spending on acquisition before fixing retention is running to stay in place.
- **Expansion is the proof that cheapens acquisition.** Expanding, referenceable customers generate the case studies, usage data, and word of mouth that lower the cost of the next new logo, so the base is also the sales asset for acquisition.
- **The base is finite, so acquisition can't stop.** Expansion has a ceiling inside any fixed set of accounts, and a company that never adds logos eventually saturates its base, which makes this a question of sequence and emphasis rather than of abandoning new-logo growth.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Give the installed base first claim on growth investment and scale new-logo acquisition only after the base compounds. Pull the last four quarters of net revenue retention; all four must clear 100 percent before acquisition spend rises.

Read the base with one number, then sequence the spend.

1. **Gate acquisition on net revenue retention.** NRR measures whether expansion inside the installed base offsets churn; above 100% the base grows on its own before you add a customer. Bessemer's *State of the Cloud 2023* grades it at 100 percent good, 110 better, and 120 or above best in class. Clear 100 before you pour on acquisition; below that line, acquisition spend subsidizes a leak.
2. **Point comp at account growth.** Reward the field for expanding accounts so it works the base instead of abandoning it at signature.
3. **Invest product in the second and third sale.** Fund the cross-sell and the upsell that raise average contract value without generating a single new lead.
4. **Make retention a growth stage.** Move it out of the support-cost column and give it to [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]] to run.
5. **Then scale acquisition.** New logos widen the base, because expansion has a ceiling inside any fixed set of accounts, so reopen acquisition once the base compounds.

Bank the second dividend, because it closes Part II's loop. A base that expands is a base full of customers using more, succeeding more, and willing to say so, and that success is the raw material of cheap acquisition: the references, the case studies, the usage numbers, and the word of mouth that make the next new logo easier and less expensive to win. Start growth inside the base, feed the acquisition that widens it, and let the widened base become the next round's expansion surface. That circuit is the [[GTM-026 Growth Loop]] this pattern completes; expansion is the stage where the loop compounds, and acquisition is the stage it makes affordable. Run the honesty test now: pull NRR for the last four quarters. Below 100 percent, freeze acquisition scaling and fix the base; above it, open the tap.

## Implications

Put expansion first and you reorder the whole go-to-market system and close the loop Part II opened. Shift your metrics from logos added to net revenue retention, and have Part III's operating cadence track the base's compounding rather than the top of the funnel. Give [[GTM-032 Customer Success as Growth Engine]] its mandate here, because a base that must grow before acquisition scales needs a function whose job is to expand it. Read [[GTM-029 Land and Expand]] for its purpose, since the land is only the entry and the expand is where the return lives. And let [[GTM-026 Growth Loop]] make its closing move: the expanding base produces the proof that makes acquisition cheap, so growth compounds instead of running out the bottom. For the unit-economics case beneath all of it, the actual cost-to-acquire against cost-to-expand, go to [[MS-036 Unit Economics as Market Truth]].

Enforce two disciplines against saturation and impatience. Expansion has a ceiling: grow only your base and you eventually run out of base to grow, so don't defer acquisition forever, only sequence it behind a base that compounds. And expansion accrues slowly and quietly while acquisition rings a bell the same quarter the spend goes in, which will tempt your team back to the legible metric and the fast dopamine of a new logo. Hold them to net revenue retention over time. Until that number clears 100 percent, treat the base as a bucket with a hole and treat acquisition spend as water poured through it.

Net dollar expansion above 120 percent means the installed base alone would grow revenue by a fifth in a year with no new customers at all. In a company at that level the growth story is the base compounding, and acquisition adds surface area on top of an engine that already runs. Dave Kellogg's net-dollar-expansion framing and Bessemer's grading point at the same order to copy: expansion first, acquisition second.

## Examples

**Snowflake and Datadog growing mostly through the base (2020–2023).** Both reported net retention far above the level at which a base grows on its own. Snowflake's S-1, filed in August 2020, put net revenue retention at 158 percent, and the figure reached 168 percent by the close of fiscal 2021, carried by consumption that rose as customers moved more data in. Datadog held dollar-based net retention above 130 percent across 2019 to 2022, as teams added products and monitored more infrastructure, and has run below that since. A base at 158 percent grows revenue by more than half in a year with no new customers at all, and both companies then used those expanding, referenceable accounts as the sales proof that won the next wave of enterprise buyers. The base carried the growth, and the base sold the next customer.

**Chewy grew 6.4 percent on a customer base that grew 2.1 percent (fiscal 2024).** Chewy reported full-year fiscal 2024 net sales of $11.86 billion, up 6.4 percent, with 20.5 million active customers at year end, up 2.1 percent from 20.1 million. Net sales per active customer rose to $578 from $555, and Autoship customers accounted for 79.2 percent of net sales against 76.2 percent the year before. Nearly all of the growth came from customers Chewy already had. Pet supplies has no seats to add and no modules to cross-sell, and the arithmetic runs the same way anyway: the recurring order is the expansion, the base carries the year, and new customers widen the surface that the next year expands on.

**A business that fixed churn and expansion before scaling acquisition spend (composite).** Scaling acquisition on top of a leaking base drives cost-to-acquire up while revenue stalls, because every new dollar backfills a churned one. The company patched activation and churn, built expansion into the account motion until NRR cleared 100 percent, and only then reopened acquisition, at which point new logos landed on a base that grew itself and the same spend produced real progress instead of standing still.
