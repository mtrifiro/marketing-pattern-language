> *Make the company understand what the market is telling it before you make it louder.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Marketing creates sense before it creates volume: buyer conversations, deal outcomes, and product use become a current interpretation the whole company can act on before amplification makes any misunderstanding larger. It sits above [[OPS-002 Win-Loss Discipline]], [[OPS-003 Feedback Before Scale]], and [[OPS-004 Closing the Loop]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't measure your marketing team on the volume it ships: campaigns, content, leads. A team paid for output gets better at broadcasting every quarter, and you end up scaling a motion built on assumptions nobody has checked.

The default definition of marketing is decoration: make the logo nicer, the deck slicker, the funnel fuller. A team that accepts that definition organizes entirely around production; it ships more content, runs more campaigns, and books more demos, and it reports on all of it, so the dashboards look busy and the quarter looks productive. The market is talking the whole time, in sales calls, support tickets, churned accounts, and the exact words prospects use to describe their problem, and a production-focused team hears none of it, because listening was never written into anyone's job description.

The cost shows up later and larger. A company that markets without sensemaking scales its own misunderstanding. It pours budget into a message that tests the founder's assumption rather than the buyer's reality, targets a persona that looked right on a slide, and optimizes a funnel whose top stage was named after the company's org chart instead of the buyer's actual journey. When growth stalls, the team reaches for the only lever it has. A production team's instinct is to produce more, so it doubles the campaigns and the leads, and the misunderstanding compounds.

Karl Weick gave the missing work a name. His *Sensemaking in Organizations* (1995) describes how organizations assemble a workable account of what's happening out of scattered cues, then act on that account and make it real. Steve Blank turned the same idea into an operating method in *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005), where customer development treats every element of a plan, the buyer, the problem, the price, the channel, as a hypothesis, and where his standing instruction is that there are no facts inside the building, so get outside and find them. Weick names what the work is; Blank names where you have to go to do it. In a young market the facts that decide everything are who actually buys, why they buy, what language moves them, and which of the company's beliefs the market has quietly rejected. A team organized around production can be extremely busy for a year and know none of them.

## Complications

- **Production is legible; interpretation is not.** Campaigns, leads, and content are easy to count and report, so a team measured on activity drifts toward output and away from the harder, quieter work of figuring out what the market means.
- **The market talks constantly, but not in a form you can read by default.** Every sales call, support ticket, and churned account carries signal, yet it arrives as unstructured noise.
- **Assumptions feel like knowledge.** A founder's read on the buyer is usually the starting hypothesis, and it's comfortable to treat it as settled truth, which is exactly what stops a team from checking it against what buyers say.
- **Speed rewards broadcasting; durability rewards listening.** Shipping more is faster and looks better this quarter, while building an interpretive loop pays off over several.
- **Interpretation only matters if it reaches decisions.** A brilliant read that never changes the roadmap, the message, or the target is decoration of a different kind.
- **Scaling amplifies whatever you've understood, including the errors.** More budget on a correct read compounds; more budget on a wrong one compounds too.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Make marketing's first operational job the interpretation of the market. Build the systems that turn buyer conversations, won and lost deals, and product usage into a clear, current read of what's true, and feed that read back into strategy on a cadence, so the company acts on understanding rather than on untested assumption.

Install the sense organ before the megaphone. Don't run sensemaking as a research project once; stand up a permanent set of intake and interpretation systems that the rest of Part III builds out. Do these three things, in this order of dependency:

1. **Capture the words and read the deals.** Record what buyers actually say in a structured, searchable form, and interrogate every won and lost deal for the pattern behind the outcome, which is [[OPS-002 Win-Loss Discipline]].
2. **Understand before you spend.** Treat the market's response as the thing to grasp before you pour money into a motion, which is [[OPS-003 Feedback Before Scale]].
3. **Move the insight.** Carry the resulting read back into product and strategy on a repeatable rhythm, which is [[OPS-004 Closing the Loop]].

Insist that these systems exist and that they outrank production in the team's definition of its work.

Blend the art with the science, and let the science govern. Keep narrative, positioning, and craft; sensemaking directs them rather than replacing them. Let the interpretation tell the team what story is true for this buyer, so the craft has something real to express rather than a guess to dress up. A mature marketing function looks exactly like this: an operation running a dashboard and a feedback loop, so metrics, experiments, and buyer signal keep correcting the narrative instead of letting it run unchecked. Test the function this way: have it state, in one sentence, something about the market that the company believes today and didn't believe six months ago, and make it point to the deal reviews or buyer conversations that changed its mind. If it can't, the loop isn't running.

Make the read reach decisions. Don't let interpretation stay inside the marketing team, where it becomes decoration wearing a lab coat. Land the output of sensemaking where you set strategy: in the roadmap, the positioning, the target account list, and the motion itself. Own that handoff as a discipline of its own through [[OPS-004 Closing the Loop]], the thing that separates a company that learns from one that merely files research. Bend the company's actions toward what the market is saying.

## Implications

Once you treat marketing as sensemaking, run the whole operations layer of Part III off it. Make the intake patterns beneath this one, [[OPS-002 Win-Loss Discipline]], [[OPS-003 Feedback Before Scale]], and [[OPS-004 Closing the Loop]], the company's nervous system, and hold the demand, content, team, and measurement machinery of the later chapters to the same premise: their real job is to produce and act on an accurate read of the market rather than to maximize output. Change measurement first. Count the numbers that change a decision, which is why [[OPS-020 Metrics That Change Behavior]] later governs the readouts. Use sensemaking to keep a scaling motion honest, because it supplies the understanding that [[OPS-003 Feedback Before Scale]] demands before the spending accelerates.

Enforce the discipline against two standing pressures. Sensemaking is slower and less legible than production, and it competes for the same people and hours, so in any given week a team committed to interpretation will ship less and report fewer vanity numbers than a pure production team; hold the line under short-horizon pressure anyway. Guard against interpretation that never closes into action, too: a team can build beautiful buyer research and deal reviews and change nothing, at which point sensemaking has become its own kind of decoration. Keep the loop closing into decisions, which is why the cadence of [[OPS-004 Closing the Loop]] and the quarterly reset of [[OPS-019 Quarterly Recalibration]] exist downstream. Run them.

## Examples

**HubSpot building its strategy on the buyer's own words (2006–2014).** HubSpot, founded in 2006, grew by treating the market as something to understand and teach rather than something to shout at. Its early operation listened to how small businesses described their marketing problem, then fed that language back into its content, its product, and the category Brian Halligan and Dharmesh Shah named in *Inbound Marketing* (2009). The marketing function behaved as a sense organ for the market it was trying to create, which is why its positioning read as recognition rather than as a pitch. The S-1 HubSpot filed in August 2014 counted 11,624 customers as of that June, up from 5,783 at the end of 2011, on revenue of $77.6 million in 2013.

**Domino's rebuilding the pizza after hearing what customers called it (2009–2010).** Domino's could have taken the market's verdict on its product as a communications problem and answered it with better advertising. Its consumer research kept returning the same sentences: the crust tasted like cardboard, the sauce tasted like ketchup. Domino's read those as a finding about the recipe instead. Under then-president Patrick Doyle the company reformulated the pizza, put it in stores in December 2009, and built the "Pizza Turnaround" campaign around the criticism itself, with the focus-group verdicts read out on camera. Domino's then reported domestic same-store sales up 14.3% in the first quarter of 2010, which it credited to store traffic from the new recipe. The interpretation changed the product, and the promotion reported the change.

**The team that scaled a message it never checked (composite).** A team runs for a year on the founder's original read of the buyer, pouring budget into a message and a persona that felt right. Companies do exactly this and then discover through win-loss review that their real buyer, their real objection, and their real competitor were all different from the assumption. The team was highly productive the whole time; it just wasn't making sense of anything. Once it started interrogating lost deals, the corrected read reshaped the ICP, the message, and the hiring plan.

**A founder-led team that turned every call into signal.** In my own operating experience, the most valuable early marketing work wasn't a campaign; it was sitting in on sales calls and support conversations and writing down the exact phrases buyers used, then noticing which objection recurred and which value claim landed. That listening changed the positioning more than any creative exercise did, because it replaced what we assumed the buyer felt with what they said. Production that comes after the listening is better for having something true to say.
