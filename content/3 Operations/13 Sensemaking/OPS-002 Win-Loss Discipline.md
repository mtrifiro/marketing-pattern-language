> *Every deal already ran the experiment; refuse to throw the result away.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Every closed deal has already run an experiment, and win-loss discipline preserves the buyer's own explanation so isolated outcomes become a record the whole company can query over time. It sits below [[OPS-001 Marketing as Sensemaking]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't let a one-word dropdown, filled in by the person with the most reason to pick a comfortable answer, be your record of why a deal closed. You'll accumulate outcomes and never learn the pattern behind them.

A rep marks a deal "lost on price" and moves to the next opportunity, and the CRM files another data point that explains nothing. Price is the reason reps reach for because it costs them nothing to say and doesn't implicate the discovery call they rushed, the objection they couldn't answer, or the competitor whose story landed better. "We lost on price" typically hides a value gap or a trust gap the buyer never framed as money at all.

The mistake survives a long time, and it feels like confidence the whole way. You tell yourself you lose on price, so you discount; the discounting doesn't move the win rate, because price was never the problem, so you discount more. Meanwhile the won deals go unexamined, because a win doesn't trigger a post-mortem the way a loss triggers a blame search, and the reasons you win are your actual value proposition stated by the only people qualified to state it.

The same loss runs one layer below the dropdown, in the words themselves. The phrase a prospect used for their problem, the objection they raised twice, the competitor they named unprompted: the rep registers some of it and has forgotten most by the next call, and what survives lands in the CRM as "good conversation, sent pricing," which records that the call happened and loses what was said. A buyer who says "I need to stop getting paged at 3 a.m." has handed you the message that will move them; a note reading "interested in reliability" has replaced it with your own abstraction.

A single loss on its own points at the last thing that went wrong. The information sits one level up, in the segment you keep losing for the same reason and the objection that recurs. That pattern stays invisible to a company that reads its deals one at a time, and it's the read [[OPS-001 Marketing as Sensemaking]] insists the operation exists to produce.

A finished campaign holds the same evidence. A review that never happens, or one that opens by asking whose fault the miss was, produces an account edited for self-protection before anyone speaks: the marketer calls the channel choice reasonable, the rep blames procurement, and the fact that would teach the team something is the fact that makes its teller look bad. Six months later the team repeats the misjudgment, because the lesson lived in one person's head.

## Complications

- **The rep's account is self-serving.** The person who lost the deal is least able to report why, because the honest reasons implicate their own execution.
- **Price is the universal alibi.** "We lost on price" is comfortable for everyone, which is exactly why it's usually a proxy for a value or trust gap the buyer felt but never named as money.
- **Raw language decays fastest and carries the most.** The buyer's exact phrasing is what resets positioning, and memory and CRM summaries both compress it into gist within days.
- **Blame feels like accountability.** A leader under pressure reaches for a name because it looks like taking the problem seriously, and it teaches the room to edit the next account.
- **Losses trigger blame; wins trigger celebration.** A lost deal gets a defensive autopsy and a won deal gets a high-five, so you study the noisier signal and ignore the clearer one.
- **One deal is an anecdote; the pattern is the asset.** Reacting to the last loss chases individual outcomes, while the value hides in the cluster of deals that share a root cause, and a read that stays in a deck changes nothing at all.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Treat every closed deal and every finished campaign as a completed experiment. Keep the buyer's own words verbatim in a record anyone can query, interview the buyer instead of the rep, run the review blamelessly, cluster the results until the root cause behind them shows itself, and route that truth back into strategy.

Run the discipline as a repeatable process:

1. **Keep the words themselves.** Pull buyer language from where it already lives (recorded sales calls, support tickets, exit interviews with churned accounts, inbound questions) into one record by default, so capture doesn't depend on a busy rep writing things down after a call they've mentally closed, and let it live in the shared memory [[OPS-006 CRM as Shared Memory]] governs. Abbie Griffin and John Hauser gave the sequence its academic form in "The Voice of the Customer" (*Marketing Science*, 1993): identify needs in the customer's own language, structure them, then prioritize them. Keep the phrasing verbatim and tag it by the problem discussed, the objection raised, and the competitor named, because a pile of transcripts nobody can query is as useless as no capture.
2. **Ask the buyer, as an outsider to the sale.** The rep has a stake in the answer, so send someone the buyer has no reason to flatter or protect, and press past the first reason to the one underneath it. Steve Blank built *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005) on the same refusal to accept an in-house account of what a customer thinks, and a rep-authored loss reason is the purest example of one. Klue publishes a seven-step guide to win-loss analysis that works as a starting method: tie every question to what you're trying to learn, keep the calls short, and structure what you hear so a pile of outcomes turns into a read you can act on. Move the loss reason into the buyer's own words, where "too expensive" resolves into "we didn't believe you could handle our compliance case," a message and product problem wearing a price label.
3. **Read won deals with the same rigor as lost ones.** Interview your wins and you'll usually find buyers chose you for a reason the marketing never emphasized. Promote that one.
4. **Say the blameless rule out loud, at the top, every time.** Ask what happened and why, and keep the question off the person, because the most useful fact in any review usually incriminates whoever reports it, and punishing that exposure guarantees you get a comfortable version next time. Decide the triggers in advance so the review fires whenever a deal, campaign, or event above a stated size finishes, and hold it while the details are sharp; speed protects the data and routine protects the practice. Fire it only after a bad quarter and it reads as punishment, which poisons the safety it depends on. Write the finding up as a short case study the next team can find, because a lesson that stays in one team's notes helps that team once.
5. **Look across the set.** The discipline pays off when several deals resolve to the same root cause, because that cluster is a strategic instruction: it names the segment to stop chasing, the objection to answer in the deck, or the capability to build. Treat one deal as noise and the pattern across many as the signal, and go find it.

Close the loop into decisions. Don't let the program produce a beautiful quarterly readout that changes no target and no roadmap, which is the decorative research [[OPS-001 Marketing as Sensemaking]] warns against. Land the findings where you set strategy, and hold the gate [[OPS-003 Feedback Before Scale]] keeps, which refuses to fund a motion whose loss pattern the deal reviews haven't produced yet.

## Implications

Run win-loss discipline and you stop arguing about your buyer from opinion and start arguing from the buyer's own words. Discounting gets harder to justify, and positioning gets sharper as the won-deal interviews keep surfacing the value proposition that closes. Every closed deal and finished campaign also leaves a written lesson behind, and inside a year that collection is the closest thing a young marketing organization has to institutional knowledge. Expect it to change how people work before the review as well as after: a team that knows the outcome gets examined honestly names its assumptions earlier and stops treating a launch as a verdict on its worth. This is the sense organ [[OPS-001 Marketing as Sensemaking]] describes, tuned to the moment of decision.

Enforce the discipline against its weaknesses, because it's slow, uncomfortable, and easy to fake. Interviewing buyers takes time the pipeline is always demanding back, so protect the hours. Capturing everything is easy and reading it is hard, so tag as you capture; a team that records every call and tags nothing has built an archive nobody queries. Answer the consent and recording question honestly, because a company taping its calls has to. Guard the blamelessness hardest, since one review where an honest account gets someone punished teaches the whole team that the safety was a performance, and the next accounts come back edited. Then guard against the version that interviews diligently, clusters carefully, files the report and changes nothing, at which point it flatters the team without correcting it. Hold the discipline by making the findings reach strategy on a cadence, which is why this pattern hands the loop to [[OPS-004 Closing the Loop]] and the reset to [[OPS-019 Quarterly Recalibration]] instead of trying to own the whole chain.

## Examples

**The loss reason the reps never reported (composite).** A B2B company read its reps' dropdowns and discounted against "lost on price." Its CRM showed a steady stream of such deals, and the discounting habit it drove never moved the win rate. Then it interviewed lost buyers and found a perceived reliability gap on larger accounts; price was the polite exit line, and the discounting had been treating a symptom. Interview before you discount.

**Conversation intelligence made verbatim capture ordinary (Gong, Chorus; 2016 onward).** Gong and Chorus turned call recording, transcription, and topic tagging into an operational default. With any of them you can ask which objection shows up most in lost deals and get verbatim quotes back with their sources, which is the capability this pattern runs on.

**Confidential reporting bought honest accounts in aviation (NASA, 1976 onward).** NASA and the FAA set up the Aviation Safety Reporting System in 1976 so pilots, controllers, mechanics, dispatchers, and cabin crew file safety reports to NASA rather than to the regulator, and NASA de-identifies every report before it analyzes or shares anything. The FAA's advisory circular AC 00-46F gives a reporter protection against certificate action and civil penalty in exchange for the information. NASA has collected over two million reports since. The design is the one a deal review needs. The person who saw what happened is the person least able to say so when saying so costs them, so take the cost off the table before you ask. Aviation bought its candor with confidentiality and immunity, and it has paid that price every year for half a century.

**The blameless review, borrowed from software operations (Etsy 2012, Google 2016).** John Allspaw's 2012 post on Etsy's engineering blog, "Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture," took the just-culture idea from the safety researcher Sidney Dekker: a review that punishes whoever reports a mistake buys a story instead of an account. The postmortem chapter of Google's *Site Reliability Engineering* (O'Reilly, 2016) supplies the mechanics, with reviews that fire on agreed conditions, get written while the incident is fresh, and get read by peers. It transfers to a campaign because honest reporting requires safety from punishment. I've watched the alternative: a campaign review opened with a leader asking who approved the spend, and the targeting call that caused the miss never came up.

**The won-deal interview that surfaced the real value proposition.** In my own operating experience, the most useful win-loss work I ever commissioned was on deals we'd already won. We assumed customers bought us for the feature we led every demo with; the interviews kept returning to something we treated as a footnote, the way our onboarding got a team live in days rather than weeks. Buyers named that as the reason they chose us, so we rebuilt the pitch around what the wins said.
