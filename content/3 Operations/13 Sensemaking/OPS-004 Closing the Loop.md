> *A loop is only closed when an insight changes a decision and the person who gave you the insight hears back.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A market insight closes its loop only when it changes a decision on a reliable cadence and the person who supplied it hears what happened; that return trip carries field evidence into company action. It sits below [[OPS-001 Marketing as Sensemaking]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't let an insight die in a research folder. A read that never crosses into a decision leaves the roadmap, the message, and the target list running on last year's assumptions while the field keeps telling you something new.

Gathering the signal is the easy half. Calls get recorded, win-loss notes get written, tickets pile up with the same complaint stated forty ways, and someone reads all of it and forms a clear view.

Turning an insight into a changed decision crosses a boundary between teams, and no one whose name is on a goal owns that crossing. The person who heard the signal has no authority over the roadmap; the person who owns the roadmap never heard the signal. The read stays true and stays inert.

The cost compounds quietly. Your customers keep asking for the same thing and keep watching it not happen, so they stop asking, and your richest source of signal goes dark because the last three suggestions vanished without a reply. An insight that never changes a decision is a wasted loop, and a company that collects feedback it never acts on trains its own market to stop talking. Meanwhile product builds from a roadmap set before the field learned what it now knows, marketing runs a message that win-loss review already contradicted, and sales targets a persona the last two quarters disproved.

The obvious responses fail in a recognizable way. More intake thickens the folder while the decisions stay the same. A quarterly "voice of customer" readout that no one is required to act on produces a slide, then silence. Asking every team to "be more customer-driven" spreads the responsibility so thin it lands nowhere.

## Complications

- **Insight and authority sit in different chairs.** The person who hears the market usually can't change the roadmap, and the person who owns the roadmap usually didn't hear the market, so the handoff crosses an org boundary no single goal spans.
- **Acting on signal is slower than collecting it.** Capturing feedback is cheap and immediate; changing a plan is expensive and contested, so under deadline pressure the intake keeps running while the action keeps slipping.
- **Silence looks free but isn't.** Not replying to a customer's feedback costs nothing this week and feels efficient, yet it teaches your best informants that talking to you is pointless and the signal quietly dries up.
- **A cadence competes with the calendar it lives in.** A standing review is easy to skip when a launch is burning, so without a fixed rhythm the loop closes only when someone remembers.
- **Every function wants the insight pointed at its own plan.** Product, sales, and marketing each read the same signal as an argument for what they already intended, so an insight with no neutral owner gets absorbed into existing bias.
- **A closed loop is harder to count than a full funnel.** "We shipped three things customers asked for and told them" is real but unglamorous next to lead volume, so the discipline gets starved of attention because its output resists a vanity number.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Give one person standing ownership of the return trip, run the handoff on a fixed weekly, monthly, and quarterly cadence, and treat the loop as closed only when an insight has changed a specific decision in product, positioning, targeting, or the plan and the customers who raised it have been told it shipped.

1. **Name the bridge.** Assign someone, often a Product Marketing Manager, as the permanent connection between go-to-market and product and engineering, and make their job the loop's existence: they analyze customer conversations, spot the pattern across deals and tickets, and route the insight to the team that can act on it. Put a name on the role and carry it on the calendar rather than doing it as a favor when someone has time. Measure the bridge on whether decisions actually move, and the insight stops dying at the boundary that used to swallow it. The deal-level lessons this person carries come from [[OPS-002 Win-Loss Discipline]]; their work is to make those lessons land somewhere they change what the company builds and says.
2. **Put the handoff on a clock.** A loop that closes when someone remembers closes rarely, so make the cadence structural. Tie the rhythm to a layered operating cycle: a weekly pass to surface fresh signal, a monthly review where insight meets the plan, and a quarterly retrospective on business strategy where win-loss patterns reshape the ICP, the messaging, and even org and hiring decisions. Both the monthly beat and the quarterly reset belong to [[OPS-019 Quarterly Recalibration]]; make those meetings carry an agenda item no one is allowed to skip, namely which field insight changed which decision since last time. When a review can't answer that, treat the loop as open and the meeting as unfinished.
3. **Close the loop all the way to the customer.** The phrase belongs to Fred Reichheld, whose *The Ultimate Question* (2006) put Net Promoter into general use and made closed-loop feedback a required part of the system: you score the customer, then you go back to the ones who spoke and tell them what happened. Close it with the people who gave you the feedback: when you ship something because a customer asked, tell your team and tell that customer. The reply keeps the signal flowing, because a customer who watches a suggestion turn into a shipped feature learns that talking to you pays, and they bring you the next one. Stop at the roadmap and the loop is half-closed; it improves the product and starves the relationship that produced the improvement. Run the full circuit from the field to a decision and back to the person who spoke.

## Implications

Close the loop and you turn your market read into an operating input instead of an archive. Let the field bend strategy: win-loss patterns flow into who you target, product usage flows into what you build next, and the exact language buyers use flows into how you position. Expect the relationship with customers to change too, because feedback that visibly ships makes customers into willing informants who bring you signal you'd never have gone looking for. This is the mechanism that lets [[OPS-003 Feedback Before Scale]] work at all; validating a motion before you spend on it only means something if the validation can still change the plan, so keep that path open once the spending starts.

Accept the tension: a closed loop asks the company to change its mind on a schedule, and changing your mind is expensive and uncomfortable. A roadmap that answers to the field is less predictable than one set once a year and defended; a message that moves with win-loss unsettles a team that just learned the old one. Guard against the cadence hardening into ritual, where a monthly meeting reviews insight, files it, and changes nothing, which reopens the loop while looking like it closed it. Hold the discipline to its test: every cycle must name a decision that moved and a customer who heard back. When it can't, sensemaking has quietly reverted to collecting research, so use the quarterly reset of [[OPS-019 Quarterly Recalibration]] to catch and name that failure.

In a well-tuned B2B operation, a Product Marketing Manager holds the bridge role between go-to-market and product. The PMM sits on sales calls, reads the win-loss notes, and catches when the same objection or feature request recurs across enough deals to be a pattern rather than a one-off. That pattern goes to product as a specific, evidenced ask, and when the fix ships, the PMM tells the customers who raised it directly. That last step, the reply to the customer, is what compounds: it builds trust and pulls more feedback in, so the loop gets richer each turn instead of drying up.

The whole discipline runs off a quarterly retrospective on business strategy, where the review treats win-loss data as an input to the plan rather than a report to file. Structure the review so an insight has to land against a decision or the meeting isn't finished. Where companies do this, the pattern in lost deals reshaped the ideal customer profile, corrected the messaging that kept losing to a competitor, and in the sharper cases changed org and hiring decisions. That structure is what separated a strategy that answered to the market from one that answered to last year's assumptions.

## Examples

**LEGO paying the customer whose suggestion shipped (2008 onward).** LEGO built the return trip into the mechanism. A fan posts a design. A project that reaches 10,000 supporters goes to a LEGO review board, which decides whether to make it, and LEGO publishes the verdict either way. A fan whose design becomes a set receives 1% of the product's net sales. The program began as LEGO Cuusoo in Japan in 2008 and was renamed LEGO Ideas in 2014. Each part of the circuit is named and public: the threshold that triggers the review, the body that decides, the answer the customer gets, and a payment that proves their insight reached a decision.

**Failing to close it, and paying for it.** In one of my own operating stretches, we ran a genuinely good listening operation and still lost the benefit, because we had no one who owned the return trip. We captured the calls, we wrote the notes, and the insight pooled in a shared doc that product never treated as a source of requirements. Customers who had asked for the same capability three quarters running stopped raising it, and I later learned they'd read our silence as a decision. The fix was unglamorous: one person owned carrying the pattern to the roadmap, a monthly slot where that handoff had to produce a named decision, and a habit of writing back to the customer when something shipped. Nothing about our listening changed; what changed was that the listening finally reached a decision and the customer heard about it.
