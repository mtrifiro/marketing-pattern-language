> *A form with seven blanks and no room to hedge: fill it honestly and you'll discover which decisions your company hasn't made.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A fixed positioning template turns vague prose into blanks that demand commitments; the exercise exposes postponed strategic decisions and leaves the finished statement inside the company as a working instrument. It sits below [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]] and [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't write the positioning statement as a paragraph. Prose lets a team name three target customers and two categories without anyone noticing, and the disagreement survives into every asset the team ships for the next two years.

Ask five people in a company to describe what it sells and you'll get five answers that everyone present will call consistent. The founder describes a shift in how work gets done. The head of sales describes the buyer who signs. Product describes the capability. Marketing describes the category. Each answer is true, each is partial, and because they're phrased as prose, the gaps between them stay invisible. Nobody has said anything another person would contradict, so the meeting ends with a shared feeling of agreement and no agreement.

The gaps surface later, expensively. Six months on, the website targets a persona the sales team never calls, the pitch deck names a category the pricing contradicts, and a campaign gets built for buyers who were never going to clear procurement. Each artifact is internally coherent and they don't fit together, because they were derived from four different unstated positions rather than from one stated one. The cost is that nothing compounds. A market learns a position through repetition, and a company that ships four positions gets no repetition at all.

A template changes what a conversation can hide. Geoffrey Moore published the version most teams still use in *Crossing the Chasm* (1991): for a target customer, who has a need or opportunity, the product is a category member that delivers a key benefit, and unlike a primary competitive alternative, it does something specifically different. Seven blanks, each demanding a singular answer. The form's value is that it can't absorb a hedge. Write two target customers into the first blank and the sentence visibly breaks. Leave the "unlike" blank empty and the omission sits there on the page where a room full of people can see it.

The blanks aren't equally uncomfortable. The one teams fight hardest to avoid is the competitive alternative, because naming it means conceding that a buyer has somewhere else to go and that the somewhere else is often good enough. The second hardest is the target customer, because a singular answer forecloses revenue the company would like to believe is available. Those two blanks are where most of the value sits, which follows: they're the blanks that force a decision rather than record one.

The obvious response is to fill the form loosely and move on. A statement reading "for modern enterprises who want to move faster" has technically been completed, and it commits to nothing, so it constrains nothing downstream and the four positions carry on undisturbed. A loosely filled template is worse than none at all, because the team now believes the question is settled and stops asking it.

## Complications

- **A singular target forecloses revenue you can currently see.** Naming one customer means writing off buyers who would have paid, and the write-off is concrete while the focus it buys is speculative.
- **Naming the competitive alternative concedes it's viable.** A team would rather believe it has no real alternative, and the blank asks it to write down the thing buyers actually choose instead.
- **The template produces a bad sentence on purpose.** Filled correctly it reads as a stiff, unpublishable construction, which tempts a team to smooth it into prose and lose the constraint that made it useful.
- **The form can be gamed with abstraction.** Every blank accepts a vague filler, and a statement made entirely of fillers passes the exercise while settling nothing.
- **Positions have to hold still to work and markets move.** A statement rewritten each quarter teaches the market nothing, and one never revisited eventually describes a company that no longer exists.
- **The people who must agree don't share a vocabulary.** Sales thinks in buyers, product in capabilities, finance in segments, and the template asks them to commit to one phrasing that will read as a loss to at least two of them.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Fill every blank with exactly one answer, and treat any blank that takes more than one as an unmade decision rather than a wording problem: a positioning statement with two target customers or two categories is a report that the company hasn't chosen yet.

The instrument only works under that rule. The template's purpose is diagnostic before it's expressive, and its output on the first pass is usually a list of the arguments the company has been avoiding. Run it in a room with the people who can settle those arguments, and expect the session to be about the two hard blanks rather than about wording.

What separates a working statement from a completed form:

1. **The target blank names a buyer specific enough to exclude someone.** If nobody is excluded, the blank is empty regardless of what's written in it. This is the craft face of [[MS-010 ICP Definition]], and the archetypes that populate it belong to [[MS-009 Buyer Archetypes]].
2. **The alternative blank names what buyers actually do instead**, which is frequently the status quo, a spreadsheet, or an internal build rather than a vendor. [[MS-025 Competitive Geography]] owns the full map of those alternatives; the statement picks one from it.
3. **The category blank commits to a shelf.** A product with no stated category gets assigned one by the market, and the assignment is usually the cheapest available reading; [[MS-005 Category Before Product]] owns why.
4. **The differentiation blank survives a competitor reading it.** Fill it with something a rival could claim verbatim and it's decoration, which is the test [[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]] applies to every claim.

The statement then stays internal. It's a constraint that outward copy must satisfy rather than copy itself; [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]] owns the sentence a stranger actually reads, and it's derived from this form rather than lifted out of it.

## Implications

A filled template turns most later marketing arguments into questions of fact. A proposed campaign either serves the named target or it doesn't; a headline either supports the stated difference or introduces a new one. Debates that used to run on taste now run on consistency with a document everyone signed, which is faster and much less personal.

It also gives you a dated record of what you believed. When results contradict the position, having written it down is what lets you tell a wrong position from bad execution, and that distinction is nearly impossible to make afterward from memory. Revisit the statement on a fixed schedule rather than continuously; a position needs long enough in market to be learned before its results mean anything. The first move is to fill the blanks alone, then have three colleagues do the same separately, then compare: the blanks where your answers differ are your real agenda.

## Examples

**Superhuman narrowed the target blank and the score moved (2018).** Rahul Vohra published Superhuman's method in First Round Review, built on Sean Ellis's benchmark that a product has product-market fit when 40 percent of users say they'd be very disappointed to lose it. Superhuman's overall score was 22 percent. Rather than change the product, Vohra segmented the responses and measured only the highest-expectation group the team had identified, and the figure moved to 32 percent. The product was identical before and after; the only thing that changed was which customer the company was answering for. That's the target blank doing its work, and it's the cleanest available demonstration that the blank is a measurement decision as much as a marketing one.

**Southwest Airlines held the category and the "unlike" for decades.** Southwest flew its first routes in June 1971 with an unusually narrow position: short-haul point-to-point flights, one aircraft type, no assigned seats, no meals, no interline baggage agreements. Each of those is a refusal, and each maps onto a blank. The category was a low-fare point-to-point carrier rather than an airline in general; the alternative was the full-service hub-and-spoke carrier; the difference was cost structure rather than service. Competitors repeatedly launched imitators and abandoned them, because copying the position meant accepting the refusals, and an incumbent with hubs, meals and mixed fleets couldn't. A position that costs a rival something to copy is the version worth having.

**Volvo committed the differentiation blank to one word and kept it.** Volvo engineer Nils Bohlin developed the modern three-point seatbelt, introduced in Volvo cars in 1959, and the company made the patent available to other manufacturers rather than holding it as an advantage. The commercial logic reads oddly until you look at the positioning: Volvo's stated difference was safety as an institutional commitment, and giving away the most important safety invention it had made was the strongest possible evidence for that claim. Decades of subsequent marketing inherited a single differentiation blank filled once and defended. The cost was equally real, since a company known for safety spent years being described as sensible and dull, which is what a genuinely singular position feels like from inside.
