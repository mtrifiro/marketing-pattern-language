> *The name is the piece of positioning you'll repeat a million times, so it had better argue your case while you sleep.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A name chooses the category, comparison, and mental slot before positioning copy can explain them; every repetition carries part of the argument. It works with [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]] and [[MS-023 Category Creation]], which supply the idea and category a name compresses.

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat a name as a label to attach once the real work is done. The name reaches the buyer before the argument does and decides what they think they're looking at.

The name is the piece of positioning repeated more than any headline, deck, or campaign, and it argues for or against the strategy every time it's spoken.

At most companies the name gets chosen last. The product is built, the positioning is written, the launch date is set, and then someone opens a spreadsheet of candidates the week before the announcement. The debate runs on taste ("does it sound cool?") and availability ("is the domain taken?"), rarely on what the word claims. The winner is whatever nobody hates. Then that word goes onto the website, into every sales call, across the docs, into the press release, and out of the mouth of every customer who recommends you. It gets said hundreds of thousands of times. A headline you'll rewrite next quarter got a month of debate; the word you can never take back got an afternoon.

A weak name makes every other piece of communication work harder to overcome it. A product sold to enterprises under a name that sounds like a weekend hobby project spends its messaging energy correcting the impression the name already planted. A new category named in the incumbent's vocabulary concedes the comparison before anyone speaks: the buyer files it as a cheaper version of what they already know. A name sets the reference class. Once the buyer decides what kind of thing you are, they judge you against that class, and the name made that decision for them. Al Ries and Jack Trout gave this a chapter in *Positioning* (1981), "The Power of the Name," plus three more on the traps a name walks into: the no-name trap, the free-ride trap, the line-extension trap. They wrote for brands competing for space in a crowded mind. A company inventing a category has the harder job, because no shelf exists yet and the name has to build one.

Amateur naming shows up in a few recognizable forms. The internal-jargon name ships a term that made sense in engineering ("the orchestration layer," "the sync engine") to buyers who've never sat in that standup. The empty-evocative name is a coined word with no meaning, which takes years of paid advertising before it means anything, a bet most companies can't fund. The me-too name borrows the category leader's cadence and guarantees the product reads as the imitation. The over-descriptive name is perfectly clear today and boxes you in tomorrow, because "PocketFax" describes exactly the product you'll want to outgrow.

Names carry visible weight whenever a company pays to change one. Renames are expensive: you rebuild recognition, redirect traffic, reprint everything, and absorb months of "wait, what's it called now?" Companies swallow that cost precisely because the old name was pulling the positioning in the wrong direction, and no amount of clever copy could out-argue a word the market repeated every day. If a name were only a label, nobody would spend seven figures to swap one. They spend it because the label was doing strategy, badly.

A name also compresses your [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]] into one or two words, the hardest compression in marketing. A name that lands does the arguing for you in rooms you're not in. A name that misses makes you the vendor whose product is good "once you understand what it actually does," a sentence no growing company can afford to have attached to it.

## Complications

- **Clarity versus room to grow.** A descriptive name explains itself on first contact and reassures a cautious buyer, yet it nails you to a definition you may want to escape once the product expands past its first use.
- **Meaning versus ownability.** A common word telegraphs value instantly but is generic and hard to protect; a coined word is yours alone but means nothing until you spend years and dollars teaching the market.
- **The buyer's vocabulary versus your own.** The words your team uses internally feel precise to you and foreign to the buyer, and a name in your dialect makes the customer translate before they can care.
- **The name sets the comparison.** Like [[MS-018 Price Creates the Buyer]], a name selects the reference class: it decides which shelf the buyer files you on, and therefore which competitors they weigh you against.
- **Frequency compounds every choice.** A name is repeated far more than any other asset, so a small wrongness is a small cost paid hundreds of thousands of times.
- **Permanence versus revisability.** Almost everything in marketing can be edited next week, but a name accrues equity the moment it ships, which makes it the one decision that gets more expensive to change with every passing day.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Choose the name before you lock the positioning-facing copy, and choose it to assert the category and comparison you want, because the name is repeated more than any message and it files you in the buyer's mind whether you've aimed it or not.

Treat the name as an argument the market will repeat for you. Before you pick one, decide what mental slot you want the buyer to put you in and what you want them to compare you against, then pick the name that puts you there. Extending a known category? Use a name in the buyer's existing vocabulary to lower the cost of understanding you. Creating a category? Make the name carry a claim the incumbent's words can't, and name the new thing on its own terms rather than borrowing the old ones, which is the naming half of [[MS-023 Category Creation]].

The mechanism holds across products, features, and categories, and across every medium the name travels through. Marty Neumeier's *The Brand Gap* (2003) lists seven tests a candidate should pass: distinctiveness, brevity, appropriateness, easy spelling and pronunciation, likability, extendability, protectability. Those seven test whether a name will function; the three below test what it claims, which is the question that decides who you get compared against. Make a good name do three things at once:

1. **Compress your [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]]** into a form small enough to survive being repeated by strangers.
2. **Read as concrete rather than abstract,** because a specific-sounding name inherits the credibility that [[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]] always earns over vague language.
3. **Set the comparison you can win,** by naming a dimension where you're strong instead of one where the leader already owns the word.

Let the permanence of names dictate the process. Spend disproportionate care up front, because this is the decision you'll pay the most to reverse. Test the candidate the way you test a message: say it aloud, put it in a sentence a customer would say to a colleague, hand it to someone outside the building and watch whether they guess the right category. A name a stranger can place correctly on first hearing is doing its positioning work; a name that needs a paragraph of explanation quietly taxes every asset downstream. Don't hunt for a word that sounds impressive in the boardroom. Find the word that argues your case in the thousand rooms you'll never enter.

## Implications

Choose a name as positioning and every downstream asset gets a head start. Your [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]] has a handle to hang on, your headlines can assume the category rather than establish it, and your concrete proof points ([[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]]) land against a claim the name already made. Sales calls open a step ahead, because the buyer arrives with the right reference class already loaded. The name becomes the smallest carrier of your strategy, and it keeps working in conversations, search results, and word of mouth long after a campaign ends.

Naming well sharpens the choice a category creator has to make, which feeds [[MS-023 Category Creation]] and [[MS-022 Positioning as Contrast]]: the name is where the decision to define new ground or inherit old ground becomes irreversible and public. Once the market repeats your name, you've committed to the comparison it implies, and reversing that commitment means a rename and its full bill.

Manage the tension between the name that's clearest today and the name that survives tomorrow. A descriptive name wins the first conversation and can lose the third, once the product has grown past the thing the name describes. An evocative name loses the first conversation and can own the market later, if you can fund the teaching. No formula dissolves the trade; naming forces a bet on how far the thing will travel, and you place the bet at the moment you have the least information and the most at stake.

## Examples

**Impossible Foods, "Impossible Burger" (2016).** When Impossible Foods launched its plant-based patty at David Chang's Momofuku Nishi in July 2016, the name asserted the category argument before anyone took a bite. A descriptive name ("PlantPatty," "SoyBurger") would have filed the product on the familiar veggie-burger shelf next to decades of compromise products. "Impossible" claimed the exact thing the product needed buyers to believe, that a burger made from plants could be indistinguishable from beef, a thing that was supposed to be impossible. By naming the claim rather than the ingredients, Impossible chose a comparison it could win (against real beef) instead of one it would lose (against other meat substitutes). The name and the product's core promise are the same sentence.

**Salesforce, "Einstein" for its AI layer (2016).** When Salesforce branded its machine-learning capabilities in a press release on September 19, 2016, a few weeks ahead of that year's Dreamforce (October 4 to 7, where the layer got its own keynote and a demo floor), it didn't ship "Salesforce AI Features" or "the Predictive Analytics Module." A generic label leaves an abstract, invisible technology feeling exactly that abstract to a business buyer. Salesforce named the layer Einstein, gave it a cartoon mascot, and made it a product with a face. The name made the technology feel concrete and approachable to business buyers who didn't want to think about model training, and it turned "AI" from a checkbox into a branded thing customers could ask for by name. I spent formative years at Salesforce, and the company's instinct for naming ran through everything: the "No Software" campaign with the red circle-slash, "Chatter," "Trailhead," "Dreamforce." Each name argued a position. Einstein said this AI is smart and on your side, precisely the reassurance a nervous first-time buyer of machine learning needed to hear before the sale.

**Apple, "Retina display" (2010).** At the iPhone 4 launch in June 2010, Apple didn't market the screen as "326 pixels per inch," a spec that means nothing to a shopper standing in a store. A number sold on its own is one the buyer can't tell apart from a rival's. Apple coined "Retina display," a name asserting that the pixels were denser than the human eye could resolve at normal distance. The name turned a number into a benefit and a benefit into a branded feature competitors couldn't claim without sounding like imitators. Rivals shipped screens with equal or higher pixel density for years afterward and still had no word for it, so shoppers kept asking whether the other phone had a "Retina" screen. That's the compounding return on a name that argues a position: once the market repeats your word, the comparison runs on your terms in every conversation you're not part of.
