> *Three claims, each nailed to something a stranger could check; a pillar with nothing under it is an opinion in a suit.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Message pillars are the three or four supports beneath the value proposition. Each addresses a distinct pain and carries proof a stranger can inspect. Together they give longer marketing enough depth to avoid a feature pile. It sits below [[CR-001 Atomic Value Prop]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't let a pillar stand without evidence under it. Three unsupported claims of value are three assertions a buyer has no reason to accept, and a competitor will publish the same three by the end of the quarter.

Most companies have pillars whether or not anyone wrote them down. Read a year of a company's blog posts, decks and campaigns and you'll find the same handful of claims recurring, arrived at by accretion rather than decision. The trouble with accreted pillars is that nobody audited them: two usually overlap, one is a restatement of the value proposition rather than a support for it, and at least one was true about the product two releases ago.

The pattern that produces this is a messaging workshop that ends at the claims. A team spends a day arguing toward "we're faster, we're safer, we're easier," writes the three on a slide, and disbands. Each claim is defensible in the room because everyone present knows the evidence, carries it in their heads, and never thinks to attach it. The slide ships to a writer who has no such context, and what reaches the market is the assertion with the evidence stripped out. The writer was handed claims and asked for copy.

The cost is that the pillars stop doing the one job pillars have. Their purpose is to let a company argue at greater length without drifting: a webinar, a landing page and a conference talk should all be arguing the same three things in different amounts of detail. Unsupported pillars can't do that, because there's nothing to go into detail about. The talk fills the time with adjacent material, the landing page fills it with features, and the three claims each acquire a slightly different meaning depending on who last wrote about them.

A second problem hides inside the count. Teams reach for a number of pillars that matches the number of things they'd like to be good at, which is usually five or six, and each additional pillar divides the attention available for the others. Pillars compete with each other for the same scarce belief. Beyond three or four the set stops being an argument and becomes a catalogue, which is the thing pillars existed to prevent.

The obvious response, adding proof after the fact, doesn't work in the direction people expect. A team that picks its claims first and then goes looking for evidence finds itself defending claims the evidence doesn't support, and the honest options at that point are to weaken the claim or to overstate the proof. Which of the two happens depends on whether anyone in the process is empowered to say the claim was wrong, and often nobody is.

## Complications

- **Every claim sounds strong until someone asks for the evidence.** A pillar can survive a year of internal use unchallenged, because the people repeating it already believe it and never test whether an outsider would.
- **Pillars compete for the same belief.** Each additional claim reduces the attention the others get, so a fifth pillar makes the first four weaker rather than making the argument fuller.
- **The strongest evidence is often the least flattering.** Publishing the specific limit or the honest comparison buys more credibility than a superlative does, and it also documents a weakness a competitor can quote.
- **Proof decays on its own schedule.** A benchmark, a customer count and a certification each age differently, so a set of pillars that was fully evidenced last year is partially evidenced now with no one having changed anything.
- **The pain-based claim and the capability-based claim feel identical inside the company.** Teams naturally phrase pillars as things the product does, while a buyer only weighs things that happen to them, and the translation gets skipped because insiders can't hear the difference.
- **Whoever owns the evidence isn't whoever owns the message.** The numbers live with product, support and finance; the claims live with marketing, and no routine connects them.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Hold the set to three or four pillars, each answering a different buyer pain, and require every pillar to carry at least one specific, dated, checkable proof point before it's allowed into the messaging; a pillar you can't evidence gets cut or downgraded to a claim you're working toward.

The rule that makes the set an argument rather than a list is that the evidence comes first and the phrasing second. Start from what you can actually demonstrate: the measured result, the named customer outcome, the published benchmark, the certification with its audit date, the architectural fact a technical buyer can verify. Then group that evidence by the buyer pain it resolves, and let the groupings tell you how many pillars you have. Companies that work this direction usually discover they have two well-evidenced pillars and a third they've been asserting for a year.

Four properties separate a working pillar from a slide bullet:

1. **It names a pain rather than a capability.** "Deploys without a maintenance window" is a capability; "you stop scheduling weekend releases" is what the buyer experiences. The pillar is the second one and the capability is its evidence.
2. **It doesn't overlap its neighbours.** Two pillars that a buyer would answer the same way are one pillar and a rewrite. Test by asking which objection each defeats; if two defeat the same objection, merge them.
3. **Its proof would be false if you weren't doing the thing.** This is the swap test [[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]] owns, applied one level down. Proof a competitor could publish verbatim proves nothing about you.
4. **Someone owns keeping the proof current, with a review date.** Pillars fail quietly, through evidence that expired rather than claims that were wrong.

The set then governs the assets rather than decorating them. Every long-form asset argues one pillar in depth, which is how this pattern and [[CR-007 One Message Per Asset]] work together: the pillars are the small number of permitted messages, and each surface takes exactly one.

## Implications

A team with evidenced pillars can brief a writer, a rep or an agency in an afternoon, because the argument and its supports travel together. It also gains an honest inventory of what it can't yet claim, which is a product and operations agenda rather than a marketing one: the pillar you want and can't evidence is a roadmap item with a marketing deadline attached.

Expect the exercise to shrink the set. Most companies enter with five or six claims and leave with three, and the two that fall away are usually the ones every competitor was also making. The first move is to list your current claims, then write the single best piece of evidence under each one from memory; the claims where you can't fill the line are the ones the market has been discounting all along.

## Examples

**Amazon made three retail claims measurable (2003).** Amazon's 2003 annual report states its business strategy as low prices, convenience, and wide selection. The report gives a buyer a way to inspect each claim: prices can be compared, convenience includes fast and reliable fulfillment plus delivery-date estimates, and selection is visible in the catalogue. The same filing describes millions of items offered by Amazon and third-party sellers. Each pillar stays at the buyer's level, and the operating detail supplies the proof.

**Patagonia pinned its pillar to factory-level disclosure (2007).** Environmental and social responsibility is the claim most apparel companies make and the one buyers most heavily discount, because every brand asserts it and almost none makes it checkable. Patagonia launched the Footprint Chronicles in 2007, publishing supply chain detail down to individual factories and mills, including the parts that didn't flatter the company. The pillar didn't change; the evidence under it did, and it moved from an assertion any competitor could copy into a public record a competitor would have to match by actually opening its own supply chain. The unflattering material is what makes it work, since a disclosure that only contained good news would read as marketing rather than as evidence.

**GitLab published the handbook as the proof (2015 onward).** GitLab's claim to run transparently is the kind of cultural assertion that normally has nothing underneath it. The company's answer was to publish its internal handbook openly on the web, covering operating procedures, compensation approach, and how decisions get made, running to thousands of pages and revised continuously in public. A buyer or a candidate evaluating the claim doesn't have to take a statement on trust; they read the artifact. What makes it a proof point rather than a content programme is that the cost of faking it is prohibitive: a company that published a handbook it didn't follow would be contradicted by its own employees within a quarter.
