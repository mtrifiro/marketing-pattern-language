> *A new-category buyer must assemble a purchase no one has shown them how to make.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A buyer in a new category must assemble a purchase process from scratch: what to evaluate, whom to involve, and how to defend the decision internally. Silence often means that internal path never formed. It sits below [[CR-011 Buyer's Journey Narrative]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't answer a stalled deal in a new category by piling on more proof that the product is good. The obstacle is that nobody has shown the buyer whose budget this comes from, who signs, or what to call it.

An enthusiastic buyer with no purchase path stalls as surely as a doubtful one, and from the outside the two look identical.

Every established category comes with a purchase script the buyer already knows. A company buying a CRM in 2020 knew the moves: pull the analyst grid, shortlist three vendors, run a bake-off, loop in security and procurement, negotiate seats. That buyer had bought this kind of thing before, or worked next to someone who had, so the process was ambient. You could spend the whole conversation on why your product wins, because the how of buying was settled.

A new category has no script. The first time a company bought a CRM, in the late 1990s, there was no shortlist to pull, no comparison grid, no budget line called "sales software," and no title on the org chart who obviously owned the decision. Wanting the thing was necessary but not sufficient. The buyer had to invent, from scratch, the internal process of acquiring it: which team owns it, who signs, what it gets measured against, how to defend it when the CFO asks why the old way isn't fine. That invention is work the buyer has never done, so most buyers don't do it. They go quiet. Deals in a new category die in silence, with the buyer having never found the path to a yes.

The seller's reflex when a promising deal stalls is more proof that the product is good: another case study, a sharper demo, a better price. None of it moves a buyer stuck on how rather than on whether. The buyer already believes. What they lack is a purchase path, a way to translate private conviction into a company decision, complete with the budget line, the approvers, and the story they'll tell to justify it. More product proof answers a question they've stopped asking.

Buyers stuck this way say recognizable things. "I love this, but I don't know where the budget would come from." "Who else does this? How did they roll it out?" "How do I explain this to my boss?" "What does a typical deployment look like?" Each one asks for the purchase script rather than the product pitch. The buyer is asking to be taught how people like them buy things like this, and a seller who hears it as a stalling tactic loses a deal already won on the merits.

Amateur work in a new category bolts a beautiful product story onto a buying process the buyer is expected to already know. The website explains the value with real craft, the demo lands, and then the call to action is "Talk to sales" or "Start your trial," as if the buyer knows what happens next, what it should cost, whom to bring, and how to get it approved. In a settled market that's fine, because the buyer fills the gap from experience. In a new market the gap is the whole deal, and a category creator that leaves it unfilled loses to inertia more often than to a competitor.

## Complications

- **Conviction without a path.** A buyer can want the product completely and still be unable to buy it, because wanting and knowing-how-to-purchase are separate capacities, and the second is missing in a new category.
- **The seller knows the process; the buyer doesn't.** You've watched a hundred companies adopt this. The buyer is doing it once, for the first time, with no template, so the knowledge gap runs entirely one way.
- **Teaching reads as slowing down.** Handing the buyer a purchase process feels like adding steps to a sale you'd rather speed up, yet skipping the teaching is what makes the deal stall indefinitely.
- **The champion has to sell internally.** The person who believes rarely holds the budget or the signature, so they have to re-sell your product to people you'll never meet, using materials you either give them or leave them to invent (the split between user and approver is developed in [[CR-011 Buyer's Journey Narrative]]).
- **No reference class to point at.** In an established market the buyer copies peers; in a new one there are few peers to copy, so "everyone's doing it" isn't available as a shortcut yet.
- **Too little teaching stalls them; too much patronizes.** A buyer handed a condescending "how to buy software" tutorial disengages as fast as one handed nothing.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Make the purchase process itself part of what you market, teaching the buyer whom to involve, what to measure, how much to budget, and how to justify the decision internally, so a buyer who already wants the product can see exactly how to acquire it.

You've run this process many times and the buyer is running it once. You've watched dozens or hundreds of companies adopt this category. You know how a good rollout goes, the objections the CFO raises, the team that should own it, the metric that proves it worked, the order the pieces go in. That knowledge is an asset, and in a new market it's often worth more to the buyer than another feature. Package it and give it away:

1. **The reference deployment.** Show what a realistic rollout looks like, in order.
2. **The sample business case.** Hand over a filled-in one instead of assuming the buyer can construct it.
3. **The internal memo the champion can forward.** Write their internal justification for them, because the champion who forwards a ready-made memo closes faster than one asked to write it from a blank page during their day job.
4. **The stakeholder map.** Name the roles to bring and the question each one will ask, instead of assuming the buyer already knows who signs.

Deliver the teaching in the buyer's frame rather than the seller's. A "buyer's guide" that's really a disguised product brochure teaches nothing, and buyers see through it instantly. Write the version that reads like advice from someone who's done this many times and has no stake in which vendor you pick: here's how companies like yours typically evaluate this, here's what a realistic budget looks like, here's the mistake most first-time buyers make. Hold that posture, the experienced guide rather than the eager salesperson, because it's what makes the instruction land and why teaching the purchase is a craft discipline rather than a sales tactic. Teach honestly and you earn the right to be the vendor the buyer trusts to have taught them.

## Implications

A buyer you've taught to buy can move without you in the room, which is the point: they can carry the decision to people you'll never meet and win it there. That unlocks the internal spread a new category depends on, because a champion armed with a business case and a stakeholder map becomes an extension of your sales team inside an account you can't otherwise reach. Build [[CR-015 Demo Script]] on this ground, since a demo that teaches the product only converts if the buyer also knows how to purchase what the demo showed. And feed the larger arc in [[CR-011 Buyer's Journey Narrative]] by supplying the "how do I actually get this" leg it frames.

Now manage the tension: teaching the purchase takes real effort per buyer and doesn't scale the way a landing page does. Decide how much of the buying process to productize into content that teaches many buyers at once, and how much to deliver by hand to the deals worth it. Teach too generically and the instruction is too thin to move anyone; teach too bespoke and you can only afford it for a handful of accounts. Work that trade-off continuously, and watch it ease as the category matures and the purchase script, once you've taught enough buyers, starts to become ambient on its own.

## Examples

**Salesforce and "No Software" as a buying lesson (1999).** When Salesforce launched in 1999, the buyer had no script for renting business software over the web; the buying process for enterprise applications assumed a server purchase, an IT project, and a multi-year implementation. Marc Benioff's "No Software" campaign, the red circle with the word "software" struck through, usually reads as positioning, and it is, but it also taught the purchase. It told the buyer that acquiring this didn't require the capital request, the hardware order, or the IT deployment they'd been trained to expect, which meant a sales VP could buy it on a credit card and a departmental budget without routing through the process that governed real "software." Salesforce spent years teaching buyers that the purchase itself was a subscription they could start small and grow. Teaching the new purchase meant a line manager could say yes without knowing how to run an enterprise software procurement, which no feature alone would have unlocked.

**Slack teaching the org how to buy a bottom-up tool (2015).** Slack spread by adoption before purchase: a team started on the free tier, then another, until the company ran on it without anyone having "bought" it. That created a problem around 2015: the person who eventually had to purchase, an IT or finance leader, had never bought a tool that arrived from the bottom up and was already in use. Slack taught that exact purchase. Its content and sales materials walked the buyer through consolidating scattered team usage into a company plan, made the internal business case explicit (here's the usage you already have, here's what a paid tier adds in security and administration), and gave the champion the numbers to justify formalizing what employees had already adopted. The teaching wasn't about whether Slack was good; the employees had settled that. It was about how an organization purchases something that grew inside it before anyone approved it.

**Intercom teaching a category it had to name first (2011).** When Intercom launched in 2011, "in-app customer messaging" wasn't a budget line, and a buyer couldn't shortlist vendors for a category that didn't exist. Intercom's content, its blog and books on customer communication, taught buyers how to think about the problem and, downstream, how to buy a solution to it: which team should own customer messaging, what to measure it against, how it differed from the support-ticket and email-marketing budgets a buyer might otherwise draw from. Teaching the buyer to place the purchase, to know whose budget it comes from and what job it does, made Intercom buyable in a category the buyer had to be taught existed. The instruction created the purchase path at the same time it created the demand.
