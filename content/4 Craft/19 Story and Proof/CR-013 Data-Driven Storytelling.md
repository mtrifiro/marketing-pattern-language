> *A number you gathered yourself is a story no competitor can tell.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Original data becomes the spine of a story when its finding creates the argument and carries it. The company's own dataset supplies both plot and proof. Competitors lack the material to tell the same story. It sits below [[CR-011 Buyer's Journey Narrative]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't reach for a statistic to dress up a claim you'd make anyway. A number anyone can look up persuades nobody, because the competitor arguing the opposite has a survey too.

The figures that move a buyer are the ones the buyer can't get anywhere else, and they arrive with the story built around them rather than sprinkled on top.

The garnish move is everywhere. A blog post argues that remote work is here to stay, then drops in "73% of executives agree" from a survey the writer read that morning. Deleting the number leaves the sentence saying the same thing; the stat was ornamental. Buyers have learned to skim past this kind of figure the way they skim past stock photography, because they've seen a thousand claims propped up by a borrowed percentage that could support the opposite argument from a different survey. A statistic bolted onto an assertion only makes it look like the writer went looking for cover.

Borrowed data has a deeper defect: everybody has it. A case for a new category resting on a Gartner projection rests where every competitor who read the same report is already standing. The number confers no advantage because it isn't yours. Worse, the buyer often already knows it, so the credibility goes into repeating what they could have looked up. Third-party research works as context, as the backdrop a finding sits against, and stops working the moment it has to be the finding.

The opposite failure is the data dump. A company commissions real research, gets back a rich dataset, and publishes all of it: forty charts, an interactive dashboard, a PDF nobody opens past page three. The team confused having data with telling a story. Raw data is inert. The reader doesn't want your spreadsheet; they want the one thing in it that changes how they see their own situation. Every number presented with equal weight means none of them lands, and the gathering effort goes into a document that reads like a lab notebook.

Original research is the content most likely to earn links, citations, and press pickup, because a journalist can't get your number anywhere else, and a competitor can't reprint it without pointing back at you. The finding becomes the headline, and the headline carries your name. Andy Crestodina, who wrote *Content Chemistry* and runs Orbit Media's annual blogger survey, prices original research at roughly ten times the effort of an ordinary post for something like a hundred times the return, and Brian Dean reports that the charts inside Backlinko's research studies drew two to three times the links, because other writers embed a chart and cite its source. A new fact is more quotable than a new opinion. A story built on data you own works for years, because the number stays scarce.

## Complications

- **Original data is slow and expensive; borrowed data is free and instant.** Gathering your own numbers means a survey, an analysis of your product's usage, or a commissioned study; citing someone else's takes a search.
- **A finding you don't control might not flatter you.** Real data occasionally contradicts the pitch. Honest storytelling means publishing the number even when it complicates the story, which is exactly what makes it believable.
- **Numbers feel objective, but a story needs an arc.** Data presented flat bores the reader, and data shaped too hard distorts what it measured.
- **One vivid figure sticks; twenty compete.** A single memorable number does more work than a comprehensive report. Concrete detail is the credibility mechanism ([[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]]), yet the pull is always toward showing everything you measured.
- **The method must be defensible and legible at once.** A finding a skeptic can attack collapses; a finding only a statistician can follow never travels.
- **Data ages.** A number you publish is true as of a date, and the world moves. A story built on a finding inherits an expiry the story built on a principle doesn't.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Build the piece around one original finding you gathered or commissioned, and let that finding carry the argument, so the story couldn't exist without the data and the data couldn't be lifted by a competitor.

1. **Start from a question only your data can answer.** You sit on things no one else has: how your customers actually use the product, what your platform sees across thousands of accounts, what a study you paid for reveals about a problem your buyer feels. Ask the question whose answer would change how the buyer sees their situation, then go get the answer and make it the protagonist. The number is the claim itself, and the prose around it exists to set it up and spell out what it means.
2. **Give the finding an arc.** Original data has a natural three-beat structure when you look for it: the question the buyer already half-holds, the result that turns out larger or stranger than expected, and the implication for the person reading. Write the setup so the reader wants the number before you deliver it, deliver it plainly, then show what it forces them to reconsider. The data becomes a chapter in [[CR-011 Buyer's Journey Narrative]] rather than a slide bolted onto the deck.
3. **Show the method just enough to make the number defensible, and no more.** State the sample, the source, and the date, so a skeptic can weigh it and a journalist can cite it, then get out of the way. Lead with the one headline figure; keep the supporting data beneath it for the reader who digs, never on top of it.
4. **Report the baseline with the result.** Without a starting point, the after has no size. "Reduced onboarding time" tells the reader nothing; "cut onboarding from three weeks to four days" lets them hold it against their own three-week problem. The pair is what makes either number credible, so publish the before even when it embarrasses the customer who agreed to be featured, and trace the mechanism between the two, because a result with no visible cause can't be reproduced in anyone else's shop.

Before you publish, test the finding on someone outside the team; if they can repeat it at dinner and a competitor can't reproduce it without crediting you, ship it.

## Implications

A pattern built on original data feeds the rest of the narrative craft. Give [[CR-011 Buyer's Journey Narrative]] its proof at each turn, so the arc gets demonstrated rather than asserted. Hand [[CR-014 Social Proof Architecture]] the figures it places where doubt occurs, and draw your persuasive force from [[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]], the pattern that explains why a precise, sourced number reads as honest where a round one reads as a guess.

Scoped to a single customer, the same discipline gives you the customer study: one before, one after, one number that moved. Buyers reach for those late in an evaluation, hunting for the account that matches their own size and problem, so build a set that covers each segment instead of one showpiece. Expect a standing fight with the featured customer's legal and communications teams, who want to sand off the specifics that carry the proof; when a study survives that negotiation with nothing concrete left, spend the effort on a customer who'll let you name the before.

Accept the accountability this creates. Once you publish a number, you're answerable to it, and running a finding that doesn't cut your way takes nerve the borrowed stat never demanded. A dataset ages, so a story built on data commits you to gathering more; publish a benchmark once and never refresh it and it becomes a liability the year it goes stale. Treat real research as an ongoing content engine, and accept that you can't fake it and can't coast on it.

## Examples

**Patagonia, "Don't Buy This Jacket" (November 2011).** On Black Friday 2011, Patagonia ran a full-page ad in *The New York Times* showing its own best-selling R2 fleece under a headline telling readers not to buy it. The ad worked because Patagonia built it on data it had gathered about that specific jacket: making one required 135 liters of water, enough for the daily needs of 45 people; it generated nearly 20 pounds of carbon dioxide, about 24 times the weight of the finished garment; and it left behind two-thirds of its weight in waste. Those numbers were the story. A generic "buy less" message would have read as posturing; the jacket's own life-cycle figures made the argument undeniable and made it Patagonia's, because no one else had run the analysis on that product. The company had been building the capability since 2007 with the Footprint Chronicles, an interactive site tracing the environmental cost of individual products through the supply chain.

**Impossible Foods and the commissioned life-cycle assessment (2019).** Impossible Foods was making a category-creation argument that a plant-based burger belonged on a meat-eater's plate, and taste alone wouldn't win the environmental case. So the company paid the analytics firm Quantis to run a peer-reviewed life-cycle assessment comparing the Impossible Burger to a quarter-pound beef burger. The finding became the spine of the company's environmental story: the Impossible Burger used about 96% less land and 87% less water and generated 89% fewer greenhouse-gas emissions. Those three figures did what a decade of "eat less meat" messaging couldn't, because they were specific, sourced, and unavailable anywhere else. A buyer, a regulator, or a journalist could repeat "87% less water" and trace it back to a study with a named methodology and a date. The data was expensive and slow to produce, and it contradicted nothing in the pitch, which is why it held up under the scrutiny a bold claim invites. When taste or opinion can't win the argument, buy the measurement that can.

**Mailchimp's email benchmarks (2019).** Mailchimp sees email performance across millions of accounts, data no analyst outside the company can assemble. Rather than argue in the abstract about what a good open rate is, Mailchimp published "Email Marketing Benchmarks by Industry," reporting average open and click rates broken out by sector from its own send data. The report became one of the most-cited references in the field because the number was genuinely scarce: a marketer couldn't get an industry-specific open-rate benchmark anywhere else, and every citation pointed back to Mailchimp.

**Mesosphere and the Verizon utilization number (2016).** At Mesosphere we drafted a case study around Verizon's use of DC/OS to run containerized workloads across their datacenters, and the first draft called Verizon "a leading telecom" and reported that the platform "improved resource utilization." I sent it back. The rewrite named Verizon and named the figure their own engineer had presented publicly at our conference: server utilization moving from the low double digits toward the seventies once workloads were bin-packed onto shared infrastructure. The before is what made the study travel, because every infrastructure buyer knew their own servers sat mostly idle and could measure themselves against Verizon's starting point. Scope the discipline down to one account, and give the reader a baseline they can stand next to.

**Gatorade's founding number (1965 onward).** The drink began as a measurement: Robert Cade's University of Florida team weighed players before and after practice and measured what the heat pulled out of them in sweat, sodium, and glucose, then mixed a drink to put it back. The finding carried the story, a team that stopped wilting in the second half, on data no beverage company owned. The gathering never stopped; the Gatorade Sports Science Institute, founded in 1988, has supplied the hydration findings the brand's marketing quotes, and a rival can't cite them without naming Gatorade.
