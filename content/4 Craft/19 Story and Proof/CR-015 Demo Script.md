> *The buyer remembers the story your demo told and forgets every feature it listed.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A demo works as short drama: the buyer enters as the protagonist, a specific situation creates tension, and the product resolves it on screen. The buyer remembers the change after the clicks disappear. Its closest companions are [[CR-012 Teach the Buyer to Buy]] and [[CR-021 Video and Podcast Craft]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't demo your product as a guided tour of its menus, paced by your org chart and your feature list. A tour leaves the buyer unable to see their own work getting done, and an hour later unable to remember what the product was for.

In the default demo, a rep shares their screen, opens the application, and starts at the top-left navigation. "So over here is your dashboard. Up here is settings, and this is where you'd configure your integrations. Let me show you the reporting module. There's a ton in here." Forty minutes later the buyer has watched forty screens, nodded at each, and retained almost none of it. They can't answer the only question that decides the deal: does this solve my problem, and can I see myself using it? The demo covered everything and landed nothing.

The tour is what you build without deciding to. You know the product by its architecture, because that's how you learned it and how the roadmap is organized. Your buyer knows the product by their pain, because that's the only reason they took the call. A demo built from the architecture narrates a map of the software; the buyer needed a story about themselves, and the two never meet. Fear makes it worse. A capability you skip is one a competitor can claim you lack, so the demo grows to cover everything and completeness becomes the goal. You end up with a demo built to survive a checklist. A demo that proves the product has no gaps proves nothing about whether the product changes the buyer's day.

The tour also ignores how memory works. People remember stories and forget lists. A buyer given a character (a version of themselves) who starts in trouble, hits a turning point, and ends up better off carries that arc into the next internal meeting and retells it to the boss who wasn't on the call. A menu walkthrough leaves them nothing to retell. Your internal champion is the person who can re-perform the demo from memory to a room you'll never enter, and a story is the only thing they can re-perform.

Product order buries your payoff. Menus put configuration and setup first, because you have to set a thing up before you use it. Following that order spends your most attentive minutes, the opening, on account settings, permissions, and data import. By the time you reach the moment that would have made the buyer lean forward, they've already decided the product is complicated and their attention is gone. The lede ends up buried under your own table of contents.

What the buyer carries out of the demo depends on how you spend that hour. In a competitive enterprise deal, the demo is frequently the single most valuable hour in the cycle, the moment an evaluator forms the mental picture they'll defend or attack for the next three months. Spent on a tour, that hour proves your product is thorough. Spent on a story, it hands the buyer a reason to buy that they can carry themselves.

## Complications

- **Completeness pulls against memorability.** The product has dozens of features and the demo has thirty minutes; show all of them and the buyer remembers none, yet cutting features feels like conceding capability.
- **Your mental model fights the buyer's.** You organize the product by architecture (modules, settings, integrations); the buyer organizes it by their own job (the task, the frustration, the win). A demo built on the first stays illegible to a buyer holding the second.
- **Fear of the gap drives the tour.** Skip a capability and a competitor may claim you lack it, so showing everything feels safe. The safe-feeling move is the one that dilutes the story.
- **Product order buries the payoff.** Software makes you configure before you use, so the natural sequence spends your best opening minutes on setup screens.
- **Rehearsal fights personalization.** A tightly authored demo is memorable and repeatable, yet a fully improvised one adapts to the buyer while losing its dramatic shape.
- **Live risk fights polish.** A real product demoed live can break, which tempts you toward safe, shallow clicks; the moments most worth showing are often the ones most likely to fail on stage.
- **The demo depends on discovery it doesn't own.** You can author the buyer's story only if you've learned the buyer's situation first, and thin discovery leaves you with a demo about a generic company.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Author the demo as a short drama with the buyer as protagonist and the product resolving a specific tension on screen. Every screen must set up the turn, deliver it, or pay it off; delete any screen that does none of the three.

A demo has the structure of a story, so build it like one. Give it a beginning that establishes the world before: the character, their role, the task in front of them, the way that task hurts today. Give it a turning point, the aha, where the product does the thing that changes the character's situation; this is the moment the whole demo exists to deliver. Give it an end that shows the world after: the same character, the same task, now finished in a fraction of the time or with a result they couldn't reach before. Everything in the demo sets up that turn, delivers it, or pays it off. Anything else is a menu item, so cut it.

Then run these moves:

1. **Lead with the payoff.** Product order buries the best moment under configuration screens, so reverse it: open on the finished result or the wow moment, then work backward to how the character got there. Peter Cohan built *Great Demo!* (2003) on that reversal and named it doing the last thing first. Cohan puts the best screen at the front; this pattern puts the character and their trouble in front of it too, so the demo opens on somebody's good morning rather than a dashboard. The buyer's attention is highest in the first two minutes, so spend those minutes on the thing worth remembering.
2. **Cast the buyer's real role, on the buyer's real world.** A demo starring a generic "Acme Corp" with dummy data forces the buyer to translate; do that work for them. Put their industry, their job title, and numbers that look like theirs on the screen. This is where specificity does the persuading ([[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]]): real-looking data and a recognizable scenario make the story credible in a way a sandbox never will.
3. **Teach while you dramatize.** The demo is the primary place the buyer learns to own the product after purchase, so let the script teach as it tells; [[CR-012 Teach the Buyer to Buy]] owns that discipline, and a well-authored demo is its main stage.
4. **Script it, rehearse it, then personalize.** Write the story. Rehearse until the timing is exact and the payoff lands on cue. Only then swap in the details for the buyer in the room. Hold the arc fixed and let the character's face change.

The craft principle holds across media and across decades. It governed a live keynote in 2007 and a screen-share sales call today, because it describes how humans attend and remember, which holds for any tool. Before you ship the demo, run the test on every screen: ask whether it sets up the turn, delivers it, or pays it off, and if it does none of the three, delete it.

## Implications

Once your demo is authored, first contact becomes an experience the buyer can retell, which is what makes the internal champion effective in the rooms you can't attend. Make the demo the central moment for [[CR-012 Teach the Buyer to Buy]], because a story that shows the buyer doing the job is also the buyer's first lesson in doing it. Reuse the arc beyond the live call: take the same drama to a one-to-many audience, remembering that roughly half of registrants never attend the live session, and capture it as an asset that runs without a presenter with [[CR-021 Video and Podcast Craft]]. Feed discovery in reverse, too: once you know which single turning point converts, sharpen the discovery questions that let you set that turn up for each buyer.

Now enforce the discipline the new tension demands. A demo scripted and rehearsed to the second is memorable and repeatable, and pushed too far it reads as a performance the buyer is watching rather than a conversation they're in, which invites the suspicion that the real product is messier than the show. So author the arc rigidly and hold the details loose: fixed beginning, fixed turn, fixed payoff, with room inside for the buyer's own situation to fill the frame. Aim for a demo rehearsed enough to feel inevitable and specific enough to feel like you built it this morning, for this buyer.

## Examples

**Salesforce, the "day in the life" demo (a standing field practice with no dated origin).** Most competitors toured the CRM module by module. Salesforce opened on a character. A sales engineer started with a rep named, say, Maria, beginning her Monday, and the demo followed Maria's day. A lead comes in, and we watch it get routed to her. She works the opportunity, and we watch the record update. Friday arrives, and her manager pulls the dashboard to see the whole team's pipeline, which is where the story pays off for the economic buyer in the room. The account settings, the permissions model, the integration catalog all existed, and almost none of it appeared, because none of it moved Maria's story forward. The buyer left able to picture their own reps living inside the product, and able to retell the arc to the VP who wasn't on the call. The same discipline shaped Marc Benioff's launch demos, which dramatized enterprise software running in a browser with no install as the resolution to a decade of on-premise pain, first at the "End of Software" launch event in February 2000. The story was the product's argument.

**Apple, the iPhone keynote demo (January 9, 2007).** Apple could have listed a 3.5-inch display, a capacitive touchscreen, and a mobile browser, which no one would remember. Steve Jobs authored the whole thing as theater where every choice serves the arc. He opened on the payoff, framing the device as three products in one and then revealing they were a single object. Each capability arrived as its own small drama with a clear turn: he placed a real phone call, scrolled a music library with a flick, pinched a photo to zoom, and loaded a web page that rendered like a desktop. He scripted and rehearsed the sequence to the second, and he accepted real live risk, since the demo ran on prototype hardware that could have failed on stage, because the payoff of doing it live was the whole point: the audience had to watch the thing work. It became one of the most retold product demos in the industry.

**Ron Popeil, where the demonstration was the entire pitch (Ronco, 1950s onward).** Popeil could have described the product and hoped the buyer pictured it working. Instead he ran the thing on camera and let the buyer watch it work. Ronco's Chop-O-Matic sold at $3.98 and moved more than two million units on the strength of a live demonstration, and when the machine chopped so fast that a day of demos ate more vegetables than a salesman could carry, Popeil's people filmed the demonstration instead. That film became the television commercial, so the product's argument and its advertising were the same object. The Showtime Rotisserie, launched in 1998, ran the same structure at scale: a live audience, food going in, the line "set it and forget it," and dinner coming out on camera. Ronco's housewares sales passed a billion dollars with the rotisserie as its biggest seller. None of it happened on a screen share, and the structure was the one a software demo needs: a person with a problem, a turn, and the result in view.

**A demo I had to rebuild.** Early at a company I worked with, our standard demo was a proud, complete tour of the platform. We showed the architecture, the dashboards, the configuration depth, every integration, because we were sure our thoroughness was the reason to buy, and we kept losing to a competitor with a thinner product. Watching a recording, the problem was plain: our first ten minutes were setup screens, and by the time we reached the one capability that actually changed an operator's day, the room had checked out. We threw the tour away and rebuilt the demo as a single story. One operator, one bad night on call, one moment where our product caught the failure before it spread, and the calm morning after. We opened on that calm morning, then rewound to show how we got there. Same product, same features underneath, roughly half the runtime. Win rate on demoed deals rose noticeably over the following quarter. We hadn't shipped a line of code. We'd stopped giving a tour and started telling the buyer's story.
