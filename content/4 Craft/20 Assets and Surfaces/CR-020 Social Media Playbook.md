> *Every platform has a house style; pay it the courtesy of its native form while you keep telling the one story.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A social media playbook holds one narrative steady while the container learns each platform's native reading, scrolling, and sharing habits; one story can travel without arriving dressed for the wrong room. It sits below [[OPS-013 Repurposing Pipeline]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't broadcast one identical post to every platform, and don't hand each platform to a different person who invents a different story. The first ignores the form and the second loses the narrative, and both leave your presence with no through-line.

The auto-cross-post is the more common failure because a tool makes it free. You write a post, check four boxes, and the same block of text with the same image lands on LinkedIn, X, Instagram, and Facebook at once. On LinkedIn it's a wall of text with a link the feed buries. On X it's truncated at the character limit with the interesting clause cut off. On Instagram the link does nothing, because Instagram captions aren't clickable, so the call to action points at a dead end. On TikTok it never appears at all, because you pasted text into a video-first surface. The content was written for no platform, so it works on none of them. Engagement flatlines, and you conclude that "social doesn't work for us" when the problem is that you published one shape into four holes.

The opposite failure looks healthier and rots slower. Staffing each platform separately produces it: an agency runs LinkedIn, a contractor runs TikTok, an intern runs X. Each produces competent, on-brand content. But nobody holds the narrative, so LinkedIn argues the product saves money, TikTok jokes about the industry, and X reposts other people's takes. A prospect who follows you across two platforms meets two different companies. The presence is busy and says nothing, because there's no spine holding the pieces into a claim. Activity substitutes for a message.

Both failures come from the same missing idea: a platform is a form factor, a set of mechanics that decides how a message can be built and how it travels. Each surface works on its own terms. LinkedIn rewards a document carousel and native text that keeps the reader on-platform. X rewards a threaded argument where each post earns the next tap. TikTok and Reels reward a spoken hook in the first two seconds and a face on camera. YouTube rewards a title and thumbnail that promise a payoff worth ten minutes. These aren't cosmetic differences. Each surface has its own ranking logic, its own reading posture, its own reason a viewer stops scrolling. Gary Vaynerchuk made this case in *Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook* (2013), where the rule is that content is king and context is God. His chapters go platform by platform, through Facebook, Twitter, Pinterest, Instagram and Tumblr, and they've dated the way such a list has to; what carries forward is the method for translating one story into whatever the surfaces reward this year. A message built for the wrong mechanics can't perform however good the underlying idea is.

Buffer, whose product exists to publish to every network at once, tells its own users not to use it that way. Its published cross-posting guide concedes that you can send an identical post everywhere, then spends the rest of the page on the tweaks to caption length, hashtags and video format that decide whether the post performs, and it names the giveaway this pattern keeps meeting: a Facebook post ending in "link in bio," which announces that nobody adapted anything. Buffer publishes that as a practitioner's rule rather than a measured comparison, which makes it guidance from the company with the most exposure to the problem rather than evidence. The platforms have trained their audiences to expect a native form, and content that arrives in the wrong one reads as spam, even when the sender is a brand the viewer likes.

## Complications

- **The tool makes broadcasting free, and the form makes it worthless.** One click sends the same post everywhere; the platforms then bury or truncate whatever wasn't built for them, so the cheapest option is also the least effective.
- **Consistency wants one message; the surfaces want four builds.** A single narrative keeps the presence coherent, but each platform's mechanics demand a different container, so coherence and native form pull against each other.
- **Staffing per platform buys competence and scatters the story.** Handing each surface to a specialist raises the craft on that surface while removing the one person who could hold the through-line.
- **Reach lives in native features that resist your links.** The algorithms reward content that keeps users on-platform (documents, video, threads) and suppress the outbound link your funnel wants, so the version that travels furthest is the version that sends nobody anywhere.
- **Speed rewards copy-paste; mastery rewards translation.** Reformatting one idea into five native forms takes real time, and the calendar always argues for the shortcut.
- **Platforms change their mechanics without warning.** A form factor that worked last quarter (a carousel, a link sticker, reach on a hashtag) can be down-ranked overnight, and a playbook built out of frozen templates expires with them.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Run one narrative spine across the whole presence, and rebuild that spine into each platform's native form factor, so every surface tells the same story in the shape its audience is trained to read.

Fix the spine first. Decide the single thing your social presence is arguing this quarter, the claim a follower should absorb whether they meet you on X or YouTube. That spine is downstream of the company's positioning and its [[CR-005 Persona Messaging]]; social's job is to distribute that existing story across surfaces. With the spine fixed, work through the build:

1. **Translate rather than syndicate.** A translator keeps the meaning and changes the grammar to fit the target language; keep the narrative and change the form to fit the target surface. Turn one insight into a LinkedIn document carousel, an X thread, a ninety-second talking-head video for Reels and TikTok, and a longer cut for YouTube.
2. **Obey each surface's mechanics.** Front-load the payload on slide one of the carousel because LinkedIn readers swipe shallow; make post one of the thread a complete hook because the rest hides behind a tap; say the claim out loud in the first two seconds of the video because that's when the viewer decides to stay.
3. **Keep each build to one idea.** Every version still obeys [[CR-007 One Message Per Asset]]; make sure all of them carry the same idea.
4. **Re-derive the form when the platform changes.** When a surface alters its mechanics, keep the narrative and ask what the surface rewards now, then build the spine into that.

Hold a playbook that pairs a spine with a per-platform form guide: what a native post looks like on each surface, what the hook has to do, and whether the link belongs in the post or the profile. Leave the tempo of those builds to [[OPS-009 Messaging Cadence]]; this pattern governs only their shape.

## Implications

A presence built this way reads as one company speaking many native dialects. A prospect who follows you across three platforms meets the same argument three times in three forms, which compounds into recognition instead of confusion, and the repetition across surfaces does the work a single channel can't do alone. Give the awareness stage a coherent voice, hand each asset to [[CR-007 One Message Per Asset]] for its internal discipline, and let this pattern guarantee the assets share a spine.

The presence also becomes legible to measure. Because every platform carries the same narrative, compare which form factor moves the story best for which audience, and shift effort toward the surfaces that earn it rather than staffing all of them at equal weight out of habit.

Now enforce the method against the calendar. Doing this well means building four or five native versions of one idea every time, which is slower than a cross-post and demands someone who understands each surface's mechanics. Under deadline pressure the discipline erodes back toward broadcasting, and the presence quietly loses its native fit. Hold the method against that pull, and write the spine down: a fixed narrative is what makes fast per-platform builds possible, because without it speed collapses back into copy-paste.

## Examples

**Buffer's own presence, rebuilt per network (2014 onward).** Buffer sells a social-scheduling tool, so its content team had to decide whether to push one post everywhere. Under content lead Kevan Lee, who joined in 2014, the published method was to take one core idea (a data-backed post on, say, the best time to publish) and rebuild it per network: the long-form study on the blog, the finding as a native thread, the takeaway in the first line of the LinkedIn version, a graphic for the surface that wants a graphic. Buffer has published the method for a decade and has never published a controlled test of syndication against rewriting, so the case is worth exactly what a practitioner's rule is worth. The narrative stays fixed (Buffer teaches you to do social well) and the form changes on every surface.

**Wendy's inside Fortnite, one claim rebuilt out of a game (November 2018).** Wendy's spine is a single durable claim, fresh beef and never frozen. When Fortnite launched its Food Fight mode on November 14, 2018, splitting players between a burger restaurant and a pizza place, the burger side kept its beef in freezers. VMLY&R put a red-pigtailed Wendy's character into the game on the pizza team and spent more than nine hours on Twitch destroying freezers instead of fighting anyone. The claim never changed; the container was a game's own mechanics and a livestream's own pacing, which is not something a scheduling tool can produce from a text box. The Drum reported more than 1.5 million minutes watched, a live audience past a quarter of a million, and a 119% rise in mentions of the brand; the developers later took the burger freezers out of the game. The campaign won the first Social and Influencer Grand Prix at Cannes Lions in June 2019. Wendy's found what the platform actually rewarded and built the claim it already had out of that material.

**Duolingo's owl, one voice in native forms (2020 onward).** Duolingo's social presence, run at first by Zaria Parvez alone after she joined in 2020, built a single narrative (the mascot Duo is an unhinged, needy character who wants you to do your lessons) and then rebuilt it into each platform's form. On TikTok it became short vertical videos of the costumed owl doing trend-native bits, a person on camera in the first second. On X it became short reactive text and images written in the platform's fast, ironic register. On Instagram it became Reels and stationary posts sized for that grid. The character (the spine) stayed identical across all three; the container matched each surface's mechanics. Duolingo fixed the character and let the container change. Parvez told *Inc.* the TikTok account went from 50,000 followers to about 16 million over roughly four years of this, with Instagram climbing from 300,000 to 4.6 million, and the through-line meant a follower who met Duo on TikTok recognized the same Duo on X.

**The cross-posted enterprise account (a common anti-pattern).** I've reviewed more than one B2B social presence run entirely through a scheduling tool's "post to all" button. The tell is always the same, a LinkedIn post ending in "link in bio," which is an Instagram convention that means nothing on LinkedIn; an X post cut off mid-sentence at the character limit; an Instagram caption with a raw, unclickable URL. The content was often fine as an idea and dead on arrival as a post, because it had been built for no surface in particular. Once the same quarterly narrative the company already had was rebuilt into each platform's native form, the accounts that had looked lifeless for a year started to earn reach.
