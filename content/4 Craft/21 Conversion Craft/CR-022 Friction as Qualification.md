> *The step you make people climb is the question you're asking them, and only the ones who answer are worth talking to.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> Friction qualifies demand when a step costs the serious buyer little and the uncommitted visitor more than they will pay. The crossing becomes a signal, and a graduated ladder sizes each next ask to the trust already earned. It sits above [[CR-024 Form as Filter]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't treat every point of friction as a leak to seal. The effort itself is the instrument that separates a buyer with a real problem from a browser who will never pay.

The reigning doctrine says friction is the enemy. It counts every extra form field, every "request a demo" wall, every price you make someone ask about as a leak, and tells you to plug them until the path from stranger to signup is as short as physics allows. Measured on raw conversion rate, this works: remove the credit-card requirement from the trial and signups jump, cut the form from seven fields to two and lead volume doubles, and the dashboard turns green.

What came through the door matters more than the count. The doubled volume is mostly students, competitors, job-seekers, and people who clicked because the button was there. The frictionless trial fills with accounts that provision once, never return, and inflate the active-user chart while contributing nothing. You've handed the humans who follow up a pile of work with a worse hit rate than before, and because volume and quality live on different dashboards, nobody connects them for a quarter. Cost per lead falls while cost per closed deal rises, and no team owns that ratio. The friction you deleted was doing work.

Friction comes in two kinds. Friction that punishes the qualified buyer, a slow page, a confusing checkout, a form that rejects valid input, is pure waste; remove it without mercy. Friction that costs the serious buyer almost nothing while costing the casual one more than they're willing to spend is a different thing entirely: a question. A refundable deposit asks "are you actually going to buy a car, or are you daydreaming?" A required onboarding call asks "is this worth thirty minutes of your calendar?" The person with the problem answers in a sentence and moves on. The person without one leaves, and their leaving is the point.

The logic underneath is costly signaling, old and well understood. A signal is only credible when it's expensive to fake. In biology, Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle explains why a peacock's tail is honest: it's costly, so only a healthy bird can carry it. Michael Spence won a Nobel for the economic version, where a job candidate's degree signals ability partly because it's hard to obtain, independent of anything learned. A frictionless action signals nothing, because anyone can perform it. The instant a step costs something, clearing it starts to mean something.

A toll also works better when it climbs in stages. Robert Cialdini named the mechanism commitment-and-consistency in *Influence* (1984): someone who takes a small step toward a thing becomes measurably likelier to take the next, larger one. Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser had shown it in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* in 1966, where homeowners who first agreed to display a small safe-driving sticker were far likelier to allow a large, ugly billboard on their lawns than homeowners approached cold. Direct-response marketers had priced it in since the mail-order era, when a two-step offer outpulled a one-step offer to a cold list, because the small toll selects for interest and manufactures a little more of it.

## Complications

- **Volume pulls against intent.** Lower friction raises the raw count and lowers the average quality; higher friction does the reverse. You can't maximize both.
- **The two kinds look identical on a chart.** Friction that taxes the qualified buyer is waste; friction that taxes only the unqualified one is a filter. A single "reduce friction" metric drives you to delete both.
- **Trust sets the price.** A cold visitor won't book a call; a warm one will resent a form, and a single ask sized for one of them loses the other.
- **A ladder costs upkeep and can read as a maze.** Every extra rung is another asset to build and keep current, and a reader who can't see both the cheap next rung and the destination reads the graduation as evasion.
- **Effort can insult or flatter.** The same required step reads as "they respect my time enough to prepare" or as "they're making me jump through hoops," depending on what the effort buys the person who spends it.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Place friction where it costs the qualified buyer least and the unqualified buyer most, and read who clears it as your intent signal, rather than optimizing every step toward zero.

Treat each conversion step as a question you're asking the visitor, priced so that only someone with real intent will pay to answer. Charge in effort (a form field, a scheduled call), money (a deposit, a paid trial, a membership fee), or commitment (a stated problem, a named budget, a calendar hold). Make the cost trivial to the person you want and prohibitive to the person you don't: a founder evaluating a $50k platform will happily give you their real title and their real problem, and a job-seeker won't.

Then sort those currencies by whether the buyer can hand the bill to somebody else. Money and calendar time can't be delegated cheaply: a deposit costs the same whoever pays it, and an hour held for a call is an hour of a specific person's life. Effort is different, because effort only signals while the person spends it themselves. The moment it can be handed to software that fills the eleven fields and drafts the free-text answer, the cost falls toward zero and the step keeps looking like friction on your dashboard while it filters nobody. Denominate the filters you rely on in money or in someone's calendar.

Stop asking "how do we reduce friction here?" and start asking "what does clearing this step prove, and do we want it proven?" Then act on the answer:

1. **If clearing it proves nothing,** delete the step; it's pure tax.
2. **If clearing it proves this person has the problem, the budget, or the seriousness you sell to,** keep it, and read the clear-through rate as your demand signal rather than the click-through rate.

Make the friction buy something for the person who pays it, or it curdles into arrogance. A required onboarding call qualifies intent and delivers a faster path to value; a deposit qualifies intent and reserves a place in line. When the effort returns value, it reads as respect. When it returns nothing, it reads as a hoop, and the qualified buyer resents it as much as the casual one. Make the step you require also the step that helps.

Match the price to the trust in the room, so graduate the friction rather than making it binary. One ask stamped onto every surface is sized for a single reader and wrong for everyone else. Put "request a demo" at the foot of an educational blog post and the stranger who found it through a search declines by closing the tab; the page records a zero for a reader who wanted a smaller step. Under-ask everywhere and you invite everyone while qualifying nobody, and the buyer ready to talk this week hunts for a door behind the soft offer you handed the tire-kicker. Build a ladder, and size each rung to the trust the reader has plausibly earned by arriving there:

1. **Cold traffic** (a blog post, a search result): ask for something that costs almost nothing and gives something back, a related guide or a two-minute assessment.
2. **The warm reader** (a comparison page, a customer story): ask for more, since they've spent real attention. A webinar seat, a free tool behind a login.
3. **The hot reader** (the pricing page, a fourth visit): put the big ask at full size, and leave it visible for anyone ready to skip ahead.

Choose each rung to make the next one easier, which is what separates a ladder from a menu. Wire the assessment to recommend the webinar, end the webinar by offering the free tool, let the tool surface the demo once someone has used it. [[CR-024 Form as Filter]] owns the same pricing one input at a time. Hold the principle underneath: the obstacle is the question, and who answers it is the lead.

## Implications

Set friction deliberately and the top of the funnel becomes a working filter, so leads that reach a human arrive pre-sorted by intent and the sales motion gets cheaper per closed deal even as it gets more expensive per lead. That trade only pays off if the rest of the system reads the signal. Weight the intent surfaced here inside [[OPS-007 Lead Scoring and Routing]], where the rung a lead entered on tells you how warm they are and a high-friction step cleared beats a dozen frictionless clicks. It also reshapes what "conversion" means, which is why it sits beside [[CR-023 Call to Conversation, Not Conversion]]: once friction qualifies instead of captures, the goal shifts from harvesting an email to starting a real exchange.

Friction cuts both ways, and you will sometimes cut the wrong people. A deposit that filters out daydreamers also filters out the cautious buyer who'd have converted with more patience; a required call that qualifies serious evaluators loses the introvert who'd have been your best customer. Set the toll too high and you'll starve the funnel while congratulating yourself on lead quality. Price the friction to the buyer you want, then measure at the far end, where closed-deal quality is the only honest reading. Hold the harder path against the organizational pull to "just make it easier"; that's its own recurring fight.

## Examples

**Superhuman's onboarding call (2017–2021).** Superhuman, the premium email client, could have removed every obstacle so a new user could sign up and start in seconds. Instead it refused to let new users self-serve. You had to book a thirty-minute one-on-one onboarding session first, and at $30 a month the product wasn't cheap either. A scheduled human call is enormous friction at the moment you're trying to activate a user. Founder Rahul Vohra kept it, because the call did two jobs. It qualified intent, since only someone serious about their email gives up thirty minutes, and it delivered value, since a specialist tuned the app to how that person worked, so they hit the payoff on day one instead of bouncing off a blank inbox. By mid-2019 the *New York Times* put 180,000 people on the waitlist, and Superhuman opened self-serve only years later, once the call had done its filtering.

**Tesla's Model 3 reservation deposit (March 2016).** Tesla asked for a $1,000 refundable deposit to hold a place in line for a car that wouldn't ship for more than a year. A refundable $1,000 is friction priced precisely: it costs a genuine buyer almost nothing, since the money comes back and reserves their spot, and it costs a daydreamer more than an idle wish can carry. On April 7, 2016, a week after the unveiling, Tesla said more than 325,000 people had paid. Higher totals circulated afterward as cancellations landed, so the week-one figure is the one worth quoting. That was qualified demand, strong enough to guide production planning and to tell the capital markets the car had real buyers. An open waitlist would have produced a bigger, emptier number.

**Alinea and Next, dinner sold as a ticket (Chicago, 2011).** Nick Kokonas opened Next in 2011 selling prepaid tickets priced by date and time, with no reservation option at all, then moved Alinea onto the same system. Paying weeks ahead costs a diner who means to come almost nothing and costs a casual booker the whole price of dinner. Kokonas told *Crain's Chicago Business* in 2014 that Alinea's net profit had risen 38% since the switch, that answering phones had been costing nearly $200,000 a year, and that no-shows had cost about half a million dollars. The toll is denominated in money, which is why it keeps working.

**Stripe's documentation as the first rung (checked August 2026).** Stripe's lowest rung is the docs plus a test key, and its testing page still prints the card number 4242 4242 4242 4242, so a developer can make a fake charge in minutes without talking to anyone. A successful test transaction is a stronger signal of intent than a form fill, and it earns the next ask: go live, which requires real business details. The custom-pricing conversation, which Stripe reserves for large payments volume, sits at the top of the ladder.

**Basecamp keeps the price at the front door (2004–present).** 37signals could have built a sprawling free tier to capture everyone and hoped some would convert. Instead it has kept the paid plan as the qualifying step for twenty years, at a public flat price identical for every customer. Basecamp is often described as having refused free, and it never did: it launched in February 2004 with a free single-project plan alongside three paid tiers, added a more generous free tier (Basecamp Personal) in November 2019, retired free entirely in the October 2022 repricing, and offers a free plan again today, capped at one project, twenty users, and one gigabyte. What held constant is a free tier too small for real work and a published price that never becomes a negotiation; Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson have argued for years that they don't want every customer.
