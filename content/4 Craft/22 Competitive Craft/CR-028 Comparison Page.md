> *The buyer is already comparing you; the only question is whether they do it on your page or on a page you don't control.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A comparison page earns trust by conceding one real competitor advantage in public. That honest row lends credibility to every row where the company wins. It sits below [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Never publish a rigged table, every row a green check for you and a red X for them, because buyers discount it on sight and the page you built to close the deal instead proves you can't be trusted.

The buyer who lands on your "us versus them" page is mid-evaluation, already holds a shortlist, and typed the competitor's name on purpose. This buyer knows what you tend to forget: you wrote this page, and you chose the rows. A heavy discount lands before the first cell loads. The page they braced for, a perfect column of wins for you and a perfect column of losses for the rival, makes that discount total. They won't conclude you're better. They'll conclude you're the kind of company that builds a table like this, and they'll go find the truth somewhere you don't control.

Four moves give the amateur version away. Picking feature rows so you win them all hides the rows the buyer cares about, the ones where the competitor is stronger. Describing the competitor's product in its two-year-old state misses what it shipped last quarter. Renaming a capability makes a thing both products do show as your checkmark and their blank. A strawman comparison wins against a product the serious buyer would never weigh. A buyer who has used both products reads every one of these, and one caught distortion poisons the rest: if the row they can verify is wrong, they won't trust the rows they can't.

The full cost runs well past the lost sale. Buyers stopped trusting vendor comparisons as a category, and an entire industry grew in the vacuum. G2 and TrustRadius exist in large part because the "us versus them" page couldn't be believed, so buyers routed around vendors to peers and reviews for the honest read. Another rigged table confirms that buyer was right to leave and check elsewhere. The page had one job, to be the trusted account of the tradeoff, and dishonesty forfeits the only thing that made it worth building.

Vendors fake the honesty too. Some concede a trivial weakness, the competitor has a mobile app in a color nobody cares about, to buy the look of fairness, then rig every row that matters. Buyers see through the token concession faster than the naked table, because now the manipulation wears a costume.

## Complications

- **The buyer already distrusts the source.** The page is authored by an interested party, so it starts underwater, and every claim reads as advocacy until something earns belief.
- **Persuasion pulls toward the rigged table; credibility pulls toward the honest one.** The short-term instinct is to win every row, but the row you lose is what makes the rows you win believable.
- **Concession costs a sale and buys a reputation.** Steering a poorly-fit buyer to the competitor loses that deal and wins the trust of the ten buyers watching how you handle the truth.
- **The comparison ages.** Competitors ship, and a claim that was accurate at publish becomes a caught lie six months later.
- **Intent is high and self-selected.** Someone searching "you versus competitor" is deep in evaluation, so you reach a small, valuable, skeptical audience that rewards candor and punishes spin.
- **The axis is a choice, and the wrong one loses on the merits.** On a dimension where you're genuinely weaker, honesty just hands the win away. Candor only pays on the ground [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]] already chose.
- **Public and private want different things.** What the [[CR-029 Battle Card]] says behind closed doors is sharper than what the published page can say in the open, where a screenshot travels.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Publish a comparison that concedes one real dimension where the competitor wins and directs that buyer to them. Every cell must be defensible to a buyer who has used both products; cut any cell that isn't.

Understand the mechanism before you write a row: it's a trust transfer. The direct-response trade has a name for the move, the damaging admission, and the research under it is older still. Carl Hovland, Arthur Lumsdaine and Fred Sheffield's *Experiments on Mass Communication* (Princeton, 1949), the third volume of the US Army's own wartime research program, found that a message acknowledging the other side beat a one-sided version among listeners who started out opposed or better educated. Your comparison-page reader is exactly that person, skeptical on arrival and inclined to check. The buyer is running one test on your page, whether you're honest enough to trust on the claims they can't verify themselves. A concession they can check, "if you need X, they're better, go with them," passes that test in a way no self-praise can. Once you've told the truth against your own interest, they extend provisional trust to the rest of the page, including the rows where you win. Spend one row you were going to lose anyway and buy credibility for the five rows that decide the sale. The honest page outsells the rigged one because it's the only version the buyer believes.

Build the page in four moves:

1. **Concede a real weakness.** Name a dimension the buyer actually weighs and admit the competitor wins it. A trivial concession fools no one.
2. **Route the buyer by fit.** State the customer for whom the competitor is the right call and send them there without hedging: "If your team is under ten people and you want the cheapest option, choose them." This earns trust and filters out the buyer you'd have churned anyway, the same discipline [[CR-026 A-B Testing Ethics]] applies to a buyer's attention.
3. **Fight on the axis you can hold.** Put the comparison on the ground [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]] chose, where your win is real and the competitor's is structural rather than a feature they ship next quarter. Compare on an evenly matched feature and honesty costs you the row.
4. **Prove every claim with checkable detail.** Give the exact number, the named limit, the screenshot a buyer can reproduce, and carry the credibility mechanism [[CR-008 Specificity Signals Truth]] describes into every row. Vague adjectives ("more powerful," "easier") convert far worse.

Keep the page current, because an honest comparison decays into a dishonest one on its own. The day the competitor ships the feature you said they lacked, your true claim becomes a caught distortion, and the buyer who catches it applies the full rigged-table discount even though you never lied on purpose. Date the comparison, name the version you tested, and revisit it on the competitor's release cadence. Run a last check before you publish: can you defend every cell to a buyer who has used both products? If you can't, cut the cell.

## Implications

Treat an honest comparison page as a durable asset rather than a disposable close. It captures the highest-intent search traffic in the category, the buyer typing "you versus them" at the moment of decision, and converts them at a rate cold traffic never matches, because it meets a skeptic with candor instead of spin. Feed it into the rest of the competitive craft: draw the [[CR-029 Battle Card]] from the same honest assessment, sharpened for the sales conversation, and make sure the public page and the private card tell one story at different volumes, so a buyer who talks to sales hears no contradiction with the page that brought them in.

Let the page discipline the company behind it. You can't publish an honest comparison you can't win, so run the exercise to surface exactly where the product is genuinely weaker and force [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]] to be real rather than aspirational. Keep your comparison page honest and you'll always know precisely where you lose and why.

Enforce the discipline against the pull to soften. Honesty in the open can arm the competitor and unsettle the sales team; concede a weakness and the rival may quote it back, and the reflex is to water the concession down into the token gesture buyers see through. Hold the line: the concession has to be real to work, and the trust it buys, across every buyer watching, exceeds the cost of the one weakness you handed over. Keep the public page candid, keep the [[CR-029 Battle Card]] sharp, and keep both true to the same axis.

## Examples

**Basecamp / 37signals, honest positioning (ongoing since Basecamp 3, 2016).** Basecamp built its market position by conceding what it isn't. The company states plainly that Basecamp is opinionated software for small and mid-sized teams that want calm and simplicity, and that a team wanting deep customization, Gantt charts, and heavy resource management should choose something else. Jason Fried and the marketing around the product have long told the wrong-fit buyer to go buy Asana or a full project-management suite. Basecamp concedes a real dimension buyers weigh (power and configurability), and the claim that matters, that Basecamp is calmer and clearer than the alternatives, becomes believable. Buyers trust the account of Basecamp's strengths because the same source told them, against its own interest, where Basecamp is the wrong tool. One move does the qualification filtering and the credibility building at once.

**Progressive quoting its rivals' prices (1993 onward).** Progressive became the first US auto insurer to hand shoppers comparison rates that included its competitors', and it kept the rows it lost. Its own history dates comparison rates by phone to 1993 and the 1-800-AUTO-PRO service to 1994, a round-the-clock line that quoted Progressive alongside up to three rival carriers. Sometimes the honest answer was that the caller should buy elsewhere, and the company gave that answer anyway, which is what made its own quote worth believing. It kept pressing the same advantage: the first auto-insurance website in 1995, and by 1997 the first insurer that let a customer buy a policy online in real time. Progressive built a market position out of concessions it published a few million times, and there's no software product anywhere in the case.

**Wistia versus YouTube (published since at least March 2019; checked August 2026).** Wistia sells video hosting, and its most-cited competitive content tells buyers to use its competitor. Its post "Wistia vs. YouTube: What's the Difference?", first captured by the Internet Archive in March 2019 and still posted, tells a reader to "embed with YouTube when your only goal is to grow your YouTube audience," and says many companies publish to both: Wistia for the on-brand player and the deep analytics, YouTube "for the social reach and discoverability." The page splits the two by funnel stage: YouTube and other social platforms carry the "top-of-funnel content that creates awareness," and Wistia's content is "designed to engage, convert, and retain." That's a genuine concession of the dimension where YouTube wins, published by the vendor that loses it. Wistia draws the fit line honestly: the marketer who needs an audience goes to YouTube, the marketer who needs on-site control and analytics stays on Wistia, and both believe Wistia's account of its own value precisely because Wistia conceded the case where it isn't the answer.

**The rigged table and the rise of the review site (a pattern I've watched close deals it never should have).** In advising early-stage companies I've seen the reflex repeatedly. The sales team wants a "why we win" page with every row green, and the first draft compares against a two-year-old version of the competitor. It converts poorly, and the deal notes show why: buyers who saw the page went to G2, found the current truth, and came back distrusting everything else we'd told them. G2 and TrustRadius grew into a category because vendor comparisons stopped being believable, and every rigged table pushes your own highest-intent buyers toward those neutral sources. Every time, we cut the rows we couldn't win honestly, named the one real thing the competitor did better, and told the buyer for whom that thing mattered to go choose the competitor. Conversion on the page climbed, because for the first time the buyer had a reason to believe the rest of it.
