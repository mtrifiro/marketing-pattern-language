> *A battle card that can't be read in the ten seconds before a rep answers the question has already lost the deal.*

> [!tldr] About this pattern
> A battle card compresses one competitor into a current page organized around the questions reps hear, ready for the ten seconds before a live answer. Its last-reviewed date tells the rep whether that answer deserves trust. It sits below [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]].

> [!warning] Admonition
> Don't ship competitive knowledge as a document. A fifteen-page teardown never reaches the rep who is on the call being asked why the buyer should choose you over the other vendor.

Every company that sells against a named rival eventually builds competitive collateral. Your product marketer interviews lost-deal prospects, reads the competitor's docs and pricing, maybe buys their product, and writes it all up, thorough and proud of it: feature-by-feature grids, teardown decks, a "state of the competition" narrative. Then it goes into a folder in the sales portal, and the rep who needed it three weeks ago never opens it again. The knowledge exists. It just doesn't exist where a human can reach it in the four seconds between a buyer saying "we're also looking at Acme" and needing to say something back.

Collateral fails to become usable in three ways, and most companies run all three at once. Length is the first: a document you have to study is useless mid-conversation, and reps fall back on improvised, off-message answers that vary by rep and by mood. Staleness is the second: competitors ship, reprice, and rebrand, and a card written eight months ago tells the rep to attack a weakness the rival fixed last release, which the buyer knows and the rep doesn't, so the rep looks uninformed at the exact moment authority matters most. Dishonesty is the third: internal competitive material drifts into trash-talk and overstated claims because it's written to make the field feel armed, and it backfires. An informed buyer catches the overstatement, discounts every claim standing next to it, and ends up thinking harder about the rival than they would have, because you just spent the call arguing on the rival's ground. A card that hands reps ammunition they can't fire without discrediting themselves is worse than no card. Gong's call analysis does support arming the rep early: a competitor raised early in the cycle correlates with a better close rate, while the same rival surfacing late correlates with a worse one.

The cost of that is concentrated in the deals you can least afford to lose. Competitive deals are the expensive ones: they close slower, discount deeper, and churn more when the buyer chose you against a rival they still admire. In those deals the rep is the last mile and the rep's confidence in the moment is your asset. When the rep freezes, contradicts the pitch, or repeats a talking point the buyer already disproved, the deal doesn't visibly die; it slides to "no decision" and gets logged as a lost opportunity nobody can explain.

The adoption numbers describe the same gap from the outside. Crayon's annual State of Competitive Intelligence survey has found, year after year, that companies invest heavily in producing competitive content while a large share of it never reaches the field in a form reps use; battle cards are among the most-produced and least-consumed sales assets. You already have the knowledge; what you're missing is a usable package and a path to the rep. The intelligence sits trapped in a format a person under time pressure, on a call, cannot metabolize.

## Complications

- **Completeness pulls against usability.** The researcher wants every fact on the card so nothing's missing; the rep wants one glanceable page so something's findable. Every fact you add slows the page down.
- **Freshness pulls against effort.** A card is only true until the competitor moves, and keeping it current is unglamorous maintenance no one owns by default, so cards rot silently.
- **Confidence pulls against honesty.** Reps ask for ammunition, so the pressure is to overstate the rival's flaws and your edge. An informed buyer exposes the overstatement and the rep loses credibility for the rest of the deal.
- **Central authorship pulls against field reality.** Marketing has the research; the reps have the objections that actually come up in the room. A card written from research alone answers questions no buyer asked.
- **Offense pulls against defense.** A rep needs the reason you win and the safe response to the rival's strongest attack, and a card weighted only toward "why we're great" leaves the rep defenseless when the buyer leads with the competitor's real advantage.
- **Recall speed pulls against depth.** The moment is live and lasts seconds. Anything the rep has to interpret, reconcile, or scroll to find might as well not be there.

## Recommendations

> [!check] Give each competitor a single, current, seller-facing page built around the questions reps actually get asked in the room, written so it can be used in real time and maintained so it stays true.

Treat the card as a different artifact from the research document, with a different job. One page, one competitor. Organize it by the buyer's moves rather than your feature list, because the rep needs to find the answer by remembering the question. Build four zones:

1. **The one-line frame.** State how you want the rep to position this matchup, drawn straight from the [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]], so the whole conversation tilts toward your ground.
2. **Why you win here.** Give two or three concrete claims the rep can say out loud and defend if pressed.
3. **The landmines.** List the rival's genuine strengths and the questions they'll plant to disqualify you, and pair each with a response the rep can give without lying. Answer a real strength by repositioning, and answer a planted trap with a better question back.
4. **The traps to set.** Write the questions that expose the competitor's real limits, phrased so the buyer discovers the gap rather than hearing you attack.

Two properties decide whether the card gets used or decorates a folder. Keep it current: assign someone to update it on the cadence the competitor moves, and stamp the card with its last-reviewed date so a rep sees at a glance whether to trust it. And keep it true: make the response to every landmine survive an informed buyer. Answer a competitor's advantage by reframing the decision onto the [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]]; never deny the advantage. This is the same honesty discipline that governs [[CR-028 Comparison Page]], applied to the private one, and the internal card is the more dangerous of the two because reps assume it's safe to repeat.

Source the card from the field as much as you deliver it to the field. Put on it the questions reps report hearing, because the fastest way to write a bad card is to write it alone from competitor research, and the fastest way to write a good one is to mine won-and-lost call notes for the exact objections buyers raise. The rep who supplies the question trusts the answer. Don't rebuild objection craft here: the deeper treatment of turning an objection into a story lives in [[CR-006 Objection-Handling Narratives]], so link to it for the two or three objections that need more than a line.

## Implications

A working battle card turns competitive knowledge into competitive behavior. The rep stops improvising, the message stops varying by who's on the call, and the field starts feeding intelligence back because they can see it show up in a tool they actually use. Run it as the front-line delivery layer of [[OPS-018 Sales Enablement Architecture]]: let that system store, version, surface the card in the CRM at the deal stage where it's needed, and retire it when a competitor exits. Anchor it to [[CR-027 Differentiation Axis]] for its spine, because a card without a chosen axis is a feature comparison, and feature comparisons drag the deal onto the rival's ground. And run it in parallel with [[CR-028 Comparison Page]], sharing the same honest claims in two registers, one for the buyer's eyes and one for the rep's.

Manage the card's central tension, maintenance against decay. A battle card is the most perishable asset in the whole book: every card bets the competitor hasn't moved since you last checked, and that bet loses a little truth every week. Leave it unowned and it arms reps with claims about a competitor that no longer exists. So the discipline the card demands is less the writing than the keeping. Assign an owner to the cadence, and match the cadence to how fast the specific rival ships. Then hold the second line, against the standing pull toward weaponized dishonesty: reps under pressure will always want a sharper knife, and your job is to keep the card sharp without letting it cut the hand that holds it.

## Examples

**Salesforce versus Siebel (2000–2005).** When Salesforce sold against Siebel in the early 2000s, the competitive matchup was the whole company's positioning, and the field carried a tight frame rather than a feature teardown. The axis was chosen and unmissable: hosted software you turn on versus on-premise software you install, packaged in the "No Software" campaign down to the red circle-slash logo. On a raw feature grid in 2002, against Siebel's mature CRM, reps lose. Salesforce pulled every conversation onto time-to-value and total cost, where Siebel's strength, a deep configurable on-premise suite, reframes as its liability, a long expensive implementation. The landmine was predictable, so the response was rehearsed; when the buyer said Siebel was more capable, the rep agreed and asked how long the last enterprise software rollout took to deliver value. The card's job was to make every rep reach for the same axis the moment Siebel's name came up.

**Crayon's State of Competitive Intelligence (2019–2023).** Crayon, a competitive-intelligence software company, has published an annual survey of competitive and product-marketing teams for years, and the same finding recurs: battle cards are the single most-produced competitive asset and among the least-adopted by sales. Teams report building card libraries reps rarely open, and the reasons reps give are consistent across the reports: the cards are too long to use live, and they're out of date. Crayon sells the tool that fixes this, so treat the exact percentages as directional. Regardless of the vendor, the constraint is packaging the intelligence into something a rep uses mid-deal and keeping it current enough to trust. Gathering it was never the hard part.

**Mesosphere, the card the field would actually open (2015–2016).** At Mesosphere we sold a container platform into infrastructure teams who were also weighing Kubernetes, Docker's own stack, and building it themselves, and our first competitive collateral was a beautiful, useless twelve-slide deck. Reps didn't open it. What they kept asking me was narrower and always the same three questions: "Why not just run Kubernetes directly?", "Isn't this the same as Docker Swarm?", and "Why would I pay when the open-source is free?" We threw out the deck and wrote one page per competitor, top-lined with those exact questions in the buyer's words and a two-sentence answer under each, anchored to the axis we'd chosen (operate a real production platform instead of assembling one), plus a dated "last reviewed" stamp because that space moved monthly and a three-month-old claim was a liability. Reps began using the card because it answered the question they were about to be asked, in the ten seconds before they had to answer it, and because they trusted it: the questions on it were theirs.
