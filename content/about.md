The 123 patterns come out of building technical and enterprise markets at Salesforce, Mesosphere and D2iQ, Vapor IO, the Linux Foundation and COSSA. That history is why some examples name a company and others describe a client whose name isn't mine to give.

Not all of those markets worked. The Mesosphere and D2iQ story runs through Parts I, II and IV as a countercase: the same sequence this language recommends, traced to the point where the chain stopped holding. A language assembled only out of wins would be a weaker instrument, and a less honest one.

## Why a pattern language

Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* (1977) gave building a way to name the problems that recur, so that solutions could accumulate instead of being rediscovered one project at a time. Marketing has the recurring problems and no shared vocabulary for them. Every founder works out alone that price creates the buyer, that the category decides the comparison set, and that a funnel stage borrowed whole forecasts somebody else's deal.

The [[Introduction]] covers the method in full. In short: each pattern names a situation, the pressures that keep the obvious response from working, and a recommendation that holds. The patterns link, so a craft problem at the keyboard can be traced up to the structural decision that produced it.

It's written for the person drawing a new market rather than one maintaining a settled one.

## If a pattern here names your problem

The patterns are most useful against a live situation. If you're building a technical or enterprise market and something in the language describes what you're in the middle of, I'd like to hear which one and what it's getting wrong.

Bring the situation rather than a prepared brief. Most conversations start by finding which patterns a company is already living inside, and which two are in contradiction.
